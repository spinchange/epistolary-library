# Translator's Note

This translation of *Persian Letters* began not from a single continuous text, but from a letter-by-letter confrontation between Montesquieu's French and John Davidson's 1892 English version. That method matters. It shaped not only the translation itself, but also the editorial principles behind it. The aim of this edition is neither to discard Davidson nor to modernize Montesquieu into a seamless contemporary voice. It is, rather, to hear Montesquieu more directly in English by returning to the French text one letter at a time, while keeping Davidson in view as a gifted, historically revealing, and imperfect predecessor.

The French base text used here is the Project Gutenberg transcription of *Lettres persanes*, Tome I and Tome II. Davidson's translation was consulted through Internet Archive witnesses for *Persian Letters*, Volume I and Volume II. Those witnesses do not divide the work at the same point. The French text runs from Letter I to Letter LXXXVIII in Tome I, and from Letter LXXXIX to Letter CLXI in Tome II. Davidson's Volume I appears to end at Letter LXXV, with Volume II beginning at Letter LXXVI. Any comparison by physical volume therefore produces a false alignment from that point onward. For that reason this edition is organized not by book division but by letter number. Each letter has been treated as its own unit of comparison, translation, and editorial judgment.

That structure made it possible to build a full per-letter corpus for the entire work. For each letter, the workspace preserves raw French excerpts, cleaned French excerpts, raw English OCR excerpts, cleaned English OCR excerpts, metadata, and comparison notes. This apparatus is not meant to overwhelm the reader, but it matters for the honesty of the edition. Davidson survives here not as a perfectly stable printed text, but through OCR-derived witnesses that are sometimes clean and sometimes visibly damaged. Footnote carryover, page headers, page numbers, printer matter, library stamps, and even later editorial intrusions occasionally entered the working English text. During cleanup, obvious non-authorial debris was removed where the recovery was secure. Where certainty was not possible, the goal was not to pretend otherwise. The cleaned English text is therefore a working editorial witness, not a diplomatic transcription.

That distinction helps explain why a new translation is justified even though Davidson often remains lively and intelligent. His version can be very readable, and at times it captures Montesquieu's movement with impressive economy. But it also repeatedly smooths or redirects the French. At the smallest scale, there are local drifts in date, number, name, or emphasis. Letter I, for example, gives a telling numerical discrepancy: the French date corresponds to the twenty-fifth day, while Davidson gives the twenty-sixth. Elsewhere the changes are tonal rather than factual. In Letter II, a French image of the beloved as a "pillar" becomes Davidson's more elevated "monument." In Letter III, a word closer to "glory" becomes "conquest," sharpening the military edge and narrowing the emotional range. Such choices are not catastrophic, but taken together they reveal a recurrent habit of dignifying, polishing, or moralizing Montesquieu's sharper turns.

That habit becomes more noticeable because Montesquieu's voice in *Persian Letters* is deliberately unstable. The letters move rapidly across satire, political theory, erotic recollection, bureaucratic parody, theological dispute, travel narrative, demographic speculation, and tragic confession. A translation that irons those shifts flat may become smoother, but it also becomes less accurate to the book's design. One of the chief aims here has therefore been to resist premature smoothing. The goal is not literalism for its own sake, and still less an antiquarian stiffness masquerading as fidelity. It is to preserve Montesquieu's changes in pressure: the sudden turn from abstraction to mockery, from ceremony to cruelty, from detached observation to private panic.

That principle also affects lexical and stylistic choices. Older English translations often prefer Latinate phrasing, ornamental balance, or a pious and elevated diction that sits more comfortably in the nineteenth century than in Montesquieu's sharper eighteenth-century prose. Davidson is especially strong when he gives the sentence room to move, but he is less reliable when the French depends on abruptness, tonal doubleness, or morally uncomfortable directness. In some letters he heightens what Montesquieu leaves dry; in others he makes devotional or romantic what the French leaves cutting, ironic, or strategic. This edition tries instead to preserve argumentative force without sacrificing readability.

A similar balance governs editorial decisions about names and place-names. Where a modern form is stable, recognizable, and helpful, it is generally preferable to use it: for example, Qom rather than Koum, or Tabriz rather than Tauris. But modernization should not erase historical texture without reason. Some older forms carry rhetorical or bibliographical significance, especially when comparing Davidson directly against the French. For that reason the edition aims at consistency without over-correction. The point is not to produce a falsely timeless text, but to let the reader move through Montesquieu's geography without unnecessary obscurity.

The comparison with Davidson has also clarified that textual repair and translation are not the same act. In the second cleanup pass, obvious boundary errors were corrected where one letter spilled into another; in the following refinement pass, footnote debris and OCR corruption were removed from especially noisy letters such as XVI, LXVII, LXXV, CVIII, and CXXII. Those interventions were editorial, not translational. They were undertaken to recover a usable witness, not to rewrite Davidson into agreement with the new translation. That distinction is worth stating plainly, because it defines the ethical limit of the edition: remove what clearly does not belong, preserve what does, and mark uncertainty rather than disguising it.

The result, I hope, is a text that lets Montesquieu sound more like himself in English: more agile, more abrupt, more ironic, and sometimes more severe. Davidson remains an important companion throughout this work, and a reader of his translation can still learn a great deal from it. But a fresh version makes newly visible how much of *Persian Letters* depends on tonal motion, on sudden changes of register, and on formulations too sharp to survive repeated smoothing. If this translation succeeds, it will not be because it has replaced Davidson, but because it has made both Davidson and Montesquieu easier to see.
