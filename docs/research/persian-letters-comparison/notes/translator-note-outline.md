# Translator's note / foreword outline

Working purpose: capture the editorial ideas emerging from the first-pass comparison corpus and the second-pass cleanup work, so a later polished edition can open with a coherent explanatory note.

## Likely best framing

A **translator's note** is probably the better fit than a general foreword.

Why:
- the project's distinctive value is not just that *Persian Letters* is important, but that this edition is built through a fresh letter-by-letter comparison against John Davidson's 1892 translation
- the corpus work has surfaced recurring editorial questions that a translator's note can explain directly:
  - transliteration choices
  - OCR witness problems
  - divergent dates / names / numbers
  - shifts in tone between Montesquieu and Davidson
- a foreword could still be added later for broader literary framing, but the translator's note is the necessary document

## Proposed title options

- `Translator's Note`
- `Note on the Translation and Text`
- `Textual Note`
- `A Note on This Edition`

## Core argument of the note

This edition should explain that it is:
- based on the French Gutenberg text of *Lettres persanes* tomes I and II
- compared throughout against the 1892 John Davidson translation
- aligned by **letter number**, not by physical volume, because the French and English volume breaks do not match
- informed by a full per-letter corpus that preserves both raw and cleaned OCR witnesses for audit

## Main themes to include

### 1. Why retranslate when Davidson already exists?
Points:
- Davidson is often intelligent, readable, and rhetorically effective
- but he also frequently:
  - smooths Montesquieu's abruptness
  - heightens passages into a more Victorian literary register
  - substitutes interpretive words for literal ones
  - occasionally drifts in dates, numbers, names, or emphasis
- the new translation aims to stay closer to Montesquieu's argumentative force, tonal shifts, and rhetorical sharpness

### 2. Montesquieu's voice is unstable on purpose
Points:
- the letters move across many registers:
  - satire
  - political theory
  - erotic recollection
  - theological dispute
  - ethnographic pose
  - bureaucratic parody
  - tragic confession
- the translation should not flatten these into one smooth English voice
- part of the point is the sudden change in pitch from one letter to another

### 3. Editorial policy on names and place-names
Likely position:
- prefer readable modern forms where stable and familiar:
  - `Qom` rather than `Koum`
  - `Tabriz` rather than `Tauris`
  - `Erzerum` / `Erzurum` depending on consistency decision
- but preserve historical forms when they matter rhetorically or bibliographically
- explain that older English translations often use Latinate or French-influenced spellings

### 4. Alignment by letter number, not volume
This is one of the strongest distinctive points of the project.

State clearly:
- French Tome I = `I-LXXXVIII`
- French Tome II = `LXXXIX-CLXI`
- Davidson Volume I = `I-LXXV`
- Davidson Volume II = `LXXVI-CLXI`

Implication:
- comparison by volume would misalign the text beginning at Letter `LXXVI`
- this edition therefore compares the texts letter by letter

### 5. OCR and witness transparency
Important to explain the textual basis honestly.

Points:
- Davidson was accessed through Internet Archive OCR-derived witnesses
- a full per-letter corpus was built with:
  - raw English OCR excerpts
  - cleaned English OCR excerpts
  - raw French excerpts
  - cleaned French excerpts
- the cleaned English text is a working editorial witness, not a perfect diplomatic transcription
- second-pass cleanup has already required secure removal of footnote carryover, page headers, page numbers, library stamps, and modern paratext that bled into OCR witnesses
- where the English witness is visibly damaged, the note or apparatus should say so rather than silently pretending certainty
- where recovery is secure, the edition may silently remove obvious non-authorial debris, but it should log those interventions in the editorial notes

### 6. What kinds of divergences mattered most
Examples already surfaced in the corpus:
- numerical drift
- date drift
- person-name inconsistencies
- lexical substitutions that soften or moralize the French
- rhetorical upgrades into Victorian elegance
- editorial / OCR intrusions that break otherwise usable Davidson prose

Examples worth citing briefly:
- Letter I: `twenty-fifth` vs Davidson's `twenty-sixth`
- Letter III: `glory` vs Davidson's `conquest`
- Letter II: `pillar of fidelity` vs Davidson's `monument of fidelity`
- later letters with large numerical drifts or title mismatches

### 7. The goal of the new translation
Possible phrasing:
- not antiquarian literalism for its own sake
- not modernization that erases historical strangeness
- instead: a readable English that preserves Montesquieu's edge, irony, and structural mobility

## Suggested structure for the finished note

### Opening paragraph
- identify the work and the translation basis
- explain that the edition emerged from a full letter-by-letter comparison against Davidson

### Textual basis paragraph
- French source(s)
- English comparison source(s)
- letter-number alignment principle

### Translation philosophy paragraph
- balance between readability and fidelity
- preserving shifts in tone and argument

### Editorial interventions paragraph
- modernized spellings / transliterations
- cleaned OCR but preserved transparency around uncertain spots
- no silent normalization where meaning is at stake

### Davidson paragraph
- acknowledge his strengths fairly
- explain why a new translation is still justified

### Closing paragraph
- frame the edition as an invitation to hear Montesquieu's motion, irony, and severity more directly in English

## Candidate opening sentences

Option A:
> This translation of *Persian Letters* began not from a single continuous text, but from a letter-by-letter confrontation between Montesquieu's French and John Davidson's 1892 English version.

Option B:
> The pages that follow are shaped by two convictions: that Davidson's *Persian Letters* remains a vivid historical translation, and that Montesquieu's own tonal sharpness still asks for a new English rendering.

Option C:
> This edition was built one letter at a time, aligning the French *Lettres persanes* with its nineteenth-century English afterlife in order to make both the strengths and the limits of that earlier translation newly visible.

## Recommendation

For the eventual polished edition:
- write a **Translator's Note** first
- keep it to roughly `900-1500` words
- if desired later, add a shorter literary **Foreword** focused on why *Persian Letters* still matters
