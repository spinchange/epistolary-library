### Translation
Here is a letter that I received yesterday from a learned man: it will seem singular to you.

`Sir,

`It is six months since I came into the inheritance of a very rich uncle, who left me five or six hundred thousand livres and a house magnificently furnished. It is a pleasure to possess wealth when one knows how to make good use of it. I have no ambition and no taste for pleasures. I am almost always shut up in a study, where I lead the life of a scholar. It is in that place that one finds a curious lover of venerable antiquity.

`When my uncle closed his eyes, I would very much have liked to have him buried with the ceremonies observed by the ancient Greeks and Romans; but at that time I had neither lachrymatories, nor urns, nor antique lamps.

`But since then I have provided myself well with those precious rarities. A few days ago I sold my silver plate in order to buy a clay lamp that had served a Stoic philosopher. I got rid of all the mirrors with which my uncle had covered almost all the walls of his rooms, in order to obtain a small mirror, a little cracked, which once belonged to Virgil; I am delighted to see my own face reflected there, in place of that of the swan of Mantua. That is not all: for a hundred louis d'or I bought five or six copper coins that circulated two thousand years ago. I do not believe that there is now in my house a single piece of furniture that was not made before the decline of the empire. I have a little cabinet of manuscripts, very precious and very expensive. Although I ruin my eyesight reading them, I greatly prefer using them to printed copies, which are not so correct and which everybody has in hand. Although I almost never go out, I nevertheless have an excessive passion for knowing all the ancient roads that existed in Roman times. There is one near my house, which a proconsul of Gaul had made about twelve hundred years ago. When I go to my country house, I never fail to pass by it, although it is very inconvenient and adds more than a league to my journey. But what drives me mad is that wooden posts have been set up there from distance to distance to mark the remoteness of the neighboring towns. I am in despair to see those wretched indicators in place of the milestones that used to stand there. I do not doubt that I shall have them restored by my heirs, and that I shall bind them to that expense by my will. If you have, sir, some Persian manuscript, you would oblige me by letting me have it. I will pay you whatever you wish; and to sweeten the bargain I will give you some works of my own, by which you will see that I am not a useless member of the republic of letters. Among them is a dissertation in which I prove that the crown formerly used in triumphs was of oak and not of laurel. You will admire another, in which I show clearly, through learned conjectures drawn from the weightiest Greek authors, that Cambyses was wounded not in the right leg but in the left. In another I demonstrate that a low forehead was a beauty much sought among the Romans. I will also send you a quarto volume containing the explanation of a line in the sixth book of Virgil's Aeneid. You will receive all these things within a few days; and meanwhile I content myself with sending you the attached fragment of an ancient Greek mythologist, not yet published, that I discovered in the dust of a library. I must leave you now for an important matter that occupies me, namely the restoration of a fine passage in Pliny the naturalist, strangely disfigured by the copyists of the fifth century. I am,' etc.

### Translation continued
**Fragment of an ancient mythologist**

On an island near the Orkneys there was born a child whose father was Aeolus, god of the winds, and whose mother was a nymph of Caledonia. It is said of him that he learned all by himself to count on his fingers, and that from the age of four he distinguished metals so perfectly that when his mother wanted to give him a brass ring instead of a gold one, he recognized the trick and threw it on the ground.

As soon as he was grown, his father taught him the secret of shutting the winds up in a leather bag, which he afterward sold to all travelers. But since the merchandise was not much prized in his own country, he left it and set off to roam the world in the company of the blind god of chance.

In his travels he learned that in Baetica gold shone on every side; this made him hasten his steps there. Saturn, who reigned there at the time, received him very badly; but that god having left the earth, the son of Aeolus took to going into all the crossroads, where he cried out in a hoarse voice without ceasing: `People of Baetica, you think yourselves rich because you have gold and silver. I pity your error. Believe me, leave the land of base metals; come into the empire of imagination, and I promise you riches that will astonish even yourselves.' At once he opened a large number of the bags he had brought and distributed his merchandise to whoever wanted it.

The next day he came back to the same crossroads and cried out: `People of Baetica, do you want to be rich? Imagine that I am very rich, and that you are very rich too. Persuade yourselves every morning that your fortune has doubled during the night; then get up, and if you have creditors, go and pay them with what you have imagined; and tell them to imagine in their turn.'

A few days later he appeared again, and spoke in this way: `People of Baetica, I can see plainly that your imagination is not as lively as it was in the first days. Let yourselves be guided by mine. Every morning I will place before your eyes a placard that will be for you the source of riches. You will see on it only four words; but they will be very significant, for they will determine the dowry of your wives, the lawful portion of your children, and the number of your servants. And as for you,' he said to those in the crowd who were closest to him, `as for you, my dear children - I may call you by that name, since you have received from me a second birth - my placard will decide the magnificence of your equipages, the sumptuousness of your feasts, and the number and pensions of your mistresses.'

A few days after that he arrived in the square, completely out of breath; and transported with anger, he cried out: `People of Baetica, I advised you to imagine, and I see that you are not doing it. Very well, now I command you to do it.' With that he left them abruptly; but reflection brought him back. `I learn that some of you are detestable enough to keep your gold and silver. Never mind the silver; but the gold... the gold... Ah! that throws me into indignation. I swear by my sacred bags that, if they do not come and bring it to me, I will punish them severely.' Then he added in a wholly persuasive tone: `Do you think I am asking you for those wretched metals in order to keep them? A proof of my candor is that, when you brought them to me a few days ago, I immediately gave you back half of them.'

The next day he was seen from afar, and people saw him insinuate himself with a soft and flattering voice: `People of Baetica, I learn that you have part of your treasures in foreign countries. I beg you, have them sent to me. You will do me a kindness, and I shall be eternally grateful to you.'

The son of Aeolus was speaking to people who were in no mood to laugh; yet they could not help themselves, and he went away very confused. But recovering courage, he ventured another little request. `I know that you have precious stones. In Jupiter's name, get rid of them. Nothing impoverishes you so much as such things. Get rid of them, I tell you. If you cannot do it yourselves, I will provide you with excellent agents. What riches will flow toward you if you do what I advise! Yes, I promise you all that is purest in my bags.'

At last he mounted a platform and, taking a firmer voice, said: `People of Baetica, I have compared the happy condition in which you are with the one in which I found you when I arrived here. I see you the richest people on earth. But, to complete your fortune, allow me to take half your goods away from you.' At those words, on a light wing, the son of Aeolus disappeared and left his listeners in inexpressible consternation. That made him come back the next day and speak thus: `Yesterday I noticed that my speech displeased you very much. Very well, suppose I said nothing. We need only take other means to reach the end I have proposed. Let us assemble our riches in one place; we can do it easily, for they take up no great volume.' At once three quarters of them disappeared.

*Paris, the 9th day of the moon of Chahban, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures both halves of the letter: the comic antiquarian's self-portrait and the mythological allegory of financial delusion. His English is lively and easy to follow, and he generally preserves the satirical architecture of the piece.

### Main differences
- **Huge inheritance discrepancy**
  - French: `cinq ou six cent mille livres`
  - Fresh translation: `five or six hundred thousand livres`
  - Davidson: `five or six thousand livres`
  - This is a major factual error in the English text and one of the clearest substantive divergences in the batch.

- **The antiquary's objects**
  - French: `un petit miroir un peu fêlé`
  - Fresh translation: `a small mirror, a little cracked`
  - Davidson is close here, but his phrasing is slightly more polished and less comic in its material pettiness.

- **Roman decline vs fall**
  - French: `avant la décadence de l'empire`
  - Fresh translation keeps `before the decline of the empire`.
  - Davidson gives `before the fall of the Roman empire`, which sharpens and historicizes the line beyond the French wording.

- **`écriteau`**
  - Fresh translation: `placard`
  - Davidson: `bill`
  - Davidson is intelligible, but `placard` better preserves the sense of a posted public sign in a financial frenzy.

- **`outres sacrées`**
  - Fresh translation: `my sacred bags`
  - Davidson: `my sacred windbags`
  - Davidson's version is vivid and arguably helpful, but it leans further into comic explanation than the French does.

- **Final disappearance of wealth**
  - Fresh translation: `At once three quarters of them disappeared`
  - Davidson: `Immediately three-quarters of their wealth had disappeared`
  - Davidson clarifies the referent. The French is terser and more abrupt in its closing sting.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: first mock-scholarly, then allegorical and satirical; the two halves mirror one another in vanity and absurd system-making.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep both the pedantry and the speculative delirium in a modern but literal enough register.
- **Davidson**: very readable, but often more explanatory than the French, and occasionally flatly wrong on particulars.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLII is especially valuable because it shows both Davidson's strengths and his liabilities. He keeps the satire vivid, but the `five or six thousand` reading is a serious error, and the OCR-clean text remains cluttered with apparatus and scan debris.
