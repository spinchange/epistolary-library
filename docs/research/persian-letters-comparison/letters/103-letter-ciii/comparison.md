## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CIII**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The most powerful states in Europe are those of the emperor and of the kings of France, Spain, and England. Italy and a great part of Germany are divided into an infinite number of little states, whose princes are, properly speaking, martyrs to sovereignty. Our glorious sultans have more wives than most of these princes have subjects. Those of Italy, who are less united, are more to be pitied: their states are open like caravanserais, where they are obliged to lodge the first who comes. So they must attach themselves to great princes and share their fear rather than their friendship.

Most governments in Europe are monarchical - or rather are called so, for I do not know whether there have ever truly been such governments, and at least it is impossible that they should have remained long in their purity. It is a violent condition that always degenerates either into despotism or into republic. Power can never be equally divided between the people and the prince; the balance is too hard to maintain. Power must diminish on one side while it increases on the other, and the advantage ordinarily lies with the prince, who is at the head of the armies.

Thus the power of Europe's kings is indeed great, and one may say they have it as they wish; but they do not exercise it with as much breadth as our sultans. First, because they do not wish to shock the morals and religion of their peoples; second, because it is not in their interest to carry it so far.

Nothing brings princes closer to the condition of their subjects than the immense power they exercise over them; nothing subjects them more to reverses and the caprices of fortune.

The practice by which they have all those who displease them put to death at the slightest sign overturns the proportion that ought to exist between faults and punishments - a proportion that is like the soul of states and the harmony of empires. This proportion, scrupulously preserved by Christian princes, gives them an infinite advantage over our sultans.

A Persian who, through imprudence or bad luck, draws the prince's disgrace upon himself is certain to die: the slightest fault, or the slightest caprice, reduces him to this necessity. But had he attempted his sovereign's life, or wished to deliver his fortresses to the enemy, he would still only lose his life; so in the latter case he runs no more risk than in the former.

Therefore, at the smallest disgrace, seeing death as certain and seeing nothing worse beyond it, he naturally turns to troubling the state and conspiring against the sovereign - the only resource left to him.

It is not the same with the great men of Europe, whose disgrace deprives them only of benevolence and favor. They leave the court and think only of enjoying a tranquil life and the advantages of their birth. As they are hardly ever made to perish except for high treason, they fear falling into it because of what they have to lose and how little they have to gain. That is why one sees few revolts there and few princes dying violent deaths.

If our princes, with their unlimited authority, did not take so many precautions to secure their lives, they would not live a day. And if they did not keep an innumerable quantity of troops in pay to tyrannize over the rest of their subjects, their empire would not last a month.

Only four or five centuries ago did a king of France, against the custom of those times, take guards in order to protect himself from the assassins a petty prince of Asia had sent to kill him. Until then kings had lived peacefully in the midst of their subjects, like fathers among their children.

Far from being able, of their own motion, to take away the life of one of their subjects as our sultans do, the kings of France, on the contrary, always carry with them the grace of all criminals. It is enough for a man to have been fortunate enough to see the august face of his prince for him no longer to be unworthy of living. These monarchs are like the sun, carrying heat and life everywhere.

*Paris, the 8th day of the moon of Rebiab II, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the main political comparison: small European princes are weak, mixed monarchy is unstable, Christian rulers preserve proportion in punishment better than Oriental despots, and that difference helps explain why Europe sees fewer conspiracies and violent royal deaths.

### Main differences
- **Monarchy as a `violent condition`**
  - French: `C'est un état violent`
  - Fresh translation keeps that phrase directly.
  - Davidson renders it as `a state in which might is right`, which is much stronger and more interpretive than the French.

- **Scope of European kings**
  - French: `le pouvoir des rois d'Europe`
  - Fresh translation keeps `Europe's kings`.
  - Davidson's clean text says `the kings of Spain`, which looks like a substantive slip rather than a mere stylistic choice.

- **Proportion as political principle**
  - French calls proportion between fault and punishment `comme l'âme des États et l'harmonie des empires`.
  - The fresh draft keeps that full metaphor.
  - Davidson trims the image and loses some of the aphoristic force.

- **Royal grace**
  - The French final paragraph balances arbitrary death against monarchical mercy with a very pointed cadence.
  - Davidson is broadly accurate, but the fresh translation keeps the contrast sharper.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: comparative, aphoristic, and politically schematic.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the wide political contrasts while staying close to the metaphors.
- **Davidson**: often effective, but more prone here to paraphrase or slip.
