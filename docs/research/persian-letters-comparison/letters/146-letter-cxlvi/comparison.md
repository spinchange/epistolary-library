## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXLVI**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
It has long been said that good faith is the soul of a great minister.

A private person may enjoy the obscurity in which he lives; he loses credit only with a few people and remains concealed from the others. But a minister who lacks probity has as many witnesses, as many judges, as there are people whom he governs.

Shall I dare say it? The greatest evil done by a minister without probity is not that he harms his prince and ruins his people; there is another, in my opinion a thousand times more dangerous: the bad example he gives.

You know that I traveled for a long time in the Indies. There I saw a nation, naturally generous, corrupted in an instant, from the lowest subject to the highest, by the bad example of a minister. I saw an entire people, among whom generosity, probity, candor, and good faith had always passed for natural qualities, suddenly become the vilest of peoples. I saw the evil spread and spare not even the soundest members; I saw the most virtuous men do shameful things, and violate, in every circumstance of life, the first principles of justice, on the vain pretext that those principles had been violated against them.

They invoked odious laws as a guarantee for the basest actions, and called injustice and perfidy necessity.

I saw the faith of contracts banished, the holiest agreements annihilated, all the laws of the family overturned. I saw miserly debtors, proud in an insolent poverty, unworthy instruments of the fury of the laws and the harshness of the times, feign payment instead of making it, and drive the knife into the breast of their benefactors.

I saw others, still more unworthy, buy for almost nothing, or rather gather from the ground, oak leaves to put in place of the substance of widows and orphans.

I saw an insatiable thirst for riches suddenly arise in every heart. I saw, in a moment, a detestable conspiracy form to grow rich, not by honest work and generous industry, but by the ruin of the prince, the state, and fellow citizens.

I saw an honest citizen, in those unhappy times, never go to bed without saying: `I ruined one family today; tomorrow I shall ruin another.`

`I am going,` said another, `with a man in black who carries an inkhorn in his hand and a pointed bit of metal at his ear, to assassinate all those to whom I am indebted.`

Another said: `I see that I am putting my affairs in order. It is true that, when I went three days ago to make a certain payment, I left a whole family in tears, dissipated the dowry of two honest girls, and deprived a little boy of an education. The father will die of grief, the mother perish of sorrow. But I have done only what the law permits.`

What greater crime can a minister commit than corrupting the morals of an entire nation, degrading the most generous souls, tarnishing the splendor of dignities, darkening virtue itself, and plunging the highest birth into universal contempt?

What will posterity say when it must blush at the shame of its fathers? What will the rising people say when it compares the iron of its ancestors with the gold of those to whom it owes its immediate birth? I do not doubt that the nobles will cut from their quarterings an unworthy degree of nobility that dishonors them, and leave the present generation in the dreadful nothingness into which it has cast itself.

*Paris, the 11th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the large denunciation effectively. He preserves the argument about corrupt government as contagious example, and he captures the letter's moral outrage against financial and political perfidy. His version is strong in sweep, but it sometimes simplifies or regularizes the sharper moral language of the French.

### Main differences
- **`Bonne foi` and `probité`**
  - French opening: `la bonne foi étoit l'âme d'un grand ministre`
  - Fresh translation: `good faith is the soul of a great minister`
  - Davidson: `a minister cannot be great unless he is sincere`
  - Davidson captures the point, but he replaces the more concrete moral vocabulary of `good faith` / `probity` with `sincere` / `unscrupulous`.

- **The witness/judge parallel**
  - French: `autant de témoins, autant de juges`
  - Fresh translation preserves the paired structure.
  - Davidson rewrites this as `a witness, a judge, in every subject`, which is readable but less striking rhetorically.

- **Natural generosity of the nation**
  - French: `une nation, naturellement généreuse`
  - Fresh translation: `a nation, naturally generous`
  - Davidson: `a nation, upright by nature`
  - `Généreuse` is not quite the same as `upright`; Davidson shifts the moral emphasis.

- **Calling injustice `necessity`**
  - French: `Ils appeloient des lois odieuses en garantie des actions les plus lâches; et nommoient nécessité l'injustice et la perfidie.`
  - Fresh translation keeps the structure of justification and renaming.
  - Davidson compresses this into `They appealed to detestable laws, necessity, injustice and treachery`, which is less exact syntactically and somewhat blurs the accusation.

- **The final historical image**
  - French: `lorsqu'il comparera le fer de ses aïeux avec l'or de ceux à qui il doit immédiatement le jour`
  - Fresh translation keeps the literal contrast of `iron` and `gold` attached to generations of people.
  - Davidson turns this into `the iron age of earlier times` and `the age of gold which gave it birth`, which interprets the metaphor rather than preserving it.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: indignant, moralizing, and relentlessly accusatory.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep the repeated `I saw` structure and the cumulative force of the examples.
- **Davidson**: forceful and fluent, but slightly smoother and more explanatory in key places.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLVI is a strong example of Davidson's general reliability at the level of argument, alongside a tendency to:
- replace exact moral terms with smoother English equivalents
- soften rhetorical parallelism
- and interpret dense metaphors instead of reproducing them literally
