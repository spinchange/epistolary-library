## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XIV**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
As the people grew larger every day, the Troglodytes thought it appropriate to choose themselves a king. They agreed that the crown should be offered to the justest man, and all eyes turned to a venerable old man, worthy alike by his age and by a long practice of virtue. He had not wished to attend the assembly; he had withdrawn to his house, his heart pressed with sadness.

When deputies were sent to him to announce the choice that had been made, he said: God forbid that I should do the Troglodytes the wrong of letting it be thought there is no one among them more just than I am. You offer me the crown; and, if you absolutely insist, I shall have to accept it. But count on this: I shall die of grief at having seen the Troglodytes born free and seeing them today subjected. At these words he began to pour out a torrent of tears. Unhappy day, he said; and why have I lived so long? Then he cried out in a severe voice: I see clearly what it is, Troglodytes: your virtue is beginning to weigh on you. In the state you are in, having no chief, you must be virtuous despite yourselves; otherwise you could not survive, and you would fall into the misfortune of your first fathers. But this yoke seems too hard to you. You would rather be subject to a prince and obey laws less rigid than your customs. You know that then you will be able to satisfy your ambition, acquire riches, and languish in cowardly pleasure; and that, provided you avoid the great crimes, you will have no need of virtue.

He paused a moment, and his tears flowed more than ever. And what do you expect me to do? How can it be that I should command anything to a Troglodyte? Do you want him to perform a virtuous action because I command it, he who would do it all the same without me and by the sole inclination of nature? O Troglodytes, I am at the end of my days, my blood is frozen in my veins, I shall soon see your sacred ancestors again. Why do you want to afflict them, and force me to tell them I left you under another yoke than that of virtue?

*From Erzurum, the 10th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the structure of the old man's refusal and his anti-monarchical argument with substantial fidelity. He reads fluently and preserves the speech's general severity, but he sometimes smooths or interprets the French's harder civic language.

### Main differences
- **Monotheistic invocation vs classical plural**
  - French: `A Dieu ne plaise`
  - Fresh translation: `God forbid`
  - Davidson: `The Gods forbid`
  - Davidson's pluralizing choice is stylistically odd here and departs from the singular French.

- **Freedom at birth**
  - French: `d'avoir vu en naissant les Troglodytes libres`
  - Fresh translation: `having seen the Troglodytes born free`
  - Davidson: `having known the Troglodites freemen`
  - Davidson keeps the sense, but the French more pointedly links freedom to origin.

- **Virtue as burden**
  - French: `votre vertu commence à vous peser`
  - Fresh translation: `your virtue is beginning to weigh on you`
  - Davidson: `uprightness becomes a burden to you`
  - Davidson is accurate, though slightly more generalized and less physically immediate.

- **Cowardly pleasure**
  - French: `languir dans une lâche volupté`
  - Fresh translation: `languish in cowardly pleasure`
  - Davidson: `while away the time in slothful luxury`
  - Davidson turns a morally charged phrase into a smoother, more elegant abstraction.

- **Law and custom**
  - French: `obéir à ses lois, moins rigides que vos moeurs`
  - Fresh translation: `obey laws less rigid than your customs`
  - Davidson: `submit to laws of his framing—laws less exacting than your present customs`
  - `of his framing` is an interpretive addition not stated in the French.

- **Natural inclination**
  - French: `par le seul penchant de la nature`
  - Fresh translation: `by the sole inclination of nature`
  - Davidson: `from natural inclination`
  - Davidson preserves the point, but the French has a slightly stronger rhetorical insistence.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: austere, political, and severe in its suspicion of monarchy and delegated command.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the speech abrupt and morally hard-edged.
- **Davidson**: broadly faithful, but more stately and polished in cadence.

## Working conclusion
Letter XIV is well preserved in Davidson at the level of argument, but the French remains sharper in its republican moral logic. The best places to watch are small tonal shifts: singular `God`, the bodily image of virtue weighing on the Troglodytes, and the harsher phrase `cowardly pleasure`.
