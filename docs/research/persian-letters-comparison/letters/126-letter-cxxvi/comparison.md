### Heading
**Letter CXXVI**

**Rica to \*\*\***

### Translation
Every religion is greatly embarrassed when it has to give an idea of the pleasures reserved for those who have lived well. It is easy enough to frighten the wicked with a long sequence of pains held over them as threats; but for virtuous people one hardly knows what to promise. Pleasure seems by nature to be short-lived; the imagination has difficulty picturing it otherwise.

I have seen descriptions of paradise enough to make all sensible people renounce it: some make those happy shades play the flute without end; others condemn them to the torture of walking forever; others again, making them dream up there of the mistresses they loved on earth, have not believed a hundred million years a term long enough to cure them of such amorous anxieties.

This reminds me of a story I once heard told by a man who had been in the country of the Mogul; it shows that Indian priests are no less barren than the rest in the ideas they form of paradise's pleasures.

A woman who had just lost her husband came ceremoniously to the governor of the city to ask permission to burn herself; but since in countries subject to the Mohammedans that cruel custom is abolished as much as possible, he refused her absolutely.

When she saw that her pleas were powerless, she flew into a violent rage. 'See how constrained one is!' she said. 'A poor woman is not even allowed to burn herself when she feels like it! Has anyone ever seen the like? My mother, my aunt, my sisters all burned themselves very well; and when I go ask that accursed governor for permission, he gets angry and starts shouting like a madman.'

By chance a young bonze was there. 'Faithless man,' the governor said to him, 'is it you who put this frenzy into the woman's mind?' 'No,' he said, 'I never spoke to her; but if she believes me, she will complete her sacrifice. She will perform an action pleasing to the god Brahma, and she will be well rewarded for it; for she will find her husband again in the other world and begin with him a second marriage.' 'What are you saying?' said the astonished woman. 'I shall find my husband again? Ah! then I will not burn myself. He was jealous, ill-tempered, and besides so old that, unless the god Brahma has made some reform in him, he surely has no need of me. Burn myself for him! I would not burn so much as the tip of my finger to pull him out of the depths of hell. Two old bonzes, who were misleading me and who knew very well how I lived with him, took good care not to tell me everything. But if the god Brahma has only that gift to make me, I renounce such bliss. Mr. Governor, I am becoming a Mohammedan. And as for you,' she added, looking at the bonze, 'you may, if you like, go tell my husband that I am doing very well.'

*Paris, the 2nd day of the moon of Chalval, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the anecdotal shape of the letter well: a reflection on promised heavenly pleasures turns into a comic story in which a widow abandons sati the moment she learns paradise would reunite her with her husband.

### Main differences
- **`ne sont pas moins stériles que les autres`**
  - Fresh translation: `are no less barren than the rest`
  - Davidson's `are as fertile as others` reverses the force of the French.
  - This is the clearest substantive mistranslation in the letter.

- **Pleasure as brief by nature**
  - French: `Il semble que la nature des plaisirs soit d'être d'une courte durée`
  - Fresh translation keeps the aphoristic form.
  - Davidson is close, but somewhat smoother and less pointed.

- **`consommera son sacrifice`**
  - Fresh translation: `complete her sacrifice`
  - Davidson is similar, though the French has a stronger ritual and devotional coloration.

- **Final comic reversal**
  - The French ending is very dry: she converts on the spot and sends a message to her dead husband.
  - Davidson captures the joke, but the fresh draft keeps the abruptness and selfish candor a little more starkly.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: playful, irreverent, and tightly constructed.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the philosophical opening and the farcical anecdote in the same register of skeptical wit.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, but not always exact in the letter's sharper formulations.
