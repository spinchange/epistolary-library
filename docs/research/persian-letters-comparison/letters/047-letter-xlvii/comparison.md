## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLVII**

**Zachi to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
I have great news to tell you: I have been reconciled with Zéphis; the seraglio, divided between us, has come back together. Only you are missing from these places where peace now reigns. Come, my dear Usbek, come and let love triumph there.

I gave Zéphis a splendid feast, to which your mother, your wives, and your principal concubines were invited; your aunts and several of your cousins were there too. They had come on horseback, covered by the dark cloud of their veils and garments.

The next day we set out for the country, where we hoped to be freer. We mounted our camels and placed four of ourselves in each litter. As the outing had been arranged suddenly, we did not have time to send around the *courouc*; but the chief eunuch, always resourceful, took another precaution, for he added to the cloth that prevented us from being seen a curtain so thick that we ourselves could see absolutely no one.

When we came to the river that had to be crossed, each of us, according to custom, got into a box and had herself carried into the boat, for we were told that the river was full of people. A curious man who came too near the place where we were shut up received a mortal blow that forever took from him the light of day; another, found bathing naked on the bank, met the same fate; and your faithful eunuchs sacrificed those two unfortunates to your honor and to ours.

But listen to the rest of our adventures. When we were in the middle of the stream, so violent a wind rose and so frightful a cloud covered the sky that our boatmen began to despair. Terrified by the danger, nearly all of us fainted. I remember hearing the voices and quarrel of our eunuchs: some said we must be warned of the danger and pulled out of our prison, but their chief kept insisting that he would die rather than allow his master to be dishonored in that way, and that he would drive a dagger into the breast of whoever made such daring proposals. One of my slave girls, completely out of herself, ran toward me undressed to help me; but a black eunuch seized her brutally and made her go back into the place she had come from. Then I fainted, and I returned to myself only when the danger had passed.

How troublesome travel is for women! Men are exposed only to the dangers that threaten their lives, while we are at every moment afraid of losing either life or virtue. Farewell, my dear Usbek. I shall always adore you.

*From the seraglio of Fatmé, the 2nd day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the main narrative clearly: reconciliation in the seraglio, the outing, the lethal enforcement of seclusion, and the storm in which honor outweighs safety. The letter's sequence and dramatic turn are preserved.

### Main differences
- **Dark cloud image**
  - French: `le sombre nuage de leurs voiles`
  - Fresh translation keeps the metaphor of a `dark cloud`.
  - Davidson does as well, and it is one of his stronger visual renderings.
- **No one / see no one**
  - French stresses that the extra curtain prevented the women themselves from seeing anyone.
  - Davidson keeps this point, though a little more lightly.
- **Mortal violence**
  - French: `reçut un coup mortel`
  - Fresh translation: `received a mortal blow`
  - Davidson is close, but the French sacrificial phrasing at the end remains starker.
- **Dishonor before death**
  - The chief eunuch's refusal to free the women in danger is central.
  - Davidson preserves the episode but slightly smooths the brutal logic of honor over survival.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: ceremonious at first, then suddenly horrifying in its fusion of seclusion, violence, and absurd honor.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the ornamental beginning and the grim absurdity of the river scene.
- **Davidson**: effective narratively, though a little more polished in moments that are deliberately cruel.

## Working conclusion
Letter XLVII is one of the clearest demonstrations of the seraglio's lethal logic. Davidson keeps the plot well, but the fresh translation better preserves the cold extremity of the chief eunuch's code.
