## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LX**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
You ask me whether there are Jews in France. Know that wherever there is money, there are Jews. You ask me what they do there. Exactly what they do in Persia: nothing resembles an Asian Jew more than a European Jew.

Among Christians, as among ourselves, they show an invincible obstinacy for their religion that goes as far as madness.

The Jewish religion is an old trunk that has produced two branches which have covered the whole earth — I mean Islam and Christianity; or rather it is a mother who has given birth to two daughters who have covered her with a thousand wounds. For in matters of religion the nearest relatives are the greatest enemies. Yet, whatever ill treatment she has received from them, she still glories in having brought them into the world. She uses both of them to embrace the whole earth, while on the other hand her venerable old age embraces all time.

Jews therefore regard themselves as the source of all holiness and the origin of all religion. We, on the contrary, appear to them as heretics who have changed the law, or rather as rebellious Jews.

If the change had taken place gradually, they think they might easily have been seduced. But because it happened all at once and in a violent way, because they can name the very day and hour of each birth, they are scandalized to find ages in us, and stand firm in a religion that even the world itself did not precede.

They have never had in Europe a calm like the one they enjoy now. Christians are beginning to rid themselves of the spirit of intolerance that used to animate them. Spain has found itself poorly served by having driven them out, and France by having harassed Christians whose belief differed only a little from that of the prince. People have realized that zeal for the progress of religion is different from the attachment one owes to it, and that in order to love and observe it, it is not necessary to hate and persecute those who do not observe it.

One could wish that our Muslims thought as sensibly on this matter as Christians do, and that peace could at last be made between Ali and Abubeker, leaving to God the care of deciding the merits of those holy prophets. I would have them honored by acts of veneration and respect, not by vain preferences; and I would have men seek to deserve their favor whatever place God has marked out for them, whether at His right hand or beneath the footstool of His throne.

*At Paris, the 18th day of the moon of Saphar, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson transmits the broad argument accurately: Jews retain strong identity because of historical continuity, Christian intolerance is receding, and sectarian violence among Muslims should likewise be moderated.

### Main differences
- **Old trunk / mother image**
  - Fresh translation preserves both metaphors in succession.
  - Davidson also keeps them, though with slightly smoother Victorian phrasing.
- **Covered her with wounds**
  - French: `accablée de mille plaies`
  - Fresh translation: `covered her with a thousand wounds`
  - Davidson: `loaded her with a thousand bruises`
  - `Wounds` is harsher and closer to `plaies`.
- **Scandalized to find beginnings**
  - French stresses that Judaism rejects religions whose origin can be historically dated.
  - Davidson turns that into `mock`, which is a little more aggressive than `se scandaliser`.
- **Vain preferences**
  - Fresh translation preserves the rebuke against empty partisan ranking.
  - Davidson's `foolish preferences` is close but more colloquial.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: polemical, historically reflective, and comparatively measured by the end.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the mixture of satirical opening and conciliatory conclusion.
- **Davidson**: reliable overall, with a few sharper or more picturesque lexical choices.

## Working conclusion
Letter LX is a largely stable comparison. Davidson is faithful, but the fresh translation keeps somewhat stronger corporeal and historical language in the maternal / wounded Judaism metaphor.
