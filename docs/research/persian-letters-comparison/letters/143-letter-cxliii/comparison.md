## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXLIII**

**Rica to Nathaniel Levi, Jewish physician, at Livorno**

### Translation
You ask me what I think of the virtue of amulets and the power of talismans. Why do you address yourself to me? You are a Jew, and I am a Mohammedan: that is to say that we are both very credulous.

I always carry on me more than two thousand passages from the holy Koran. I tie to my arms a small packet in which are written the names of more than two hundred dervishes. The names of Ali, of Fatmé, and of all the Pure are hidden in more than twenty places in my clothes.

Yet I do not disapprove of those who reject the virtue attributed to certain words. It is much harder for us to answer their arguments than it is for them to answer our experiences.

I wear all these sacred scraps through long habit, to conform myself to a universal practice. I believe that, if they have no more virtue than the rings and other ornaments with which one adorns oneself, they have no less. But you place your whole confidence in a few mysterious letters, and without that safeguard you would live in continual fear.

Men are very unfortunate. They are always tossed between false hopes and ridiculous fears. And instead of relying on reason, they make for themselves monsters that intimidate them, or phantoms that seduce them.

What effect do you want the arrangement of certain letters to produce? What effect do you want their disorder to disturb? What relation do they have with the winds, that they should calm storms; with gunpowder, that they should overcome its force; with what physicians call the peccant humor and the morbific cause of diseases, that they should cure them?

What is extraordinary is that those who exhaust their reason in trying to relate certain events to occult virtues do not have to make any less effort to keep themselves from seeing the true cause of them.

You will tell me that certain spells have won a battle; and I will tell you that you must blind yourself not to find, in the lay of the ground, in the number or courage of the soldiers, in the experience of the captains, sufficient causes to produce that effect whose cause you want to ignore.

I grant you for a moment that there are spells. Grant me in your turn, for a moment, that there are not; for that is not impossible. This concession that you make me does not prevent two armies from fighting. Do you want it to follow that in that case neither of the two can carry off the victory?

Do you think their fate will remain uncertain until some invisible power comes to determine it? That every blow will be wasted, every prudence vain, and all courage useless?

Do you think that death, made present on such occasions in a thousand ways, cannot produce in men's minds those panics that you have so much trouble explaining? Do you want it to be impossible, in an army of a hundred thousand men, for there to be a single timid man? Do you think that his discouragement cannot produce another man's discouragement? that the second, abandoning a third, would not soon make him abandon a fourth? Nothing more is needed for the despair of conquering suddenly to seize an entire army, and to seize it all the more easily because that army is the more numerous.

Everyone knows, and everyone feels, that men, like all creatures that strive to preserve their being, love life passionately. That is known in general. And yet people go looking for the reason why, in some particular circumstance, they were afraid of losing it.

Although the sacred books of all nations are full of these panic or supernatural terrors, I can imagine nothing so frivolous, because in order to be sure that an effect, which can be produced by a hundred thousand natural causes, is supernatural, one would first have to examine whether none of those causes had acted; which is impossible.

I shall say no more to you about it, Nathanael. It seems to me that the matter does not deserve to be treated so seriously.

*Paris, the 20th day of the moon of Chahban, 1720.*

_P. S._ As I was finishing, I heard cried in the street a letter from a provincial physician to a physician in Paris - for here every trifle is printed, published, and bought. I thought I would do well to send it to you, because it has some relation to our subject. There are many things in it that I do not understand; but you, who are a physician, ought to understand the language of your colleagues.

**Letter from a provincial physician to a physician in Paris**

There was in our town a sick man who had not slept for thirty-five days. His physician prescribed opium for him; but he could not bring himself to take it, and while he held the cup in his hand he was more undecided than ever. At last he said to his physician: `Sir, I ask a truce from you only until tomorrow. I know a man who does not practice medicine, but who has in his house an innumerable quantity of remedies against insomnia. Allow me to send for him; and if I do not sleep tonight, I promise that I will come back to you.' The physician dismissed, the sick man had the curtains drawn and said to a little lackey: `Here, go to Monsieur Anis's and tell him to come speak to me.' Monsieur Anis arrived. `My dear Monsieur Anis, I am dying, I cannot sleep. Would you happen to have in your shop the C. du G., or some devotional book composed by a reverend Jesuit father that you have not been able to sell? For often the remedies kept longest are the best.' `Sir,' said the bookseller, `I have Father Caussin's Holy Court in six volumes, at your service. I shall send it to you, and I hope you will find yourself the better for it. If you want the works of the reverend Father Rodriguez, a Spanish Jesuit, do not deny yourself. But believe me, let us stick to Father Caussin. With God's help, I hope that a single period of Father Caussin will have as much effect on you as a whole leaf of the C. du G.' Thereupon Monsieur Anis went out and ran to fetch the remedy from his shop. The Holy Court arrived. They shook off the dust. The sick man's son, a young schoolboy, began to read it. He was the first to feel the effect. By the second page he could pronounce only in an indistinct voice, and already the whole company felt weakened. A moment later everyone was snoring, except the sick man, who, after being tested for a long time, finally dropped off.

The physician arrived very early the next morning. `Well then! has my opium been taken?' Nobody answered him. The wife, the daughter, the little boy, all transported with joy, showed him Father Caussin. He asked what it was. They said to him: `Long live Father Caussin! We must have him bound. Who would have said it? who would have believed it? It is a miracle! Here, sir, just look at Father Caussin. It is that very volume that made my father sleep.' And thereupon they explained the thing to him just as it had happened.

The physician was a subtle man, filled with the mysteries of the cabala and with the power of words and spirits. That struck him; and after several reflections he resolved to change his practice entirely. `Here is a very singular fact,' he said. `I possess an experiment; it must be pushed further. And why should not a spirit transmit to its work the same qualities that it itself possesses? Do we not see that every day? At least it is worth the trouble of trying. I am tired of apothecaries. Their syrups, their juleps, and all galenic drugs ruin both the sick and their health. Let us change methods. Let us test the virtue of spirits.' On this idea he drew up a new pharmacy, as you will see from the description I am going to give you of the principal remedies he put into practice.

*Light purgative.*

Take three leaves of Aristotle's Logic in Greek; two leaves of some very sharp scholastic theology, such as the subtle Scotus; four of Paracelsus; one of Avicenna; six of Averroes; three of Porphyry; as many of Plotinus; as many of Iamblichus. Infuse the whole for twenty-four hours, and take four doses a day.

*More violent purgative.*

Take ten A*** of the C*** concerning the B*** and the C*** of the I***; distill them in a bain-marie; weaken one drop of the acrid and pungent humor that will come from it in a glass of common water. Swallow the whole with confidence.

*Emetic.*

Take six harangues; a dozen funeral orations, at random, taking care, however, not to make use of those of Monsieur de N.; a collection of new operas; fifty novels; thirty new memoirs. Put the whole into a vessel; leave it to digest for two days; then distill it over a sand fire. And if all that is not enough,

*Another, more powerful.*

Take one leaf of marbled paper that has served to cover a collection of the pieces of the J. F.; infuse it for three minutes; warm one spoonful of this infusion, and swallow.

*Very simple remedy for curing asthma.*

Read all the works of the reverend Father Maimbourg, formerly a Jesuit, taking care to pause only at the end of each period; and you will feel the faculty of breathing return to you little by little, without there being any need to repeat the remedy.

*To preserve against the itch, rash, ringworm, and farcy in horses.*

Take three of Aristotle's categories, two metaphysical degrees, one distinction, six lines of Chapelain, one phrase taken from the letters of Monsieur l'abbé de Saint-Cyran. Write the whole on a piece of paper, which you will fold, attach to a ribbon, and wear at your neck.

*Miraculum chymicum, de violenta fermentatione cum fumo, igne et flammâ.*

Misce Quesnellianam infusionem, cum infusione Lallemaniana; fiat fermentatio cum magna vi, impetu et tonitru, acidis pugnantibus, et invicem penetrantibus alcalinos sales: fiet evaporatio ardentium spirituum. Pone liquorem fermentatum in alembico: nihil inde extrahes, et nihil invenies, nisi caput mortuum.

*Lenitive.*

Recipe Molinæ anodyni chartas duas; Escobaris relaxativi paginas sex; Vasquii emollientis folium unum: infunde in aquæ communis libras iiij. Ad consumptionem dimidiæ partis colentur et exprimantur; et in expressione dissolve Bauni detersivi et Tamburini abluentis folia iij.

Fiat clyster.

*For chlorosis, which the vulgar call pallor, or love-fever.*

Recipe Aretini figuras quatuor; R. Thomæ Sanchii de matrimonio folia ij. Infundantur in aquæ communis libras quinque.

Fiat ptisana aperiens.

These are the drugs that our physician put into practice, with imaginable success. He did not want, he said, so as not to ruin his patients, to employ rare remedies, and ones that are scarcely to be found: for example, a dedicatory epistle that has never made anyone yawn; a preface that is too short; a pastoral charge made by a bishop; and the work of a Jansenist despised by a Jansenist, or admired by a Jesuit. He said that such remedies are fit only to keep charlatanry going, and he had an insurmountable antipathy to that.

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the argumentative structure of the main letter well: Rica begins by mocking amulets and talismans, then shifts into a rational explanation of panic in battle, and finally appends a comic medical satire. The English is lively and often effective, especially in the nested anecdote of Father Caussin as a sleeping draught.

### Main differences
- **Credulous vs superstitious**
  - French: `nous sommes tous deux bien crédules`
  - Fresh translation: `we are both very credulous`
  - Davidson: `two very superstitious people`
  - Davidson interprets rather than translates. `Crédules` is more directly about gullibility.

- **`chiffons sacrés`**
  - Fresh translation: `sacred scraps`
  - Davidson: `sacred scraps`
  - Davidson is very good here; this is one of several places where he is nearly exact.

- **Occult causes and real causes**
  - French: `s'empêcher d'en voir la véritable cause`
  - Fresh translation keeps the active refusal: `keep themselves from seeing the true cause`.
  - Davidson is close, though somewhat smoother in rhythm.

- **Military panic**
  - Fresh translation keeps the cascading logic of one frightened soldier discouraging another, then another.
  - Davidson reproduces the sequence well, but with slightly more polished connective prose.

- **The comic medical coda**
  - French: `avec un succès imaginable`
  - Fresh translation: `with imaginable success`
  - Davidson: `with remarkable success`
  - Davidson suppresses the dry irony. The French sounds deliberately deadpan.

- **Late duplications and apparatus**
  - Davidson's clean text contains intrusive footnotes, editorial glosses, and a duplicated Latin-remedy tail that breaks the structure of the letter.
  - The French is formally neat; the English clean text is materially much messier.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: argumentative, mocking, and progressively more comic; it begins in skeptical prose and ends in learned farce.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the logical force of the anti-superstition argument while preserving the deadpan absurdity of the appended physician letter.
- **Davidson**: generally strong, but a little more explanatory and occasionally too earnest where the French is dryly ridiculous.

### OCR / cleanup notes

- The clean file also drops the explicit heading `LETTRE D'UN MÉDECIN DE PROVINCE A UN MÉDECIN DE PARIS.` as a proper section title in the same tidy way as the French.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLIII is a rich comparison letter because Davidson handles the main argument intelligently, but the surviving English clean text is unusually noisy and editorially cluttered. The biggest translation-pattern issue is his tendency to normalize terms like `crédules`; the biggest practical issue is OCR and apparatus contamination.
