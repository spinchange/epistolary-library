## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LVIII**

**Rica to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
In Paris, my dear Rhédi, there are a great many trades. There one obliging man will offer you, for a little money, the secret of making gold.

Another promises to make you sleep with airy spirits, provided only that you spend thirty years without seeing women.

Next you will find fortune-tellers so skillful that they will tell you the whole course of your life, provided only that they have had a quarter of an hour's conversation with your servants.

Clever women make virginity into a flower that perishes and is reborn every day, and is gathered for the hundredth time more painfully than the first.

There are others who, repairing by the force of their art all the injuries of time, know how to restore to a face a beauty that is wavering, and even call a woman back from the summit of old age to bring her down again to the tenderest youth.

All these people live, or seek to live, in a city that is the mother of invention.

The incomes of its citizens are not farmed out there: they consist only in wit and industry; each has his own, and makes the most of it.

Whoever wished to count all the lawyers pursuing the revenues of some mosque would soon have counted the sands of the sea and the slaves of our monarch.

An infinite number of teachers of languages, arts, and sciences teach what they do not know; and this talent is considerable indeed. For it does not require much wit to show what one knows; but it requires an infinite amount to teach what one does not know.

One can die here only suddenly; death could not otherwise exercise its empire, for in every corner there are people with infallible remedies for all imaginable diseases.

All the shops are hung with invisible nets into which every buyer goes to be caught. Yet one sometimes gets out cheaply enough: a young shopwoman will cajole a man for a whole hour to make him buy a packet of toothpicks.

No one leaves this city as incautious as he entered it: by continually sharing his wealth with others, one learns how to preserve it. That is the only advantage strangers carry away from this enchanting city.

*From Paris, the 10th day of the moon of Saphar, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the catalogue form of the letter well. He preserves the sense that Paris is a marketplace of fraud, ingenuity, cosmetic artifice, and commercial seduction, with each sentence adding another profession to Rica's amused indictment.

### Main differences
- **Sleeping with spirits**
  - French: `coucher avec les esprits aériens`
  - Fresh translation: `sleep with airy spirits`
  - Davidson: `the love of the spirits of the air`
  - Davidson makes the claim more romantic and less bluntly physical.

- **Beauty `that is wavering`**
  - French: `une beauté qui chancelle`
  - Fresh translation keeps the unstable image of beauty faltering.
  - Davidson's `beauty enough to strike one blind` is much more ornamental and not really supported by the French.

- **Wit and industry**
  - French: `ils ne consistent qu'en esprit et en industrie`
  - Fresh translation: `they consist only in wit and industry`
  - Davidson's `skill and industry` is serviceable, but `esprit` matters in a letter devoted to cleverness, fraud, and verbal dexterity.

- **Lawyers vs dervishes**
  - French: `gens de loi`
  - Fresh translation: `lawyers`
  - Davidson: `dervishes`
  - This is a substantial shift. The French is mocking legal practitioners chasing religious revenue, not monks.

- **The city's lesson**
  - French: `plus précautionné`
  - Fresh translation: `more cautious / warier`
  - Davidson preserves the sense, though his closing `sorceress of a city` adds a flourish not present in the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: rapid, list-like, amused, and economically precise; almost every sentence turns on ingenuity used for deception.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the compressed catalogue and Rica's dry urban astonishment.
- **Davidson**: vivid and enjoyable, but occasionally more decorative than the French and at least once materially off (`gens de loi` -> `dervishes`).

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text contains a visible page-marker intrusion near the close: `*33`.
- Davidson's text is otherwise readable, though a few phrases look lightly regularized by OCR cleanup.
- The French clean text is stable and comparatively clear.

## Working conclusion
Letter LVIII is a miniature anatomy of Parisian invention as fraud, trade, and performance. Davidson conveys the pace of the catalogue well, but the fresh translation is stronger where the French depends on concrete terms like `coucher`, `esprit`, and `gens de loi`.
