## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXVI**

**Rica to \*\*\*.**

### Translation
People here are much attached to the sciences, though I do not know whether they are very learned. The man who doubts everything as a philosopher dares deny nothing as a theologian; that contradictory man is always pleased with himself, provided people agree that he has the requisite qualities.

The craze of most Frenchmen is to have wit; and the craze of those who want wit is to write books.

Yet nothing is so badly conceived. Nature seems wisely to have provided that men's follies should be transient, and books immortalize them. A fool ought to be content with having bored everyone who lived with him; he wants to torment future generations as well. He wants his folly to triumph over the oblivion he might have enjoyed like a tomb. He wants posterity to be informed that he lived, and to know forever that he was a fool.

Of all authors, there are none I despise more than compilers, who go everywhere hunting scraps from other people's works and patch them into their own like pieces of turf in a parterre. They are no better than those printing-house workers who arrange type and, by combining letters, produce a book to which they have contributed only their hands. I wish original books to be respected, and it seems to me a kind of profanation to pull out the pieces that compose them from the sanctuary where they belong and expose them to a contempt they do not deserve.

When a man has nothing new to say, why does he not keep quiet? What is the use of these duplicate employments? But I will give you a new example. You are an able man: that is to say, you come into my library and put on the bottom shelf the books that were on the top, and on the top those that were on the bottom. You have produced a masterpiece.

I am writing to you on this subject, \*\*\*, because I am exasperated by a book I have just laid aside, a book so large it seemed to contain universal knowledge; but it has split my head without teaching me anything. Farewell.

*At Paris, the 8th day of the moon of Chahban, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson is very close to the satirical movement of the letter. He preserves Rica's contempt for pretentious half-learning, bad books, and especially compilation as a form of literary vanity.

### Main differences
- **Attachment to science vs actual learning**
  - French: `On s'attache ici beaucoup aux sciences, mais je ne sais si on est fort savant`
  - Fresh translation: `People here are much attached to the sciences, though I do not know whether they are very learned`
  - Davidson: `People are very much devoted to the sciences here, but I question if they are very learned`
  - Davidson is close; the fresh version keeps the slight irony of `s'attacher` more than the warmer `devoted`.

- **Philosopher / theologian contradiction**
  - French: `Celui qui doute de tout comme philosophe n'ose rien nier comme théologien`
  - Fresh translation stays close to the paradox.
  - Davidson's version is also faithful, but his punctuation smooths the abrupt contradiction a little.

- **Having wit vs passing for a wit**
  - French: `d'avoir de l'esprit`
  - Fresh translation: `to have wit`
  - Davidson: `to pass for a wit`
  - Davidson adds the social appearance of wit; the French more simply names the obsession itself.

- **Books immortalize folly**
  - French: `les sottises des hommes fussent passagères, et les livres les immortalisent`
  - Fresh translation: `men's follies should be transient, and books immortalize them`
  - Davidson: `the follies of men should be short-lived; but books interfere and immortalize them`
  - `Interfere` is a lively addition, but it is not in the French.

- **Compilers as patchers**
  - French: `comme des pièces de gazon dans un parterre`
  - Fresh translation keeps the concrete image of turf patches.
  - Davidson's `patch the turf of a lawn` works well, though it domesticates the image slightly.

- **`Doubles emplois`**
  - Fresh translation: `duplicate employments`
  - Davidson: `useless repetitions`
  - Davidson gives the sense, but the French phrase is more pointedly satirical about redundant labor.

- **Final complaint**
  - French: `il m'a rompu la tête sans m'avoir rien appris`
  - Fresh translation: `it has split my head without teaching me anything`
  - Davidson: `it has only split my head without putting anything into it`
  - Davidson is vivid here and close, though a little more colloquial.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dry, epigrammatic, and contemptuous in a very controlled way.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the clean satirical edge and the repeated logic of Rica's complaints.
- **Davidson**: lively and often very good, though sometimes slightly freer in idiom.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXVI is a compact satire on intellectual vanity and bad books. Davidson is broadly faithful, but the fresh translation keeps a little more of the French's dry wording and its mock-administrative way of ridiculing compilation.
