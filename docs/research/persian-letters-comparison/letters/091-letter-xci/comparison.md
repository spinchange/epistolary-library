## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCI**

**Usbek to the same, at Smyrna**

### Translation
From the general passion of the French nation for glory, there has formed in individuals' minds a certain something they call honor. Properly speaking, it is the character of each profession; but it is most marked among men of war, and there it is honor in the fullest sense. It would be very difficult for me to make you feel what it is, because we do not have a precise idea of it.

Formerly the French, above all the nobles, scarcely followed any laws except those of honor. Those laws ruled the whole conduct of their lives, and they were so severe that one could not, without a penalty crueler than death, not merely violate them, but even evade their slightest provision.

When differences had to be settled, they prescribed almost only one mode of decision: the duel, which cut through all difficulties. But the trouble was that judgment was often rendered between parties other than those truly concerned.

As soon as one man was known to another, he had to enter the quarrel and pay with his own person as if he himself had been offended. He always felt honored by such a choice and by so flattering a preference. Thus a man who would not have given four pistoles to save another from the gallows, together with all his family, would make no difficulty about risking his life for him a thousand times.

That way of deciding was badly conceived enough, since it did not follow from one man being more skillful or stronger than another that he had the better reasons.

So kings forbade it under very severe penalties; but in vain. Honor, which always wants to reign, revolts and acknowledges no laws.

Thus the French live in a very violent condition. The same laws of honor oblige an honest man to avenge himself when offended; but justice, on the other hand, punishes him with the cruelest penalties when he does avenge himself. If one follows the laws of honor, one dies on the scaffold; if one follows those of justice, one is forever banished from the society of men. There remains only this cruel alternative: to die, or to be unworthy of living.

*Paris, the 18th day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives a strong account of the French `point d'honneur`, its aristocratic strictness, its link to dueling, and the contradiction between social honor and formal justice.

### Main differences
- **`point d'honneur`**
  - Davidson translates it literally.
  - My draft introduces the phrase and then often uses simply `honor`, which better reflects how the letter treats it as a social code rather than just a technical term.

- **Severity of the code**
  - French says one could not even evade its smallest provision.
  - Davidson's `slightest punctilio` is elegant, though slightly more mannered than the original.

- **`payer de sa personne`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the bodily stake explicit: `pay with his own person`.
  - Davidson is smooth, but a bit more idiomatic.

- **Final alternative**
  - Davidson's `barbarous alternative` is stronger than the French `cruelle alternative`.
  - My draft keeps the colder force of `cruel alternative`.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: social analysis sharpened into paradox.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the legal and moral contradiction prominent.
- **Davidson**: vivid and often excellent, but with slightly higher rhetorical color.
