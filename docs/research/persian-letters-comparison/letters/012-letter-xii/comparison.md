## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
You have seen, my dear Mirza, how the Troglodytes perished through their very wickedness and became the victims of their own injustices. Of so many families, only two remained, and these escaped the disasters of the nation.

There were in that country two very singular men. They had humanity; they knew justice; they loved virtue. Bound to one another as much by the uprightness of their hearts as by the corruption of everyone else's, they saw the general desolation and felt it only through pity: that pity became the motive of a new union between them. They worked with a common solicitude for the common interest. They had no quarrels except those born of a gentle and tender friendship. In the most remote part of the country, separated from compatriots unworthy of their presence, they led a happy and tranquil life. The earth seemed to produce of itself, cultivated by such virtuous hands.

They loved their wives and were tenderly loved by them. Their whole attention was to raise their children in virtue. They constantly set before them the misfortunes of their countrymen and held up that moving example before their eyes. Above all, they made them feel that the interest of individuals is always found in the common interest; that to wish to separate oneself from it is to wish to destroy oneself; that virtue is not something that ought to cost us dearly; that it should not be looked upon as a painful exercise; and that justice toward others is a charity toward ourselves.

They soon had the consolation of virtuous fathers, which is to have children who resemble them. The young people who grew up before their eyes increased through happy marriages. Their number grew, their union remained the same, and virtue, far from weakening in the multitude, was strengthened by a greater number of examples.

Who could describe the happiness of those Troglodytes? So just a people had to be beloved by the gods. As soon as they opened their eyes to know them, they learned to fear them, and religion softened in their manners whatever nature had left too rough.

They instituted festivals in honor of the gods. Young girls adorned with flowers and young men celebrated them with dances and the harmonies of rustic music. Then came feasts, where joy reigned no less than frugality. It was in those gatherings that simple nature spoke; there one learned to give one's heart and to receive one in return; there virginal modesty, blushing, let slip a confession, soon confirmed by the fathers' consent; and there tender mothers took pleasure in foreseeing in advance a gentle and faithful union.

They went to the temple to ask the favors of the gods. It was not riches and burdensome abundance: such wishes were unworthy of the happy Troglodytes, and if they desired them at all it was only for their compatriots. At the foot of the altars they asked only for the health of their fathers, the union of their brothers, the tenderness of their wives, the love and obedience of their children. The girls came there to offer the tender sacrifice of their hearts and asked no other grace than to be able to make a Troglodyte happy.

In the evening, when the flocks left the meadows and the weary oxen had brought back the plow, they gathered together; and over a frugal meal they sang of the injustices of the first Troglodytes and their misfortunes, of virtue reborn in a new people and of its happiness. Then they sang of the greatness of the gods, of their favors always present to men who implore them, and of their inevitable anger toward those who do not fear them. Afterward they described the delights of country life and the happiness of a condition always adorned by innocence. Soon they gave themselves over to a sleep never interrupted by cares or sorrows.

Nature provided for their desires no less than for their needs. In that happy country greed was a stranger. They exchanged gifts in which the giver always believed he had the advantage. The Troglodyte people regarded themselves as a single family. Their flocks were almost always mingled; the only trouble they usually spared themselves was that of separating them.

*From Erzurum, the 6th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson presents the virtuous Troglodytes as a pastoral moral counter-image to the earlier corrupt society. He captures the general pattern well, though his diction often becomes more polished and sentimental than the French.

### Main differences
- **Common interest formula**
  - French: `l'intérêt des particuliers se trouve toujours dans l'intérêt commun`
  - Fresh translation stays close to the political maxim.
  - Davidson renders it well, but in slightly more generalized moral prose.

- **`charité pour nous`**
  - Fresh translation: `justice toward others is a charity toward ourselves`
  - Davidson: `we bestow blessings on ourselves`
  - Davidson captures the sense but loses the deliberately religious word `charity`.

- **Pastoral courtship scene**
  - French: `apprendre à donner le coeur et à le recevoir`
  - Fresh translation keeps the compact image of giving and receiving the heart.
  - Davidson's `make love's bargain of hearts` is lively but more figurative than the original.

- **Burdensome abundance**
  - French: `une onéreuse abondance`
  - Fresh translation: `burdensome abundance`
  - Davidson smooths the expression into more conventional English.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: idealizing, but still morally severe and civic in emphasis.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep both the pastoral sweetness and the underlying political didacticism.
- **Davidson**: graceful and readable, though often slightly more sentimentalized.

## Working conclusion
Letter XII extends the Troglodyte lesson from punishment into civic virtue. Davidson follows it closely, but the French's key ethical vocabulary (`charity`, `common interest`) deserves close attention.
