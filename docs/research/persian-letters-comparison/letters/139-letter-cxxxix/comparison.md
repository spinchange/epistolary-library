## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXXIX**

**Rica to the same**

### Translation
Here is a great example of conjugal tenderness, not only in a woman, but in a queen. The Queen of Sweden, determined at all costs to associate the prince her husband with the crown, has, in order to smooth away every difficulty, sent the Estates a declaration by which she renounces the regency if he is elected.

It is sixty-odd years since another queen, named Christina, abdicated the crown in order to give herself entirely to philosophy. I do not know which of these two examples we ought to admire more.

Although I rather approve of everyone standing firm in the post where nature has placed him, and although I cannot praise the weakness of those who, finding themselves beneath their station, leave it as by a kind of desertion, I am nevertheless struck by the greatness of soul of these two princesses, and by seeing the mind of the one and the heart of the other superior to their fortune. Christina thought of knowing at the time when others think only of enjoying; and the other wishes to enjoy only in order to place all her happiness in the hands of her august husband.

*Paris, the 27th day of the moon of Maharram, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson is very close here. The letter is short, epigrammatic, and easy to align, and he preserves the contrast between Queen Christina's abdication for philosophy and the Swedish queen's self-renunciation for conjugal affection.

### Main differences
- **Regency vs government**
  - French: `elle se désiste de la régence`
  - Fresh translation: `she renounces the regency`
  - Davidson: `resigning the regency`
  - Davidson is essentially correct; this is one of the places where the versions are nearly identical.

- **Station and desertion**
  - French: `le poste où la nature l'a mis` and `une espèce de désertion`
  - Fresh translation preserves both the image of a fixed post and the military metaphor of desertion.
  - Davidson also keeps the point, but in slightly smoother English.

- **`supérieurs à leur fortune`**
  - Fresh translation: `superior to their fortune`
  - Davidson: `rise superior to their fortunes`
  - Davidson turns the phrase into a more elevated Victorian idiom. The French is plainer and more pointed.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: concise, moralizing, and admiring without losing its aphoristic form.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the compactness and the final balancing contrast between heart and mind.
- **Davidson**: very faithful, with only light elevation of register.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text contains a clear OCR slip: `Faris` for `Paris`.
- It also reads `ht an age` where the sense demands `at an age`.
- Because the letter is so short, these small defects stand out more than usual.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXXIX is one of the closest matches so far. Davidson's rendering is solid and only slightly more elevated in tone than the French; the main cleanup concern is OCR, not translation drift.
