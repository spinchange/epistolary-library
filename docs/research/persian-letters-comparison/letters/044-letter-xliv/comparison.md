## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLIV**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
In France there are three estates: the Church, the sword, and the robe. Each has sovereign contempt for the other two: thus a man who ought to be despised because he is a fool is often despised only because he belongs to the robe.

Even the lowliest artisans dispute over the excellence of the trade they have chosen. Each sets himself above men of another profession in proportion to the superiority he imagines in his own.

Men all resemble, more or less, that woman from the province of Erivan who, having received some favor from one of our monarchs, wished him a thousand times over, in the blessings she gave him, that Heaven might make him governor of Erivan.

I read in a travel account that a French ship, having put in on the coast of Guinea, sent some of its crew ashore to buy sheep. They were led before the king, who was dispensing justice to his subjects beneath a tree. He sat upon his throne — that is, on a piece of wood — as proudly as if he had been seated on that of the Great Mogul. He had three or four guards with wooden pikes; a parasol like a canopy sheltered him from the heat of the sun; all the ornaments of both him and the queen his wife consisted in their black skin and a few rings. This prince, more vain than miserable, asked the foreigners whether people talked much of him in France. He imagined his name ought to be carried from one pole to the other; and, unlike that conqueror of whom it was said that he had made all the earth keep silence, he imagined that he ought to make the whole universe talk.

When the khan of Tartary has dined, a herald cries out that all the princes of the earth may go and dine if they wish. And that barbarian, who lives on milk, has no house, and survives only by brigandage, regards all the kings of the world as his slaves and insults them regularly twice a day.

*From Paris, the 28th day of the moon of Rhegeb, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the structure and satirical point of the letter well: social estate vanity, the Erivan anecdote, the Guinea king, and the khan's absurd universal pretensions. The sequence and governing irony remain intact.

### Main differences
- **Three estates vs three privileged classes**
  - French: `trois sortes d'états`
  - Fresh translation: `three estates`
  - Davidson: `three privileged classes`
  - Davidson makes the social meaning explicit, but the French has the more compact political term.
- **The robe**
  - French: `la robe`
  - Fresh translation keeps the institutional term.
  - Davidson turns it into `lawyer`, which narrows a broader magistrate/judicial estate.
- **Black skin detail**
  - French: `leur peau noire`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stark phrasing.
  - Davidson's `their own black skins` is vivid but slightly more rhetorical.
- **Make the universe talk**
  - French: `il devoit faire parler tout l'univers`
  - Fresh translation preserves the active conceit.
  - Davidson's `kept the whole world chattering` is lively but more idiomatic and less pointed.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dry, satirical, and structurally cumulative.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the deadpan progression from local vanity to geopolitical absurdity.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, but sometimes a shade freer in the final sting of an image.

## Working conclusion
Letter XLIV shows Davidson at his best in satirical prose: he is generally faithful and often elegant. The main differences are slight narrowing choices such as `lawyer` for `la robe` and a somewhat more idiomatic English energy.
