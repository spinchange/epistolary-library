## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXXVII**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The king of France is old. We have no example in our histories of a monarch who has reigned so long. It is said that he possesses to a very high degree the talent of making himself obeyed: with the same genius he governs his family, his court, and his state. He has often been heard to say that, of all the governments in the world, that of the Turks, or that of our august sultan, would please him best: so highly does he esteem Oriental policy.

I have studied his character, and found in it contradictions that I am unable to resolve. For example, he has a minister who is only eighteen years old, and a mistress who is eighty; he loves his religion, and cannot endure those who say it must be observed with strictness; although he shuns the tumult of cities and communicates little with others, he is occupied from morning until night only with making people talk about him; he loves trophies and victories, but he fears seeing a good general at the head of his troops as much as he would have reason to fear him at the head of an enemy army. I do not think it has ever happened to anyone but him to be at once loaded with more riches than a prince could hope for, and crushed by a poverty that a private person could not bear.

He likes to reward those who serve him; but he pays as liberally for the assiduities, or rather the idleness, of his courtiers as for the laborious campaigns of his captains; he often prefers a man who undresses him, or who hands him the napkin when he sits down to table, to another who takes cities for him or wins him battles; he does not believe sovereign greatness should be constrained in the distribution of favors; and without examining whether the man he heaps with benefits has merit, he believes his choice will make him so. Thus one has seen him give a small pension to a man who had fled two leagues, and a fine governorship to another who had fled four.

He is magnificent, above all in his buildings: there are more statues in the gardens of his palace than citizens in a large city. His guard is as strong as that of the prince before whom all thrones are overthrown; his armies are as numerous, his resources as great, and his finances as inexhaustible.

*From Paris, the 7th day of the moon of Maharram, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the structure of the portrait and most of its ironies: long reign, absolutist control, personal contradictions, arbitrary reward, and theatrical magnificence. His English is effective, but it sometimes explains or moderates formulations whose force in French lies in their compact exactness.

### Main differences
- **`Politique orientale`**
  - French: `il fait de cas de la politique orientale`
  - Fresh translation: `he esteems Oriental policy`
  - Davidson: `his high opinion of Oriental statecraft`
  - Davidson's `statecraft` is plausible, but a little more elevated and interpretive.

- **Making himself talked about**
  - French: `il n'est occupé ... qu'à faire parler de lui`
  - Fresh translation: `he is occupied ... only with making people talk about him`
  - Davidson: `he is engaged in getting himself talked about`
  - Davidson is close; the main point is the line's dry, almost mechanical self-publicity.

- **Wealth and poverty in the same figure**
  - French: `comblé ... et accablé d'une pauvreté`
  - Fresh translation: `loaded with more riches ... and crushed by a poverty`
  - Davidson: `burdened with more wealth ... and yet at the same time steeped in such poverty`
  - `Steeped` is less exact than the French opposition between being heaped up with riches and weighed down by poverty.

- **Court idleness versus military labor**
  - French: `les assiduités, ou plutôt l'oisiveté`
  - Fresh translation: `the assiduities, or rather the idleness`
  - Davidson: `the assiduous indolence`
  - Davidson's phrase is elegant, but more stylized than the French's blunt correction.

- **The line about fleeing**
  - French: `fui deux lieues` / `fui quatre`
  - Fresh translation keeps the plain repetition of `fled`.
  - Davidson: `run off two leagues from the enemy` / `gone four`
  - Davidson clarifies slightly, but the French is more curt and mocking.

- **Thrones `overthrown`, not merely `trembling`**
  - French: `le prince devant qui tous les trônes se renversent`
  - Fresh translation: `the prince before whom all thrones are overthrown`
  - Davidson: `the prince before whom all the thrones of the earth tremble`
  - This is a real shift in image and intensity: `renversent` suggests toppling, not just fear.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dryly admiring on the surface, but heavily ironic in its accumulation of contradictions.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the straight-faced cadence of the satire and its abrupt, memorable formulations.
- **Davidson**: strong overall, though occasionally more rhetorical and less cutting than the French.
