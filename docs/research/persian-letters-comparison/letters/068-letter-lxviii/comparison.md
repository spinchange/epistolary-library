## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXVIII**

**Rica to Usbek, at \*\*\*.**

### Translation
The other day I went to dine with a magistrate who had invited me several times. After we had spoken of many things, I said to him: Sir, it seems to me that your profession must be very taxing. Not nearly so much as you imagine, he answered: the way we practice it, it is only an amusement. But how, I said, is your head not always full of other people's business? Are you not constantly occupied with things that do not interest you? You are right, he answered: those things are not interesting, for we take so little interest in them that it is almost nothing; and that is exactly why the profession is not so tiring as you say. When I saw that he took the matter so lightly, I went on and said to him: Sir, I have not seen your study. I believe that, he answered, because I have none. When I took this office, I needed money to pay my fees; I sold my library, and out of a prodigious number of volumes the bookseller left me only my account book. It is not that I regret them: we judges do not swell ourselves with vain learning. What have we to do with all those volumes of law? Nearly all cases are hypothetical and fall outside the general rule. But might it not be, sir, I said to him, because you yourselves make them fall outside it? After all, why should all the peoples in the world have laws if those laws are not to be applied? And how can one apply them if one does not know them? If you knew the Palace, the magistrate replied, you would not speak as you do: we have living books, namely the advocates; they work for us and take it upon themselves to instruct us. And do they not also sometimes take it upon themselves to deceive you? I replied. Then it would not be amiss for you to guard against their snares; they have weapons with which to attack your equity; it would be good if you had some as well to defend it, and if you did not go throwing yourself into the fray lightly dressed among people armored to the teeth.

*At Paris, the 13th day of the moon of Chahban, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures the point of the exchange well: Rica's irony exposes a judicial culture in which magistrates can boast of ignorance because lawyers do the real intellectual work.

### Main differences
- **`Homme de robe`**
  - Fresh translation: `magistrate`
  - Davidson: `magistrate`
  - Both versions are sensible, though the French idiom points a little more specifically to a robe-wearing judicial officeholder.

- **Lack of interest as professional method**
  - French: `nous nous y intéressons si peu que rien`
  - Fresh translation: `we take so little interest in them that it is almost nothing`
  - Davidson: `we take not the least interest in them`
  - Davidson is sharper and more idiomatic; the fresh version stays closer to the odd French construction.

- **Hypothetical cases**
  - French: `Presque tous les cas sont hypothétiques`
  - Fresh translation: `Nearly all cases are hypothetical`
  - Davidson: `Almost all the cases are questions of fact`
  - This is the largest substantive difference in the letter. Davidson departs noticeably from the wording of the French.

- **Living books**
  - French: `nous avons des livres vivants, qui sont les avocats`
  - Fresh translation: `we have living books, namely the advocates`
  - Davidson: `We have living books, the advocates`
  - Davidson is very close here.

- **Lightly dressed / armed to the teeth**
  - French: `habillés à la légère, parmi des gens cuirassés jusqu'aux dents`
  - Fresh translation keeps the contrast between being lightly dressed and facing men armored to the teeth.
  - Davidson's `thinly clad` is effective, though a little more literary than the French phrase requires.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, ironic, and conversational, with the punch landing on the magistrate's cheerful anti-intellectualism.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the dialogue moving while preserving Rica's escalating sarcasm.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, though his handling of `cas hypothétiques` deserves checking against the source.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXVIII is a compact institutional satire. Davidson handles most of it well, but the fresh translation stays closer to the French wording at the key point where the magistrate claims that cases are `hypothetical` rather than simply matters of fact.
