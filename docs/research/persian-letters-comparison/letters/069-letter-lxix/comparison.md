## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXIX**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
You would never have imagined that I had become more of a metaphysician than I was before: yet so it is, and you will be convinced when you have weathered this overflow of my philosophy.

The soundest philosophers who have reflected on the nature of God have said that he is a supremely perfect being; but they have greatly abused that idea. They have drawn up a list of all the different perfections man is capable of possessing and imagining, and they have loaded the idea of the divinity with them, without considering that those attributes often obstruct one another and cannot subsist in the same subject without destroying one another.

The poets of the West say that when a painter wanted to make a portrait of the goddess of beauty, he gathered together the loveliest Greek women and took from each whatever she had of most grace, making from it all a whole meant to resemble the fairest of goddesses. If a man had concluded from this that she was blond and dark, that she had black and blue eyes, that she was gentle and proud, he would have passed for ridiculous.

God often lacks a perfection that would give him a great imperfection; but he is never limited except by himself; he is himself his necessity. Thus, though God is all-powerful, he cannot violate his promises or deceive mankind. Often indeed the incapacity lies not in him but in the things themselves; and that is why he cannot change essences.

So there is no reason to be astonished that some of our doctors have dared to deny God's infinite foreknowledge, on the ground that it is incompatible with his justice.

Bold though that idea may be, metaphysics lends itself to it wonderfully. According to its principles, it is impossible for God to foresee things that depend on the determination of free causes, because what has not happened is not, and consequently cannot be known; for nothingness, having no properties, cannot be perceived. God cannot read in a will that does not yet exist, nor see in the soul something that is not in it; for until it has determined itself, the action that determines it is not there.

The soul is the artisan of its determination; but there are occasions when it is so indeterminate that it does not even know to which side it should determine itself. Often it determines itself only in order to make use of its freedom; so God cannot see that determination in advance, either in the action of the soul or in the action objects have upon it.

How could God foresee things that depend on the determination of free causes? He could see them only in two ways: by conjecture, which contradicts infinite foreknowledge; or else he would see them as necessary effects that would infallibly follow from a cause producing them just as infallibly, which is even more contradictory. For the soul would be free in the supposition, and in fact it would be no freer than a billiard ball is free to move when it is struck by another.

Do not think, however, that I mean to limit God's knowledge. Since he makes creatures act as he pleases, he knows all that he wishes to know. But although he can see everything, he does not always make use of that power. He ordinarily leaves the creature the power to act or not to act, so as to leave it the power to merit or demerit. It is then that he renounces the right he has to act on it and determine it. But when he wants to know something, he always knows it, because he has only to will that it should happen as he sees it, and to determine creatures in conformity with his will. In that way he draws what must happen out of the number of purely possible things by fixing through his decrees the future determinations of minds and depriving them of the power he had given them to act or not to act.

If one may use a comparison in a matter above all comparison: a monarch does not know what his ambassador will do in an important affair; if he wants to know, he has only to order him to conduct himself in such-and-such a way, and he can be certain that the thing will happen as he planned it.

The Koran and the books of the Jews never cease rising up against the dogma of absolute foreknowledge. God appears there everywhere as ignorant of the future determinations of minds; and it seems that this was the first truth Moses taught to men.

God places Adam in the earthly paradise on condition that he not eat a certain fruit: an absurd command in a being who would know the future determinations of souls. For in the end, can such a being attach conditions to his graces without making them ridiculous? It is as if a man who knew beforehand of the capture of Baghdad said to another: I will give you a thousand écus if Baghdad is not taken. Would that not be a poor joke?

My dear Rhédi, why so much philosophy? God is so high that we do not even perceive his clouds. We know him well only in his precepts. He is immense, spiritual, infinite. May his greatness bring us back to our weakness. To humble oneself always is to adore him always.

*At Paris, the last day of the moon of Chahban, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the argumentative line of the letter closely: critique of composite divine perfection, defense of free causes against absolute foreknowledge, and a final retreat from speculation into humility before God's greatness.

### Main differences
- **God as his own necessity**
  - French: `il est lui-même sa nécessité`
  - Fresh translation: `he is himself his necessity`
  - Davidson: `He is His own law`
  - Davidson makes the phrase smoother and more theological, but the French is stranger and more metaphysical.

- **Essences**
  - French: `il ne peut pas changer les essences`
  - Fresh translation: `he cannot change essences`
  - Davidson: `He cannot change the nature of things`
  - Davidson paraphrases rather than translating the scholastic term.

- **Renouncing the right to determine creatures**
  - French: `il renonce au droit qu'il a d'agir sur elle, et de la déterminer`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stronger language of divine non-intervention.
  - Davidson turns the phrase into `renounces the absolute authority`, which is interpretive and more political in tone.

- **A thousand écus**
  - French: `Je vous donne mille écus`
  - Fresh translation: `I will give you a thousand écus`
  - Davidson: `I will give you a hundred tomans`
  - This is a genuine translation substitution rather than a mere stylistic variation.

- **Clouds of God**
  - French: `nous n'apercevons pas même ses nuages`
  - Fresh translation: `we do not even perceive his clouds`
  - Davidson: `we cannot perceive even His clouds`
  - Davidson is very close here and preserves the striking image well.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: speculative, argumentative, and self-consciously abstract, before ending in devotional modesty.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the conceptual precision without making the prose heavier than it already is.
- **Davidson**: often excellent in this letter, though he occasionally translates technical language into smoother English theology.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXIX is one of the more philosophically explicit letters in the sequence. Davidson generally handles it well, but the fresh translation keeps closer to the French's technical wording, especially around `necessity`, `essences`, and the Baghdad example.
