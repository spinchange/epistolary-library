## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCIII**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
The monarch who reigned so long is no more. He gave people much to talk about during his life; everyone was silent at his death. Firm and courageous at that last moment, he seemed to yield only to destiny. Thus died the great Shah Abbas, after filling the whole earth with his name.

Do not think that this great event has produced here only moral reflections. Everyone thought of his own affairs and of how to take advantage of the change. The king, great-grandson of the dead monarch, being only five years old, a prince, his uncle, has been declared regent of the kingdom.

The late king had made a will that limited the regent's authority. This skillful prince went before the parlement, and there, laying out all the rights of his birth, had the dead monarch's arrangement annulled, he who, wishing to survive himself, seemed to want to reign even after death.

Parlements are like those ruins one tramples underfoot, but which still recall the idea of some temple famous in the old religion of the peoples. They deal now hardly at all except with the administration of justice; and their authority is always languishing, unless some unforeseen occasion comes to restore force and life to it. These great bodies have followed the destiny of human things: they have yielded to time, which destroys everything; to corruption of morals, which has weakened everything; and to supreme authority, which has struck everything down.

But the regent, wishing to make himself pleasing to the people, first appeared to respect this image of public liberty; and, as though he meant to raise up from the ground both temple and idol, he wanted them to be regarded as the support of monarchy and the foundation of all legitimate authority.

*Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Rhegeb, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the political sequence well: the death of Louis XIV, the regency, the cancellation of the late king's testament, and the symbolic revival of parlement as a vestige of public liberty.

### Main differences
- **Silence at death**
  - Davidson's `everybody is silent at his death` is close.
  - The French is slightly starker because it places the silence directly against all the talk of the monarch's life.

- **`se survivre à lui-même`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the irony of a king wishing to outlive himself.
  - Davidson is good here too, but the French phrasing is especially pointed.

- **Temple image**
  - Davidson preserves the ruin/temple metaphor.
  - My draft keeps more of the French's compact parallelism in the final paragraph.

- **`liberté publique`**
  - Davidson turns the phrase into somewhat grander constitutional English.
  - The French remains formal, but more imagistic than doctrinal.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: observant, political, and faintly sardonic.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the symbolic language while staying modern.
- **Davidson**: stately and effective, though slightly more elevated in constitutional tone.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean file contains intrusive footnote material in the middle of the parlement paragraph.
- The French clean file also preserves an editorial note after the opening sentence.
- The letter remains usable in both witnesses, but those interruptions should be ignored in close comparison.

## Working conclusion
Davidson tracks the event sequence well. The main comparative gain is in preserving the letter's balance between immediate court politics and the striking ruin/temple metaphor for French institutions.
