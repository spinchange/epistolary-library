## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLVIII**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
Those who love to learn are never idle. Although I am charged with no important business, I am continually occupied. I spend my life examining things; in the evening I write down what I have noticed, seen, and heard during the day. Everything interests me, everything astonishes me: I am like a child whose still-tender organs are vividly struck by the smallest objects.

You might not believe it, but we are well received in every circle and society. I think we owe much of that to Rica's lively wit and natural gaiety, which make him seek everyone out and make everyone seek him in turn. Our foreign air now offends no one; indeed we enjoy the surprise people feel on discovering us to have some politeness, for the French do not imagine that our climate produces men. Yet it must be admitted that they are worth undeceiving.

I spent several days in a country house near Paris with a man of standing who is delighted to have company around him. He has a very agreeable wife who joins great modesty to a gaiety that the secluded life of our Persian women always takes away from them. As a foreigner, I had nothing better to do than study, as is my custom, the crowd of people who were constantly arriving there, whose characters always offered me something new.

I first noticed a man whose simplicity pleased me; I attached myself to him, and he to me, so that we were always beside one another. One day, while we were talking apart in a large company and leaving the general conversation to itself, I begged him to answer some questions, since I was weary of living among people I could not sort out. I asked him first about a man who boasted of the great dinners he had given, spoke familiarly of dukes and ministers, and yet had a mean face and no breeding. He laughed and told me that the man was a tax farmer: far above others by wealth and below everyone by birth. He would have the best table in Paris if he could bring himself never to dine at home. He is impertinent, he added, but his cook is excellent, and at least he is grateful to him.

I then asked about a stout man dressed in black, whom a lady had seated beside her, a man with a mournful coat but a cheerful face, who smiled graciously whenever he was spoken to. My companion told me it was a preacher — worse still, a director of consciences. He knows more than husbands do about women; he knows their weak side, and they know his. In public he thunders; in private he is gentle as a lamb. He is a necessary man in retired life, one who relieves headaches with little counsels, attentive visits, and officious cares.

Then I asked about a badly dressed man opposite us, who grimaced, spoke oddly, lacked wit to speak well, yet spoke in order to have wit. That, he said, is a poet, the grotesque of the human race. Such people claim they were born what they are, and indeed will remain so all their lives: almost always the most ridiculous of men. This one has been driven by hunger into the house, where the master and mistress receive him kindly; he wrote their epithalamium when they married, and it turned out to be the best thing he ever did.

My companion added, to correct my Eastern prejudices, that there are happy marriages in France and women whose virtue is a severe enough guardian. The couple we were discussing are peaceful and universally esteemed; their only fault is to admit all sorts of people, which sometimes brings bad company among them. Still, he said, the so-called good company are often only those whose vice is more refined, and perhaps they resemble poisons, the subtler ones being the most dangerous.

Lowering my voice, I next asked about an old man who looked so sour, dressed unlike the others, condemned everything done in France, and seemed to dislike the government. My companion said he was an old soldier who makes himself memorable to all his hearers by the length of his exploits. He cannot endure that France should have won battles in which he was absent, nor that a siege should be praised where he did not mount the trench. He believes history ended where he ended; his wounds seem to him the ruin of the monarchy. Unlike philosophers who say only the present is real, he lives only in the past and exists only in the campaigns he once served. Why, I asked, did he leave military service? He did not leave it, came the answer; service left him. He now fills a small post where he will recount his adventures for the rest of his days. In France, my companion added, officers who have grown old in subordinate positions are rarely raised higher: men think that a true general must already have, by thirty, that sweeping view of terrain and that presence of mind which use every advantage in victory and every resource in defeat.

A little later, I asked one last question: who was that tall young man with his own hair, little wit, and so much impertinence? My companion said he was a fashionable man of gallantry. Soon afterward chance placed the fellow beside me, and he invited me to walk with him in the garden. He told me he had come to the country to please the mistress of the house, with whom he was not ill acquainted. Some other woman would be angry, he said, but what did that matter? He saw the prettiest women in Paris, never fixed himself on one, and took pleasure in making husbands rage, fathers despair, and women fear losing him. A number of young men, he said, divide Paris among themselves in this way and keep the whole city attentive to their slightest moves. I answered that such a man made more noise than the bravest warrior and was more considered than a grave magistrate; but that in Persia he would be thought better suited to guard women than to please them. The blood rushed to my face, and had I gone on speaking I think I would have treated him roughly.

What do you say to a country where such men are tolerated, where faithlessness, abduction, perfidy, and injustice lead to consideration, and where a man is esteemed for taking a daughter from her father or a wife from her husband? Happy the children of Ali, who defend their families from dishonor and seduction. Daylight itself is not purer than the fire burning in the hearts of our women. Our daughters think with trembling of the day that will deprive them of that virtue which makes them like angels and incorporeal powers. Beloved native land, on which the sun casts its first gaze, you are not stained by the horrible crimes that force that star to hide itself as soon as it appears in the dark West.

*At Paris, the 5th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries over the long salon sequence clearly: the tax farmer, confessor, poet, veteran, and libertine each embody a social type, before the letter closes with Rica's fierce moral recoil against French gallantry.

### Main differences
- **Tax farmer vs farmer-general**
  - French: `un fermier`
  - Fresh translation: `a tax farmer`
  - Davidson: `a farmer-general`
  - Davidson historicizes accurately for ancien régime France, but the French wording itself is plainer.
- **Director of consciences**
  - French: `un directeur`
  - Fresh translation keeps the idea explicit.
  - Davidson's `confessor` narrows a broader social-religious role.
- **Poet as grotesque**
  - French: `le grotesque du genre humain`
  - Fresh translation: `the grotesque of the human race`
  - Davidson: `the grotesquest of human kind`
  - Davidson is vivid here; the fresh version stays slightly closer to the noun-based insult.
- **Final moral indictment**
  - The French ending is a deliberate, almost prophetic swing from comic observation to civilizational denunciation.
  - Davidson preserves it, but in a somewhat more Victorian rhetoric.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: observational, witty, then suddenly severe and indignant.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the typological comedy but preserves the seriousness of the close.
- **Davidson**: excellent on social caricature, though a little more literary and less abrupt in the moral turn.
