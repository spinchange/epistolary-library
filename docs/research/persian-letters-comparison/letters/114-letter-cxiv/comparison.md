## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXIV**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
The world, my dear Rhédi, is not incorruptible; even the heavens are not. Astronomers are eyewitnesses to all their changes, which are the very natural effects of the universal motion of matter.

The earth, like the other planets, is subject to the same laws of motion; within itself it suffers a perpetual struggle among its principles. Sea and continent seem to be engaged in an eternal war; every instant produces new combinations.

Men, in a dwelling so exposed to change, are in an equally uncertain condition: a hundred thousand causes can act upon them, and the smallest can destroy them, and all the more can increase or diminish their number.

I will not speak to you of those particular catastrophes, so common in historians, that have destroyed entire cities and kingdoms. There are more general ones, which have many times brought the human race within two fingers' breadth of ruin.

Histories are full of those universal plagues that have in turn laid waste the world. They speak, among others, of one so violent that it burned plants down to their roots, and made itself felt throughout the known world, as far as the empire of Cathay: one degree more of corruption might perhaps, in a single day, have destroyed the whole human race.

It is not yet two centuries since the most shameful of all diseases made itself felt in Europe, Asia, and Africa; in a very short time it produced prodigious effects. It would have been all over with mankind if it had continued its progress with the same fury. Crushed by afflictions from birth, incapable of bearing the weight of society's burdens, men would have perished miserably.

What would it have been if the poison had been a little more virulent? And it would no doubt have become so, if people had not been fortunate enough to discover a remedy as powerful as the one that was found. Perhaps that disease, by attacking the organs of generation, would have attacked generation itself.

But why speak of the destruction that might have happened to the human race? Has it not in fact happened already, and did not the Flood reduce it to a single family?

Can those who know nature, and who have a reasonable idea of God, understand how matter and created things can be only six thousand years old? That God should have delayed his works throughout all eternity, and have used his creative power only yesterday? Would that be because he could not, or because he would not? But if he could not at one time, he could not at another. So it must be because he would not. But since there is no succession in God, if one admits that he willed something once, he willed it always, and from the beginning.

One must not, then, count the years of the world; the number of grains of sand in the sea is no more comparable to them than an instant is.

And yet all historians speak to us of a first father; they show us human nature at its birth. Is it not natural to think that Adam was saved from some common disaster, just as Noah was saved from the Flood, and that such great events have often occurred on the earth since the creation of the world?

But not all destructions are violent. We see several parts of the earth grow weary of providing for the subsistence of men: what do we know whether the whole earth may not have general causes of weariness, slow and imperceptible?

I have been glad to give you these general ideas before replying more particularly to your letter on the decrease in population that has taken place over the last seventeen or eighteen centuries. In a following letter I shall show you that, independently of physical causes, there are moral ones that have produced this effect.

*Paris, the 8th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the sequence and argumentative shape of the letter quite closely. He preserves the large movement from cosmic corruption to plague, from plague to creation history, and from there to depopulation. His version is readable and often faithful, but it sometimes interprets the philosophical argument instead of simply carrying over Montesquieu's terms.

### Main differences
- **Opening term: `le monde`**
  - Fresh translation: `The world ... is not incorruptible`
  - Davidson: `The earth ... is not incorruptible`
  - The French says `world`, not `earth`, so Davidson narrows the scope at the very start.

- **Creation argument reframed**
  - French: `Ceux qui connoissent la nature, et qui ont de Dieu une idée raisonnable`
  - Fresh translation keeps the direct appeal to those who know nature and think rationally about God.
  - Davidson instead introduces `philosophers who distinguish two creations`, which is not in the French and gives the passage a more doctrinal cast.

- **Image of immeasurable age**
  - French: `le nombre des grains de sable de la mer`
  - Fresh translation preserves the explicit comparison with the grains of sand in the sea.
  - Davidson omits this sentence entirely, which removes one of the letter's strongest images.

- **Cathay / China**
  - Fresh translation: `the empire of Cathay`
  - Davidson: `the empire of China`
  - This is an updating move. It makes the reference clearer for modern readers, but it is less exact to the historical wording.

- **Disease and generation**
  - French: `attaquant les parties de la génération ... auroit attaqué la génération même`
  - Fresh translation keeps the pointed repetition: the disease might have attacked generation itself.
  - Davidson is close, but his phrasing is slightly smoother and less rhetorically emphatic.

- **Final transition to depopulation**
  - Davidson compresses the last two French paragraphs more heavily than the fresh translation does.
  - The French makes a distinct pause between universal destructions and the slower `weariness` of the earth; keeping that separation clarifies the logic of the letter.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: speculative, philosophical, and deliberately wide-ranging; it moves from natural philosophy to scriptural history without any sense of contradiction.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the letter's grave, probing tone while keeping the syntax readable in contemporary English.
- **Davidson**: generally lucid, but a little more explanatory and Victorian in phrasing, especially in the theological argument.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text still preserves a footnote intrusion around `a remedy as power- / ful as any yet discovered ! 1`.
- Davidson's text also introduces material not present in the French in the creation paragraph, so that passage should be treated as a real interpretive divergence, not just an OCR problem.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXIV shows Davidson at his most competent as a carrier of large argumentative structure, but also willing to reshape details when the philosophical prose becomes difficult. The most important points here are the narrowing of `world` to `earth`, the insertion of a different framing for the creation argument, and the omission of the grains-of-sand sentence.
