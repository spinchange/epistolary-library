### Heading
**Letter CXXXV**

**Rica to the same**

### Translation
I came back at the appointed hour, and my man led me precisely to the place where we had left one another.

`Here,' he said to me, `are the grammarians, the glossators, and the commentators.' `My father,' I said to him, `can all those people do without good sense?' `Yes,' he said, `they can; and it does not show, nor are their works any worse for it; which is very convenient for them.' `That is true,' I said to him; `and I know a good many philosophers who would do well to apply themselves to that sort of learning.'

`There,' he continued, `are the orators, who have the talent of persuading independently of reasons; and the geometers, who force a man, whether he wants to or not, to be persuaded, and convince him tyrannically.

`Here are the books of metaphysics, which deal with such great interests, and in which the infinite appears everywhere; the books of physics, which find no more marvel in the economy of the vast universe than in the simplest machine made by our craftsmen; the books of medicine, those monuments to the frailty of nature and the power of art, which make us tremble when they treat even the lightest illnesses, so much do they bring death before our eyes; but which put us in complete security when they speak of the virtue of remedies, as though we had become immortal.

`Very near these are the books of anatomy, which contain far less a description of the parts of the human body than the barbarous names that have been given to them: a thing that cures neither the sick man of his illness nor the physician of his ignorance.

`Here is chemistry, which dwells now in the hospital and now in the madhouse, as in two residences equally suited to it.

`Here are the books of science, or rather of occult ignorance: such are those that contain some species of devilry; detestable according to most people, pitiable according to me. Such too are the books of judicial astrology.' `What are you saying, my father?' I replied hotly. `Books of judicial astrology! Those are the very books we value most in Persia: they regulate all the actions of our lives, and decide us in all our undertakings; astrologers are properly our directors; more than that, they enter into the government of the state.' `If that is so,' he said to me, `you live under a yoke much harsher than that of reason: that is what may truly be called the strangest of all empires. I very much pity a family, and even more a nation, that lets itself be so completely ruled by the planets.' `We use astrology,' I replied to him, `as you use algebra. Every nation has its science, by which it regulates its politics. All the astrologers together have never committed in our Persia as many follies as a single one of your algebraists has committed here. Do you think the accidental conjunction of the stars is not as reliable a rule as the fine reasonings of your system-maker? If votes were counted on that point in France and in Persia, it would be a fine triumph for astrology; you would see mathematicians very much humiliated. What a crushing corollary one might draw from that against them!'

Our dispute was interrupted, and we had to part.

*Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the guided-tour structure and the antithetical wit of the letter. His English is fluent and generally accurate, and he catches the joke that the sciences are being classified as much by vanity and social effect as by truth.

### Main differences
- **Good sense and scholarly uselessness**
  - French: `tous ces gens-là ne peuvent-ils pas se dispenser d'avoir du bon sens?`
  - Fresh translation keeps the bluntness: `can all those people do without good sense?`
  - Davidson's phrasing is close, but he adds a slightly more conversational polish.

- **The power of geometry**
  - French: `le convainquent avec tyrannie`
  - Fresh translation: `convince him tyrannically`
  - Davidson: `conquer him by sheer force`
  - Davidson gets the violence, but `tyrannically` is closer to the pointed political metaphor in the French.

- **Chemistry vs alchemy**
  - French: `Voici la chimie`
  - Fresh translation: `Here is chemistry`
  - Davidson: `Here are the alchemists`
  - This is a real shift. Montesquieu names the field, not its practitioners, and Davidson nudges the satire toward a more personified target.

- **Astrologers as `directeurs`**
  - Fresh translation: `our directors`
  - Davidson: `our spiritual fathers`
  - `Directeurs` suggests guides or directors of conscience; `spiritual fathers` is interpretive and more ecclesiastical than the French requires.

- **`algébriste` and political satire**
  - Fresh translation: `one of your algebraists`
  - Davidson: `a single algebraist`
  - The difference is small, but Davidson's surrounding phrasing tends to smooth the satire into polished English rather than leaving it as a sharp absurdity.

- **`faiseur de système`**
  - Fresh translation: `system-maker`
  - Davidson: `system-monger`
  - Davidson's choice is lively and not wrong, but it is somewhat more idiomatic and colorful than the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, mocking, and structurally neat; each discipline gets one satirical stroke.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the categorial rhythm and the bite of the final astrology exchange.
- **Davidson**: elegant and readable, though slightly more polished and gentlemanly in register than the French's quick satire.
