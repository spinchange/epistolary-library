## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLI**

**The chief black eunuch to Usbek**

### Translation
Ismaël, one of the black eunuchs, has just died, magnificent lord, and I cannot help replacing him. Since eunuchs are extremely rare at present, I had thought of making use of a black slave of yours who is in the country; but up to now I have not been able to persuade him to suffer himself to be consecrated to that office. Seeing that in the end it is to his advantage, I wished the other day to use a little severity toward him; and, in concert with the steward of your gardens, I ordered that, despite himself, he should be put in a condition to render you the services that flatter your heart the most, and to live, like me, in those dreadful places he does not even dare to look at. But he began to howl as if someone had wanted to flay him, and made such an outcry that he escaped from our hands and avoided the fatal knife. I have just learned that he wants to write to you to ask for mercy, maintaining that I conceived this plan only from an insatiable desire for vengeance over certain cutting jests that he says he made about me. Yet I swear to you by the hundred thousand prophets that I acted only for the good of your service, the only thing dear to me, and beyond which I regard nothing. I prostrate myself at your feet.

*From the seraglio of Fatmé, the 7th day of the moon of Maharram, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the basic situation and the letter's chilling comic surface: a dead eunuch must be replaced, a slave is selected, and bureaucratic brutality is presented as loyal service. His version is effective overall, but it occasionally dignifies or euphemizes expressions whose force in French lies in their stark bluntness.

### Main differences
- **Replacing the eunuch is presented as unavoidable**
  - French: `je ne puis m'empêcher de le remplacer`
  - Fresh translation: `I cannot help replacing him`
  - Davidson: `I tried to avoid any delay in filling up his place`
  - Davidson shifts from inevitability to efficiency; the French is colder and more matter-of-fact.

- **Consecration to office**
  - French: `souffrir qu'on le consacrât à cet emploi`
  - Fresh translation: `suffer himself to be consecrated to that office`
  - Davidson: `undergo the sacrifice necessary for his consecration to that office`
  - Davidson makes the implication explicit, but the French's dry circumlocution is part of the letter's cruelty.

- **`Ces redoutables lieux`**
  - French: `ces redoutables lieux`
  - Fresh translation: `those dreadful places`
  - Davidson: `those sacred quarters`
  - `Sacred` significantly softens and sacralizes what in French is menacing.

- **The slave's resistance**
  - French: `il se mit à hurler comme si on avoit voulu l'écorcher`
  - Fresh translation: `he began to howl as if someone had wanted to flay him`
  - Davidson: `he fell a-roaring, as if we had wanted to flay him`
  - Davidson is close and vivid here.

- **The motive imputed to the eunuch**
  - French: `un désir insatiable de vengeance`
  - Fresh translation: `an insatiable desire for vengeance`
  - Davidson: `a relentless desire to be avenged`
  - Davidson is serviceable, but the French noun phrase is slightly harder and more direct.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: bureaucratic, self-justifying, and grotesquely calm about violence.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the letter's administrative coldness and euphemistic brutality.
- **Davidson**: readable and often strong, but sometimes more ceremonious than the original.

## Working conclusion
Letter XLI is a good example of Davidson's general strength and limitation: he preserves the scene, but a fresh translation better retains the terrifying flatness with which the original treats mutilation as household management.
