### Translation
Five or six months ago I was in a coffeehouse. I noticed there a gentleman, fairly well dressed, who had people listening to him. He was talking about the pleasure of living in Paris, and lamented his condition, being obliged to live in the provinces. 'I have,' he said, 'fifteen thousand livres a year in landed income, and I would think myself happier if I had a quarter of that property in cash and effects portable everywhere. In vain I press my farmers and overwhelm them with legal costs: I only make them more insolvent. I have never managed to see a hundred pistoles at once. If I owed ten thousand francs, all my lands would be seized, and I would end up in the hospital.'

I went out without paying much attention to all that talk. But yesterday, finding myself again in that neighborhood, I entered the same house and saw there a grave man, pale and long-faced, who, in the middle of five or six talkers, seemed gloomy and thoughtful, until, suddenly taking the floor: 'Yes, gentlemen,' he said, raising his voice, 'I am ruined; I no longer have the means to live, for at this moment I have at home two hundred thousand livres in banknotes and a hundred thousand crowns in silver. I find myself in a dreadful condition; I thought myself rich, and here I am in the hospital. At least if I had only some little patch of land to which I could retire, I would be sure of having enough to live on; but I do not own as much landed property as would fill this hat.'

By chance I turned my head another way, and saw another man making the grimaces of one possessed. 'Who can be trusted now?' he cried. 'There is a traitor whom I thought such a friend of mine that I had lent him my money - and he paid it back to me! What horrible perfidy! Do what he may, in my mind he will always be dishonored.'

Close by there was a very badly dressed man who, lifting his eyes to heaven, said: 'God bless the projects of our ministers! May I live to see shares at two thousand, and all the lackeys of Paris richer than their masters!' I was curious to ask his name. 'He is an extremely poor man,' I was told; 'which is not surprising, for he has a poor trade: he is a genealogist, and he hopes his craft will pay, if fortunes go on as they are; and that all these new rich people will need him to reform their names, scrub clean their ancestors, and adorn their coaches. He imagines he will make as many people of quality as he likes; he trembles with joy at seeing his clientele multiply.'

At last I saw come in a pale, dry old man, whom I recognized as a newsmonger before he had even sat down. He was not one of those who have victorious assurance against every reverse, and who always predict victories and trophies; on the contrary, he was one of those tremblers who never have anything but sad news. 'Affairs are going very badly on the side of Spain,' he said. 'We have no cavalry on the frontier, and there is reason to fear that Prince Pio, who has a large force, will make all Languedoc pay contributions.' Opposite me sat a philosopher, rather poorly turned out, who felt pity for the newsmonger and shrugged his shoulders in proportion as the other raised his voice. I moved closer to him, and he said in my ear: 'You see that this fool has been entertaining us for an hour with his fear for Languedoc; and I, yesterday evening, noticed a spot in the sun which, if it grew larger, could cast all nature into torpor - and I have not said a single word.'

*Paris, the 17th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the sequence of coffeehouse absurdities well: the impoverished landowner envies liquid wealth, the paper-rich man envies land, the lender resents repayment, the genealogist prays for nouveaux riches, and the philosopher quietly dwarfs the panic of political gossip with a cosmic danger.

### Main differences
- **`effets portables partout`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the preference for movable wealth that can be carried anywhere.
  - Davidson's `portable effects` is close, though slightly stiffer in English.

- **`il me l'a rendu!`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the comic shock of `and he paid it back to me!`
  - Davidson catches the joke very well here.

- **`décrasser leurs ancêtres`**
  - Fresh translation: `scrub clean their ancestors`
  - Davidson's `polish up their ancestors` is effective, but a little gentler than the French's dirty, physical image.

- **`engourdissement`**
  - Fresh translation uses `torpor`.
  - Davidson's `a state of stagnation` is understandable, but less vivid and less bodily.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, satirical, and economically precise.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the coffeehouse succession of absurd self-interests and ends on the philosopher's dry cosmic counterpoint.
- **Davidson**: strong overall, with only occasional smoothing.
