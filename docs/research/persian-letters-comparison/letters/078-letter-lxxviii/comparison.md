**Rica to Usbek, at ***.**

### Translation
I am sending you the copy of a letter that a Frenchman now in Spain wrote here; I think you will be glad to see it.

I have been traveling through Spain and Portugal for six months, and I live among peoples who, despising all the others, do the French alone the honor of hating them.

Gravity is the striking characteristic of the two nations: it chiefly shows itself in two ways, by spectacles and by the mustache.

Spectacles demonstrate beyond question that the man who wears them is thoroughly accomplished in the sciences and buried in deep reading, to such a point that his sight has grown weak from it; and any nose adorned or burdened with them can, without contradiction, pass for the nose of a scholar.

As for the mustache, it is respectable in itself and apart from its consequences; though people still often derive great advantages from it for the prince's service and the honor of the nation, as a famous Portuguese general in the Indies showed very well: finding himself in need of money, he cut off one of his mustaches and sent to ask the inhabitants of Goa for twenty thousand pistoles on that pledge; they lent them to him at once, and afterward he redeemed his mustache with honor.

One easily understands that peoples as grave and phlegmatic as these may be vain; indeed, they are. They usually base that vanity on two very considerable things. Those who live on the mainland of Spain and Portugal feel their hearts extremely uplifted when they are what they call old Christians; that is to say, when they do not descend from those whom the Inquisition persuaded in recent centuries to embrace the Christian religion. Those who are in the Indies are no less flattered when they reflect that they have the sublime merit of being, as they say, men of white flesh. There has never been in the Grand Seigneur's seraglio a sultana so proud of her beauty as the oldest and ugliest cur is of the olive whiteness of his complexion when, in a city of Mexico, he sits in his doorway with folded arms. A man of such consequence, a creature so perfect, would not work for all the treasures of the world, and would never resolve, through some vile mechanical trade, to compromise the honor and dignity of his skin.

For you must know that when a man has a certain merit in Spain - for example, when, to the qualities I have just mentioned, he can add that of owning a great sword, or of having learned from his father the art of making a discordant guitar swear - he no longer works: his honor is invested in the repose of his limbs. The man who remains seated ten hours a day obtains exactly half as much again in consideration as one who remains seated only five, because it is on chairs that nobility is acquired.

But although these invincible enemies of work make a display of philosophical tranquility, they do not truly have it in their hearts; for they are always in love. They are the foremost men in the world for dying of languor beneath their mistresses' windows; and any Spaniard who does not have a cold cannot pass for gallant.

They are first devout and second jealous. They are careful not to expose their wives to the enterprises of a soldier riddled with wounds, or of a decrepit magistrate; but they shut them up with a fervent novice who lowers his eyes, or a robust Franciscan who raises them.

They know better than others the weak point of women; they do not want anyone to see their heels, or to catch them by the tips of their feet: they know that imagination is always moving on, and nothing distracts it on the way; it arrives, and there one was sometimes warned in advance.

People say everywhere that love's severities are cruel; they are even more cruel for Spaniards: women cure them of their pains, but only by making them exchange one pain for another, and they are always left with a long and disagreeable memory of an extinguished passion.

They have little courtesies that in France would seem badly placed: for example, a captain never beats his soldier without asking his permission; and the Inquisition never burns a Jew without offering him its excuses.

The Spaniards who are not burned seem so attached to the Inquisition that it would be bad temper to take it away from them: I would only like another one established; not against heretics, but against heresiarchs who attribute to petty monastic practices the same efficacy as to the seven sacraments; who adore everything they venerate; and who are so devout that they are scarcely Christian.

You may find wit and good sense among Spaniards; but do not look for either in their books: look at one of their libraries, with the novels on one side and the scholastics on the other, and you would say the parts had been made - and the whole assembled - by some secret enemy of human reason.

The only one of their books that is good is the one that showed the absurdity of all the others.

They have made immense discoveries in the New World, and they still do not know their own continent: on their rivers there is some port that has not yet been discovered, and in their mountains there are nations unknown to them.

They say that the sun rises and sets in their country; but it must also be said that in making its course it encounters only ruined fields and deserted regions.

I would not mind, Usbek, seeing a letter written at Madrid by a Spaniard traveling in France; I think he would avenge his nation very well. What a vast field for a phlegmatic and thoughtful man! I imagine he would begin the description of Paris in this way.

There is here a house where madmen are put; at first one would think it the largest in the city; no: the remedy is much too small for the disease. No doubt the French, greatly discredited among their neighbors, shut a few madmen into one house in order to persuade people that those outside are not mad.

I leave my Spaniard there. Farewell, my dear Usbek.

*Paris, the 17th day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the broad satirical thrust of the letter very effectively. His version is lively, often funny, and it preserves Rica's sequence of observations on Spanish gravity, vanity, idleness, jealousy, piety, books, and national decline. At the same time, several local choices either smooth away the oddness of the French or introduce notable distortions.

### Main differences
- **Opening national characterization**
  - French: the Spaniards and Portuguese do the French `l'honneur de les haïr`.
  - Fresh translation keeps the deliberately paradoxical `honor of hating them`.
  - Davidson is close here and captures the wit well.

- **`hommes de chair blanche`**
  - Fresh translation: `men of white flesh`
  - Davidson: `white-skinned men`
  - Davidson normalizes a deliberately bodily and faintly absurd phrase.

- **The guitar joke**
  - French: `l'art de faire jurer une discordante guitare`
  - Fresh translation: `the art of making a discordant guitar swear`
  - Davidson: `to strum a jangling guitar`
  - Davidson loses the comic violence and personification in `faire jurer`.

- **Women's heels and the imagination**
  - French: `ils ne veulent pas qu'on leur voie le talon`
  - Fresh translation keeps the heel / feet imagery.
  - Davidson rewrites this as `They allow their wives to appear with uncovered bosoms; but they would not have anyone see their heels`, introducing material not present in the French and redirecting the joke.

- **Discoveries at home**
  - French: `il y a sur leurs rivières tel port qui n'a pas encore été découvert`
  - Fresh translation: `some port ... has not yet been discovered`
  - Davidson: `an undiscovered bridge or two`
  - This is a genuine lexical error, not just stylistic variation.

- **The good Spanish book**
  - French: `Le seul de leurs livres qui soit bon est celui qui a fait voir le ridicule de tous les autres`
  - Davidson: `Their only good book is one which was written to expose the absurdity of all the others`
  - Davidson gets the point, though the fresh version keeps the more economical and allusive phrasing of the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: extended satirical performance - dry, outrageous, and full of compact comic images.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the odd metaphors and the accumulative rhythm without flattening them into essay prose.
- **Davidson**: spirited and readable, but more willing to normalize weird phrases or replace them with smoother equivalents.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXVIII confirms that Davidson can reproduce the sweep of Montesquieu's satire very well, but the line-by-line comparison matters because some of the sharpest jokes depend on odd literal details - heels, white flesh, a swearing guitar, an undiscovered port - that should not be normalized away.
