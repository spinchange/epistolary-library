## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
The ordinary effect of colonies is to weaken the countries from which they are drawn, without populating those to which they are sent.

Men must remain where they are. Some illnesses come from exchanging a good air for a bad one; others come precisely from the fact of changing it.

The air, like plants, is charged with the particles of the soil of each country. It acts upon us so strongly that our temperament is fixed by it. When we are transported into another country, we become ill. Our fluids, accustomed to a certain consistency, our solids to a certain arrangement, and both to a certain degree of motion, can no longer endure others and resist a new pattern.

When a country is deserted, that is a presumption of some particular defect in the nature of its climate. Thus, when men are taken from a fortunate sky and sent into such a country, one does precisely the opposite of what one intends.

The Romans knew this from experience. They relegated all criminals to Sardinia, and sent Jews there as well. It was easy enough to console themselves for that loss; the contempt they had for those wretches made it very easy.

The great Shah Abbas, wishing to deprive the Turks of the means of maintaining large armies on the frontiers, transported almost all the Armenians out of their own country, and sent more than twenty thousand families into the province of Guilan, where almost all of them perished in a very short time.

None of the transfers of peoples made to Constantinople has ever succeeded.

That prodigious number of Blacks of which we have spoken did not fill America.

Since the destruction of the Jews under Hadrian, Palestine has been without inhabitants.

It must therefore be admitted that great destructions are almost irreparable, because a people that falls below a certain point remains in the same condition; and if by chance it recovers, that takes centuries.

If, in a state of weakness, the least of the circumstances we have spoken of comes to join in, not only does it fail to recover, it declines every day and tends toward annihilation.

The expulsion of the Moors from Spain is still felt as on the first day. Far from that void being filled, it grows greater every day.

Since the devastation of America, the Spaniards, who took the place of its former inhabitants, have not been able to repeople it. On the contrary, by a fatality that I would do better to call a divine justice, the destroyers destroy themselves and consume themselves every day.

Princes ought not, then, to think of populating great countries by colonies. I do not say they never succeed; there are climates so happy that the species always multiplies there: witness those islands that were peopled by sick people abandoned there by some ships, and who immediately recovered their health there.

But even if such colonies did succeed, instead of increasing power they would only divide it, unless they were very limited in extent, like those sent to occupy some trading post.

The Carthaginians had, like the Spaniards, discovered America, or at least great islands in which they carried on an immense trade. But when they saw the number of their inhabitants decreasing, that wise republic forbade its subjects that trade and that navigation.

I dare say it: instead of sending Spaniards to the Indies, it would be necessary to send the Indians and the métifs back to Spain. It would be necessary to restore to that monarchy all its scattered peoples; and if only half of those great colonies were preserved, Spain would become the most formidable power in Europe.

Empires may be compared to a tree whose branches, when they spread too far, draw all the sap from the trunk and serve only to cast shade.

Nothing ought to cure princes of the fury for distant conquests better than the example of the Portuguese and the Spaniards.

Those two nations, having conquered immense kingdoms with inconceivable rapidity, more astonished at their victories than the conquered peoples were at their defeat, considered the means of preserving them and each took a different path for that purpose.

The Spaniards, despairing of keeping the conquered nations faithful, resolved to exterminate them and to send faithful peoples there from Spain. Never was a horrible design executed with greater exactness. One saw a people, as numerous as all the peoples of Europe together, disappear from the earth at the arrival of those barbarians, who, in discovering the Indies, seemed at the same time to have wished to show mankind what the furthest limit of cruelty was.

By that barbarity they kept the country under their domination. Judge from that how fatal conquests are, since such are their effects; for in the end that frightful remedy was the only one. How could they have kept so many millions of men in obedience? How could they have sustained a civil war from such a distance? What would have become of them if they had given those peoples time to recover from their astonishment at the arrival of those new gods and from the fear of their thunderbolts?

As for the Portuguese, they took an entirely opposite road; they did not employ cruelties, and so they were soon driven from all the countries they had discovered. The Dutch favored the rebellion of those peoples and profited from it.

What prince would envy the fate of those conquerors? Who would want conquests at such a price? Some were driven out of them at once; the others turned them into deserts, and made their own country the same.

It is the destiny of heroes to ruin themselves by conquering countries they immediately lose, or by subjecting nations they are themselves obliged to destroy, like that madman who ruined himself buying statues only to throw them into the sea, and mirrors only to shatter them at once.

*Paris, the 18th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the broad architecture of this long letter: climatic unsuitability, failed transfers of population, colonial depopulation, the Spanish and Portuguese examples, and the closing analogy of self-ruining conquest. His version is largely faithful, but the English clean text is heavily disturbed by footnotes and page debris, and a few lexical choices blunt the French.

### Main differences
- **Colonies do not `people` their destinations**
  - Fresh translation keeps the hard antithesis: colonies weaken one place without populating the other.
  - Davidson's `without peopling those which are colonized` is close, though slightly more abstract.

- **Climate as `fortunate sky`**
  - French: `un ciel heureux`
  - Fresh translation: `a fortunate sky`
  - Davidson: `an agreeable clime`
  - Davidson smooths the image into normal English, but the French has a more striking idiom.

- **`métifs`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the historical term `métifs` rather than fully normalizing it.
  - Davidson renders it as `cross-breeds`, which is intelligible but much harsher in modern English and less exact to the French spelling preserved here.

- **Limit of cruelty**
  - French: `quel étoit le dernier période de la cruauté`
  - Fresh translation: `what the furthest limit of cruelty was`
  - Davidson: `the utmost reach of cruelty`
  - Davidson is strong here; the difference is mainly tonal.

- **Thunderbolts**
  - Fresh translation preserves the image of `their thunderbolts`.
  - Davidson does as well, and this is one of the places where he remains very close to the French's mythic rhetoric.

- **Mirrors vs glasses**
  - French: `des glaces`
  - Fresh translation: `mirrors`
  - Davidson: `glasses`
  - `Mirrors` better captures the intended object in context.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: sweeping, violent, and rhetorically amplified; it mixes demographic theory with moral denunciation and imperial example.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the breadth and violence of the argument while staying readable.
- **Davidson**: often quite good sentence by sentence, but the surviving text is compromised by cleanup issues and occasional Victorian smoothing.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is heavily contaminated by note markers and debris, including `1` intrusions, an inserted page number `101`, and broken note material in multiple paragraphs.
- Because of that, this letter is one of the strongest candidates in the set for later manual correction against the scan or a better text witness.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXII is now fully drafted, but it remains one of the messiest English witnesses in the run. Davidson's translation is broadly useful, yet any close editorial use of it should be checked against the underlying source because OCR noise and footnote carryover repeatedly interrupt the text.
