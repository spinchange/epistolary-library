## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCV**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
I have never heard public law discussed without people carefully beginning by investigating the origin of societies; that seems ridiculous to me. If men did not form societies, if they left and fled one another, then one would have to ask why they remain apart. But they are born linked to one another: a son is born beside his father, and remains there. That is society, and the cause of society.

Public law is better known in Europe than in Asia. Yet one may say that the passions of princes, the patience of peoples, and the flattery of writers have corrupted all its principles.

That law, as it exists today, is a science that teaches princes how far they may violate justice without harming their own interests. What a project, Rhedi - to harden their consciences by reducing iniquity to a system, giving it rules, forming principles for it, and drawing consequences from it.

The unlimited power of our sublime sultans, which has no rule but itself, produces no more monsters than this shameful art that wishes to make inflexible justice bend.

One would say, Rhedi, that there are two entirely different kinds of justice: one that regulates the affairs of private persons and reigns in civil law; the other that regulates disputes arising from people to people and tyrannizes in public law - as though public law were not itself a civil law, not of one particular country, to be sure, but of the world.

I will explain my thoughts on that in another letter.

*Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1716.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the argumentative structure well. He keeps Usbek's impatience with abstract origin stories and his central complaint that modern public law systematizes injustice in the service of princely interest.

### Main differences
- **Opening ridicule**
  - French: `rechercher soigneusement quelle est l'origine des sociétés`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sense of a fussy, habitual beginning that Usbek finds absurd.
  - Davidson's `a preliminary careful inquiry` is accurate, but a little smoother and less impatient.

- **Public law vs international law**
  - French consistently says `droit public`.
  - Fresh translation keeps `public law` throughout.
  - Davidson repeatedly modernizes this to `international law`, which explains the concept but blunts Montesquieu's paradox that public law is itself a kind of civil law of the world.

- **Hardening conscience**
  - French: `endurcir leur conscience`
  - Fresh translation: `harden their consciences`
  - Davidson: `harden the conscience`
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh draft keeps the plural force implied by the surrounding reference to princes and their theorists.

- **Bending justice**
  - French: `cet art indigne qui veut faire plier la justice`
  - Fresh translation keeps the image of justice being made to bend.
  - Davidson's `unworthy art` is strong, but his phrasing is slightly more literary and less blunt.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: polemical, compressed, and conceptually sharp.
- **Fresh translation**: stays close to the juridical vocabulary.
- **Davidson**: clear and effective, though a bit more modernizing in terminology.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text is reasonably clean here.
- The main caution is editorial rather than OCR-related: Davidson's use of `international law` subtly reframes the terminology.

## Working conclusion
Davidson is substantively reliable, but this letter shows why a fresh translation matters for conceptual vocabulary. `Droit public` and `justice` should not be modernized so far that Montesquieu's paradoxes lose force.
