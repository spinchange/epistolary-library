## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XX**

**Usbek to Zachi, his wife, at the seraglio in Isfahan**

### Translation
You have offended me, Zachi, and I feel movements in my heart that you ought to fear, if my distance did not leave you time to change your conduct and calm the violent jealousy by which I am tormented.

I learn that you were found alone with Nadir, a white eunuch, who will pay for his infidelity and perfidy with his head. How could you forget yourself so far as not to feel that you are not permitted to receive a white eunuch in your chamber while black ones are assigned to serve you? You may tell me that eunuchs are not men, and that your virtue places you above thoughts that an imperfect likeness might awaken in you. That is not enough either for you or for me: not for you, because you have done something the laws of the seraglio forbid; not for me, because you take away my honor by exposing yourself to looks — what do I say? perhaps to the attempts of a traitor who may have sullied you by his crimes, and still more by his regrets and the despair of his impotence.

You may perhaps tell me that you have always been faithful to me. Ah! could you help being so? How could you have deceived the vigilance of the black eunuchs, who are so astonished at the life you lead? How could you have broken those bolts and doors that keep you enclosed? You boast of a virtue that is not free, and perhaps your impure desires have a thousand times taken away the merit and worth of that fidelity of which you boast so much.

I am willing to suppose that you have not done all I have reason to suspect; that the traitor did not lay sacrilegious hands upon you; that you refused to lavish before his eyes the delights of his master; that, covered by your clothes, you left at least that weak barrier between him and yourself; that he, struck by holy respect, lowered his eyes; that, his boldness failing him, he trembled at the punishments he was preparing for himself. Even if all that were true, it would still remain true that you have done something contrary to your duty. And if you violated it gratuitously, without satisfying your disordered inclinations, what would you have done to satisfy them? What would you do now if you could leave that sacred place which is for you a harsh prison, just as for your companions it is a favorable refuge against the assaults of vice, a sacred temple where your sex loses its weakness and becomes invincible despite all the disadvantages of nature? What would you do if, left to yourself, you had for your defense only your love for me, so gravely offended, and your duty, so indignantly betrayed? How holy are the customs of the country where you live, which tear you away from the attempts of the vilest slaves! You ought to thank me for the constraint in which I make you live, since only by it do you still deserve to live.

You cannot bear the chief of the eunuchs because he always keeps his eyes on your conduct and gives you his wise counsels. His ugliness, you say, is so great that you cannot look at him without pain, as though in such offices one put more beautiful objects there. What afflicts you is not having, in his place, the white eunuch who dishonors you.

But what has your first slave done to you? She told you that the familiarities you were taking with young Zelide were against propriety. There is the reason for your hatred.

I ought, Zachi, to be a severe judge; I am only a husband seeking to find you innocent. The love I have for Roxane, my new wife, has left me all the tenderness I ought to have for you, who are no less beautiful. I divide my love between you both; and Roxane has no advantage except that which virtue can add to beauty.

*At Smyrna, the 12th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the jealousy, coercion, and self-justifying rhetoric of Usbek's reprimand. He stays close in sequence and incident, but he often polishes the phrasing and occasionally alters details that matter for Usbek's psychology.

### Main differences
- **Impotence and regret**
  - French: `plus encore par ses regrets et le désespoir de son impuissance`
  - Fresh translation: `still more by his regrets and the despair of his impotence`
  - Davidson: `still more by his repining and his impotent despair`
  - Davidson is close, though slightly more euphemistic and less syntactically sharp.

- **Virtue that is not free**
  - French: `une vertu qui n'est pas libre`
  - Fresh translation keeps the key paradox intact.
  - Davidson also keeps it, but within a generally smoother rhetorical flow.

- **Harsh prison vs melancholy prison**
  - French: `une dure prison`
  - Fresh translation: `a harsh prison`
  - Davidson: `a melancholy prison`
  - Davidson softens the material hardness of the phrase into a mood word.

- **Holy manners and gratitude for constraint**
  - French: `Que les moeurs du pays où vous vivez sont saintes`
  - Fresh translation: `How holy are the customs of the country where you live`
  - Davidson: `How immaculate are the manners of the country in which you live`
  - Davidson heightens the diction, but the French is colder and more socially concrete.

- **Your first slave is female in the French**
  - French: `votre première esclave`
  - Fresh translation: `your first slave` / `She told you`
  - Davidson: `your chief slave ... He has told you`
  - Davidson changes both the rank and the gender.

- **Two wives, not all wives**
  - French: `Je partage mon amour entre vous deux`
  - Fresh translation: `I divide my love between you both`
  - Davidson: `I share my love among you all`
  - This is a substantive shift from the explicit pair Zachi/Roxane to the whole harem.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: accusatory, possessive, legalistic, and revealingly self-contradictory.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps both the violence of the argument and the coercive logic that presents confinement as benevolence.
- **Davidson**: broadly faithful, but more euphonious and intermittently less exact.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text is comparatively readable and stable.
- Minor OCR noise persists in the title line, in `dgliness`, and in the date spacing (`1 1711`).
- No major sentence-level corruption obstructs interpretation.

## Working conclusion
Letter XX is central for Usbek's authoritarian psychology. Davidson transmits the broad force of the letter, but the French remains more revealing where it insists on constraint, humiliation, and the explicitly limited division of love between Zachi and Roxane.
