## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CLV**

**Usbek to Nessir, at Ispahan**

### Translation
Happy is the man who, knowing the full worth of a gentle and tranquil life, rests his heart in the midst of his family and knows no land but the one that gave him birth.

I live in a barbarous climate, present to everything that troubles me and absent from everything that concerns me. A dark sadness seizes me; I sink into dreadful dejection; it seems to me that I am being annihilated; and I find myself again only when a gloomy jealousy comes to kindle itself and gives birth in my soul to fear, suspicions, hatred, and regrets.

You know me, Nessir; you have always seen into my heart as into your own. You would pity me if you knew the deplorable state I am in. At times I wait a full six months for news from the seraglio; I count every instant that passes; my impatience always lengthens them; and when the one so long awaited is near to arriving, a sudden revolution takes place in my heart; my hand trembles to open a fatal letter; and that anxiety which was driving me to despair I find to be the happiest state in which I can be, and I fear to be torn out of it by a blow more cruel to me than a thousand deaths.

But whatever reason I had for leaving my country, although I owe my life to my withdrawal, I can no longer, Nessir, remain in this dreadful exile. And would I not die all the same, prey to my sorrows? A thousand times I have urged Rica to leave this foreign land; but he opposes all my resolutions; he keeps me here with a thousand pretexts; it seems that he has forgotten his country; or rather it seems that he has forgotten me myself, so insensitive is he to my troubles.

Wretch that I am! I long to see my country again, perhaps only to become even more unhappy there. And what shall I do there? I am going to carry my head back to my enemies. Nor is that all: I shall enter the seraglio; I shall have to demand an account of the fatal time of my absence; and if I find guilty women there, what will become of me? And if the mere idea crushes me from so far away, what will it be when my presence makes it more vivid? What will it be if I must see, if I must hear, what I do not dare imagine without trembling? What will it be, finally, if punishments that I myself pronounce must become eternal marks of my confusion and my despair?

I shall go shut myself up within walls more terrible for me than for the women kept there; I shall carry all my suspicions there with me; their attentions will hide nothing from me; in my bed, in their arms, I shall possess nothing but my anxieties; at a time so little suited to reflection, my jealousy will still find matter for it. Unworthy refuse of human nature, vile slaves whose hearts have been closed forever to every feeling of love, you would no longer groan over your condition if you knew the misery of mine.

*From Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Chahban, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the movement and argument of the letter closely. He captures Usbek's homesickness, the self-consuming cycle of jealous anticipation, and the dread of returning to the seraglio only to become judge, witness, and victim of its crimes.

### Main differences
- **Resting the heart in the family**
  - French: `repose son coeur au milieu de sa famille`
  - Fresh translation: `rests his heart in the midst of his family`
  - Davidson: `makes his own family the centre of his thought`
  - Davidson gives the sense, but the French is more affective and intimate than the more intellectual `centre of his thought`.

- **Withdrawal versus flight**
  - French: `je dois ma vie à ma retraite`
  - Fresh translation: `I owe my life to my withdrawal`
  - Davidson: `I owe my life to my flight`
  - `Flight` heightens the drama. The French `retraite` suggests retreat or withdrawal rather than a panicked escape.

- **Rica's indifference**
  - French: `il semble qu'il m'ait oublié moi-même`
  - Fresh translation: `it seems that he has forgotten me myself`
  - Davidson: `he seems to have forgotten me`
  - Davidson is readable, but the French's emphatic `moi-même` sharpens the sense of personal abandonment.

- **Returning to enemies**
  - French: `Je vais rapporter ma tête à mes ennemis`
  - Fresh translation: `I am going to carry my head back to my enemies`
  - Davidson: `I shall but hand my head to my enemies`
  - Davidson turns the phrase into smoother English, while the French keeps a more literal image of return and exposure.

- **What the wives' attentions can and cannot do**
  - French: `leurs empressements ne m'en déroberont rien`
  - Fresh translation: `their attentions will hide nothing from me`
  - Davidson: `the eagerness of my wives will not remove them in the least`
  - Davidson shifts from concealment to removal. The French means that their efforts will not keep his suspicions from his sight.

- **Final insult to the eunuchs**
  - French: `Rebut indigne de la nature humaine`
  - Fresh translation: `Unworthy refuse of human nature`
  - Davidson: `Worthless scum of human kind`
  - Davidson is forceful, but also more idiomatic and somewhat more coarse than the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: claustrophobic, self-lacerating, and recursive; Usbek imagines future jealousy so vividly that exile and return both become intolerable.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the accumulating syntax and the repeated self-questioning that drive the letter forward.
- **Davidson**: strong on narrative coherence, but he often converts the French's more peculiar or bodily phrasing into smoother Victorian idiom.

## Working conclusion
Letter CLV shows Davidson at his usual strength: he is faithful to the emotional structure and highly readable. The main differences are not omissions, but shifts toward smoother idiomatic English:
- more abstract phrasing in the opening sentence
- a more dramatic `flight` for `retraite`
- a changed nuance in the sentence about the wives' attentions
- a more aggressively idiomatic final insult
