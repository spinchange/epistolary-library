## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter VII**

**Fatmé to Usbek, at Erzurum**

### Translation
It is two months since you left, my dear Usbek, and in my dejection I still cannot persuade myself of it. I run through the whole seraglio as if you were there; I am not undeceived. What is to become of a woman who loves you, who was used to holding you in her arms, who was occupied only with giving you proofs of her tenderness — free by the advantage of her birth, a slave through the violence of her love?

When I married you, my eyes had not yet seen the face of a man: you are the only one I have been allowed to see. I do not count those hideous eunuchs among men; their least defect is that they are not men. When I compare the beauty of your face with their deformity, I cannot help thinking myself happy. My imagination offers me no idea more ravishing than the enchanting charms of your person. I swear to you, Usbek: if I were permitted to leave this place where the necessity of my condition shuts me up; if I could escape the guard that surrounds me; if I could choose among all the men who live in this capital of nations, I would choose only you. There can be only you in the world who deserve to be loved.

Do not think your absence has made me neglect a beauty dear to you. Though I am seen by no one, and though the adornments with which I deck myself are useless to your happiness, I still try to keep up the habit of pleasing. I never lie down without perfuming myself with the most delicious essences. I recall that happy time when you came into my arms; a flattering dream that deceives me shows me the dear object of my love; my imagination loses itself in its desires as it flatters itself with hope. Sometimes I think that, wearied by a painful journey, you are coming back to us. The night passes in dreams that belong neither to waking nor to sleep. I search for you at my side, and it seems to me you flee me. At last the very fire that devours me dispels these enchantments and brings me back to myself. Then I find myself so inflamed ... You would not believe it, Usbek: it is impossible to live in this state. Fire runs through my veins. Why can I not express what I feel so well? And how is it that I feel so well what I cannot express? In those moments, Usbek, I would give the empire of the world for a single one of your kisses. How unhappy a woman is, with such violent desires, when she is deprived of the only one who can satisfy them; when, abandoned to herself and with nothing to distract her, she must live in the habit of sighing and in the fury of an irritated passion; when, far from being happy, she does not even have the advantage of contributing to another's happiness — a useless ornament of a seraglio, kept for her husband's honor and not for his happiness!

You men are very cruel! You are delighted that we should have desires we cannot satisfy. You treat us as if we were without feeling, and you would be very sorry if we were. You think that our desires, so long mortified, will be inflamed at your sight. It takes effort to make oneself loved; it is quicker to get from our temperament what you do not dare hope for from your merit.

Farewell, my dear Usbek, farewell. Know that I live only to adore you. My soul is full of you, and your absence, far from making me forget you, would only animate my love, if it could become more violent.

*From the seraglio of Isfahan, the 12th day of the first moon of Rebiab, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson transmits the main emotional line very well: longing, erotic frustration, complaint, and final devotion. He is often close, but he sometimes sentimentalizes or slightly moralizes the letter.

### Main differences
- **Birth and love slavery**
  - French: `libre par l'avantage de sa naissance, esclave par la violence de son amour`
  - Fresh translation preserves the opposition between social freedom and emotional enslavement.
  - Davidson's `born to the blessings of freedom` is elegant, but a little more generalized.

- **Capital of nations**
  - French: `cette capitale des nations`
  - Fresh translation: `this capital of nations`
  - Davidson keeps the phrase, but elsewhere smooths similar Persian hyperbole into polite English.

- **Temperament vs weakness**
  - French: `obtenir de notre tempérament`
  - Fresh translation: `get from our temperament`
  - Davidson: `obtain through our constitutional weakness`
  - That introduces a more clinical and weaker formulation than the French's sharper complaint.

- **Useful / useless ornament**
  - French: `ornement inutile d'un sérail`
  - Fresh translation: `a useless ornament of a seraglio`
  - Davidson is close, but the surrounding sentence is more polished and less jagged than the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: sensuous, feverish, and accusatory, with sudden swings between tenderness and complaint.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the heat and the rhetorical restlessness of Fatmé's voice.
- **Davidson**: strong overall, though a shade more romantic and decorous.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text contains a visible break and ellipsis-like corruption around `Then my agitation knows no bounds`.
- The underlying sense is recoverable, but the raw text should still be consulted if this passage is quoted closely.

## Working conclusion
Letter VII shows Davidson handling long emotional prose competently, but the French remains more abrupt, bodily, and argumentative than his smoother version suggests.
