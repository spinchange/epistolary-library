## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXII**

**Usbek to [***]**

### Translation
The reign of the late king was so long that by its end people had forgotten its beginning. Nowadays it is fashionable to concern oneself only with events that happened during his minority; nothing is read anymore but memoirs of that period.

Here is the speech one of the generals of the city of Paris delivered in a council of war; and I confess that I do not understand much of it.

`Gentlemen, although our troops have been driven back with loss, I believe it will be easy for us to repair this setback. I have six verses of a song ready to publish, which I am sure will restore everything to equilibrium. I have chosen some very clear voices which, issuing from the cavity of certain very strong chests, will stir the people wonderfully. They are set to a tune that has so far produced a quite particular effect.

`If that is not enough, we will bring out a print showing Mazarin hanged.

`Fortunately for us, he does not speak French well; and he mangles it so badly that his affairs cannot fail to decline. We never fail to make the people notice the ridiculous tone in which he pronounces it. A few days ago we seized upon a grammatical error so gross that jokes were made of it at every crossroads.

`I hope that before eight days are over, the people will make the name Mazarin into a generic word for all beasts of burden, and those used for draught.

`Since our defeat, our music has vexed him so furiously over original sin that, in order not to see his supporters reduced by half, he has been obliged to dismiss all his pages.

`Take heart again, then; recover your courage, and be sure that we shall drive him back over the mountains with a storm of whistles.`

*From Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the broad joke of the passage: political struggle during the Fronde is represented as if it could be won by songs, prints, mockery of Mazarin's accent, and public ridicule rather than by military force.

### Main differences
- **Usbek's framing sentence**
  - French: `j'avoue que je n'y comprends pas grand'chose`
  - Fresh translation keeps the literal sense: `I do not understand much of it.`
  - Davidson changes this into `I do not see anything very remarkable in it`, which weakens the comic bewilderment.

- **Song couplets**
  - French: `six couplets de chanson`
  - Fresh translation keeps the concreteness of six verses of a song.
  - Davidson is close here.

- **Mangled French**
  - French: `il l'écorche tellement`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stronger sense that Mazarin `mangles` the language.
  - Davidson is readable, but slightly milder.

- **Crossroads and public mockery**
  - Fresh translation preserves the image of jokes repeated `par tous les carrefours`.
  - Davidson's `all the streets` is broadly correct but less pointed.

- **Whistles / hisses**
  - French: `à coups de sifflets`
  - Fresh translation keeps the physical force of driving him back with whistles.
  - Davidson's `with our hisses` is effective, though a bit more narrowed.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: burlesque, topical, and pleased with the disproportion between trivial means and grand political claims.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the mock-military rhetoric and Usbek's bemusement intact.
- **Davidson**: serviceable overall, but less exact in the framing irony.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is badly affected by a broken footnote in the middle of the Mazarin paragraph, interrupting the sentence about his pronunciation.
- That note also obscures part of the historical explanation, so the raw text should be consulted for any close editorial work.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXII is less about factual narration than about satirical framing. Davidson preserves the main joke, but the fresh translation better keeps Usbek's baffled tone and the letter's relish in propaganda as a theatrical weapon.
