## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXLIV**

**Usbek to Rica**

### Translation
A few days ago, in a country house where I had gone, I found two learned men who enjoy great celebrity here. Their character seemed admirable to me. The conversation of the first, properly judged, came down to this: “What I have said is true because I have said it.” The conversation of the second concerned something else: “What I have not said is not true because I have not said it.”

I rather liked the first; for when a man is obstinate, that does not matter to me at all; but when he is impertinent, it matters a great deal. The first defends his own opinions; that is his property. The second attacks the opinions of others; and that is the property of everyone.

Oh, my dear Rica, how badly vanity serves those who have a larger dose of it than nature needs for self-preservation! Such people want to be admired by dint of being disagreeable. They seek to be superior; and they do not even manage to be equal.

Modest men, come, let me embrace you. You make life gentle and charming. You think you have nothing, and I tell you that you have everything. You think that you humiliate no one; and you humiliate everyone. And when, in my mind, I compare you with those absolute men whom I see everywhere, I hurl them down from their tribunal and place them at your feet.

*Paris, the 22nd day of the moon of Chahban, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the brief satirical movement of the letter closely. He preserves the contrast between dogmatic self-assertion and censorious impertinence, and he keeps the concluding praise of modest men as a moral counterweight to vanity.

### Main differences
- **Internal addressee correction**
  - French clean text reads `Oh, mon cher Usbek`, which conflicts with the title `USBEK A RICA`.
  - Davidson prints `Oh, my dear Rica` and even notes that `my dear Usbek` in the original is evidently a mistake.
  - The fresh translation follows that correction, since the letter otherwise clearly runs from Usbek to Rica.

- **Admiration vs astonishment**
  - French: `Leur caractère me parut admirable`
  - Fresh translation: `Their character seemed admirable to me`
  - Davidson: `Their characters astonished me`
  - Davidson shifts the line from approval to surprise.

- **The closing verdict on vanity**
  - French: `Ils cherchent à être supérieurs; et ils ne sont pas seulement égaux.`
  - Fresh translation: `They seek to be superior; and they do not even manage to be equal.`
  - Davidson: `They wish to be superior, but they do not even attain to mediocrity.`
  - `Mediocrity` is sharper and more literary, but it is not what the French literally says.

- **Possession and property imagery**
  - French: `c'est son bien` / `c'est le bien de tout le monde`
  - Fresh translation keeps the pointed metaphor of property.
  - Davidson explains it as `his own property` and `the property of the whole world`, which is faithful but slightly more explicit.

- **Religious / proverbial coloring at the end**
  - French: `vous avez tout`
  - Fresh translation: `you have everything`
  - Davidson: `you possess the one thing needful`
  - Davidson adds a more elevated, quasi-biblical tone not present in the French wording.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compact, epigrammatic, and dryly satirical.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the aphoristic sharpness while staying readable in modern English.
- **Davidson**: elegant and lively, but slightly more interpretive in a few key turns.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLIV is a close match overall, but it is useful because it shows two different kinds of intervention:
- an editorial correction to the addressee inside the body of the letter
- slight rhetorical elevation in phrases like `attain to mediocrity` and `the one thing needful`
