## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXXVI**

**Rica to the same**

### Translation
At the next meeting my learned guide led me into a private cabinet. `Here,' he said to me, `are the books of modern history. First look at the historians of the Church and of the popes; books that I read for my edification, and which often produce in me quite the opposite effect.

`There are those who have written about the decline of the formidable Roman Empire, which had been formed out of the wreckage of so many monarchies, and from whose fall so many new ones were formed. An infinite number of barbarous peoples, as unknown as the countries they inhabited, appeared all at once, flooded over it, ravaged it, carved it up, and founded all the kingdoms you now see in Europe. Those peoples were not properly barbarians, since they were free; but they became so afterward, when, subjected for the most part to absolute power, they lost that gentle liberty so consonant with reason, humanity, and nature.

`Here you see the historians of Germany, which is only a shadow of the first empire, but which is, I think, the only power on earth that division has not weakened; the only one too, I still think, that grows stronger in proportion to its losses, and that, slow to profit from success, becomes indomitable through defeat.

`Here are the historians of France, where one sees first the power of kings taking shape, dying twice, being reborn twice in the same way, then languishing for several centuries, but little by little gathering strength, increasing on every side, and rising to its final height: like those rivers that in the course of their run lose their waters, or hide them underground, and then, appearing again, swollen by the streams that join them, sweep away with rapidity everything that resists their passage.

`There you see the Spanish nation coming out of a few mountains; the Mohammedan princes subdued as gradually as they had conquered rapidly; so many kingdoms united in a vast monarchy, which became almost the only one; until, crushed by its false opulence, it lost its strength and even its reputation, and kept nothing but the pride of its former power.

`These are the historians of England, where one sees liberty constantly issuing from the fires of discord and sedition; the prince always tottering on an immovable throne; a nation impatient, wise even in its fury; and, mistress of the sea - a thing unheard of until then - mixing commerce with empire.

`Very near these are the historians of that other queen of the sea, the republic of Holland, so respected in Europe and so formidable in Asia, where its merchants see so many kings prostrate before them.

`The historians of Italy show you a nation once mistress of the world, today the slave of all the others; its princes divided and weak, with no other attribute of sovereignty than an empty policy.

`There are the historians of the republics: of Switzerland, which is the image of its liberty; of Venice, which has no resources except in its economy; and of Genoa, which is proud only because of its buildings.

`Here are those of the north, and among others of Poland, which makes such poor use of its liberty and of the right it has to elect its kings that it seems to want by that means to console the neighboring peoples who have lost both the one and the other.'

At that, we parted until the next day.

*Paris, the 2nd day of the moon of Chalval, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson's rendering is accurate in broad political sense and follows the catalogue-like organization very closely. He keeps the panoramic survey of European powers and reproduces most of the sharp national characterizations without much loss.

### Main differences
- **Freedom and barbarism**
  - French: `Ces peuples n'étoient point proprement barbares, puisqu'ils étoient libres`
  - Fresh translation keeps the paradox intact: they were not really barbarians because they were free.
  - Davidson is close here, though slightly more explanatory in cadence.

- **Germany and division**
  - French: `la division n'a point affoiblie`
  - Fresh translation: `division has not weakened`
  - Davidson uses `faction`, which is more political and more pejorative than the plain `division` of the French.

- **France's rise**
  - French: `monter à son dernier période`
  - Fresh translation: `rising to its final height`
  - Davidson: `it achieves its final stage`
  - Davidson is understandable, but the French image is more visibly one of ascent and culmination.

- **England and empire**
  - French: `mêle le commerce avec l'empire`
  - Fresh translation: `mixing commerce with empire`
  - Davidson: `combines commerce with power`
  - `Empire` is stronger, more historically pointed, and worth preserving.

- **Switzerland**
  - French: `l'image de sa liberté`
  - Fresh translation: `the image of its liberty`
  - Davidson: `the type of liberty`
  - Davidson interprets the metaphor; the French is simpler and more visual.

- **Italy's sovereignty**
  - French: `une vaine politique`
  - Fresh translation: `an empty policy`
  - Davidson: `an ineffectual policy`
  - Davidson makes the judgment more practical; `vaine` also carries vanity and hollowness.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compressed, political, and epigrammatic; each nation is reduced to a memorable historical formula.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the parade of national portraits and the rhetorical balance of the French clauses.
- **Davidson**: faithful in substance, but often a little more explanatory and less aphoristic.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXXVI shows Davidson translating political summary very competently. The main losses are small but characteristic: he sometimes weakens Montesquieu's key abstractions (`division`, `empire`, `image`) by replacing them with more fluent but less exact English.
