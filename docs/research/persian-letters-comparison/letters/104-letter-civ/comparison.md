### Heading
**Letter CIV**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
To continue the line of thought from my last letter, here is roughly what a fairly sensible European was saying to me the other day.

The worst course the princes of Asia could have taken is to hide themselves as they do. They mean to make themselves more respectable; but they make royalty respected, not the king, and attach the minds of subjects to a certain throne, not to a certain person.

This invisible power that governs is always the same for the people. Even if ten kings, known only by name, have cut one another's throats one after another, the people feel no difference: it is as if they had been governed in succession by spirits.

If the detestable parricide of our great king Henri IV had struck one of the kings of India, master of the royal seal and of an immense treasure that would have seemed amassed for him, he would calmly have taken the reins of empire, without a single man thinking of reclaiming his king, his family, or his children.

People are astonished that there is almost never any change in the government of Eastern princes. And where does that come from, if not from the fact that it is tyrannical and dreadful?

Changes can be made only by the prince or by the people. But princes there take care not to make them, because in so high a degree of power they already have all they can have; if they changed anything, it could only be to their own disadvantage.

As for subjects, if one of them forms any resolution, he cannot carry it out upon the state: he would have to counterbalance all at once a formidable power that is always one and the same. Time fails him, as do the means. But he has only to go to the source of that power, and he needs only an arm and an instant.

The murderer mounts the throne while the monarch descends from it, falls, and goes to expire at his feet.

A malcontent in Europe thinks of maintaining some secret intelligence, going over to enemies, seizing some stronghold, stirring up a few vain murmurs among subjects. A malcontent in Asia goes straight to the prince, astonishes, strikes, overturns. He effaces even the very idea of him. In one instant, slave and master; in one instant, usurper and legitimate ruler.

Unhappy the king who has only one head! It seems he gathers all his power upon it only to show the first ambitious man the place where he will find it in its entirety.

*Paris, the 17th day of the moon of Rebiab II, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the letter's central argument clearly in view: Asiatic monarchy becomes impersonal when rulers hide themselves, so the whole state can be seized by striking one visible point.

### Main differences
- **Royalty vs the king**
  - French: `ils font respecter la royauté, et non pas le roi`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sharp opposition between the institution and the person.
  - Davidson is close, but my draft keeps the contrast slightly tighter.

- **Parricide / India / empire**
  - French: `le détestable parricide ... un roi des Indes`
  - Fresh translation keeps both the loaded word `parricide` and the broader reference to `India`.
  - Davidson's `detestable murderer` is serviceable, but less pointed than the French.

- **Source of power**
  - French: `aller à la source de ce pouvoir`
  - Fresh translation preserves the image of going straight to the source.
  - Davidson's phrasing is smoother, but less concentrated.

- **Rapid political violence**
  - French: `étonne, frappe, renverse`
  - Fresh translation keeps the clipped three-verb sequence.
  - Davidson remains effective, though slightly more expanded in rhythm.

- **Final image of concentrated sovereignty**
  - French: `indiquer au premier ambitieux l'endroit où il la trouvera tout entière`
  - Fresh translation keeps the idea that all power has been gathered into one vulnerable spot.
  - Davidson's `shows the first upstart where to strike` is vivid, but more interpretive.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: epigrammatic, brutal, and politically schematic.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the argument terse and the imagery of visible sovereignty sharp.
- **Davidson**: vigorous and readable, but often slightly smoother than the French.
