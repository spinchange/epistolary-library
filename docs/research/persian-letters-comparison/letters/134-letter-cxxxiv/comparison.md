### Translation
I returned to that library the next day, where I found a very different man from the one I had seen the first time: his manner was simple, his face intelligent, and his reception very gracious. As soon as I had made my curiosity known to him, he set himself to satisfying it, and even, because I was a foreigner, to instructing me.

`My father,' I said to him, `what are those great volumes that fill this whole side of the library?' `Those,' he said, `are the interpreters of Scripture.' `There are a great many of them!' I replied. `Scripture must have been very obscure in former times, and very clear now. Are any doubts still left? can there still be disputed points?' `If there are any, good God! if there are any!' he answered me. `There are almost as many as there are lines.' `Indeed?' I said. `And what then have all these authors done?' `These authors,' he replied, `have not searched Scripture for what must be believed, but for what they themselves believe. They have not regarded it as a book containing the doctrines they were supposed to receive, but as a work that might lend authority to their own ideas: that is why they have corrupted all its meanings, and put every passage to the rack. It is a country into which men of every sect make incursions and go as though to pillage; it is a battlefield where hostile nations, meeting one another, fight many battles, attack one another, and skirmish in a thousand ways.'

`Quite nearby you see the ascetic books, or books of devotion; then the books of morals, far more useful; those of theology, doubly unintelligible, both because of the matter treated in them and because of the way it is treated; and the works of the mystics, that is to say, devotees with tender hearts.' `Ah, my father,' I said to him, `one moment; do not go so fast; tell me about these mystics.' `Sir,' he said, `devotion warms a heart disposed to tenderness, and makes it send vapors to the brain that warm it in the same way, from which ecstasies and raptures are born. This state is the delirium of devotion; it often perfects itself, or rather degenerates into quietism. You know that a quietist is nothing other than a madman, devout and libertine.'

`Look at the casuists, who bring to light the secrets of the night; who form in their imagination all the monsters that the demon of love can produce, gather them, compare them, and make them the eternal object of their thoughts: happy are they if their hearts do not enter into the business too, and do not themselves become accomplices in so many wanderings so naively described and so nakedly depicted!'

`You see, sir, that I think freely, and that I tell you everything I think. I am naturally frank, and even more so with you, who are a foreigner, who want to know things, and to know them as they are. If I wished, I would speak to you of all this only with admiration; I would say to you again and again: That is divine, that is worthy of reverence, there is something marvelous in it. And then one of two things would happen: either I would deceive you, or I would dishonor myself in your eyes.'

We stopped there; some business that came up for the dervish broke off our conversation until the next day.

*Paris, the 23rd day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson's version is close in structure, argument, and imagery. He preserves the satirical movement of the letter: the library tour, the attack on scriptural commentary, the mock-clinical description of mysticism, the swipe at casuistry, and the dervish's insistence on speaking plainly.

### Main differences
- **`Les interprètes de l'Écriture`**
  - Fresh translation: `the interpreters of Scripture`
  - Davidson: `interpretations of the Scriptures`
  - Davidson turns the people into products. The sense is recoverable, but the French points more directly toward commentators rather than commentaries.

- **Violence done to the text**
  - French: `ils en ont corrompu tous les sens, et ont donné la torture à tous les passages`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sharper force of `corrupted all its meanings` and `put every passage to the rack`.
  - Davidson's `corrupted its meaning, and twisted every text` is readable, but it softens the juridical and physical brutality of `donner la torture`.

- **Mystics as `coeur tendre`**
  - Fresh translation: `devotees with tender hearts`
  - Davidson: `passionate-hearted devotees`
  - Davidson heightens the emotional coloring. The French is suggestive, but a little plainer and more mocking.

- **Quietism defined**
  - French: `un homme fou, dévot et libertin`
  - Fresh translation: `a madman, devout and libertine`
  - Davidson: `a madman, a devotee, and a libertine, all in one`
  - Davidson expands the cadence; the French is more abrupt and epigrammatic.

- **Casuists and erotic monstrosity**
  - French: `si nuement peints`
  - Fresh translation: `so nakedly depicted`
  - Davidson: `with such simplicity and directness`
  - Davidson gets the clarity, but he tones down the exposed, almost indecent quality of `nuement`.

- **`naïf` / `artless` / `frank`**
  - Fresh translation uses `frank`.
  - Davidson uses `artless`.
  - `naïf` here is less innocence than openness. `Frank` better matches the dervish's declared candor.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: witty, anticlerical, and aggressively lucid; the dervish speaks with the pleasure of someone exposing institutional absurdity.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the satire sharp while staying readable in modern English.
- **Davidson**: broadly faithful, but a little smoother and more literary, especially when the French becomes harsh or indecorous.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXXIV shows Davidson at his usual best when the broad argument is clear and compact. He follows Montesquieu's sequence closely, but he repeatedly smooths harsher phrasing, especially where the French speaks of torture, naked description, or textual corruption.
