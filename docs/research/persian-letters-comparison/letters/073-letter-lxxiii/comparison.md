## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXIII**

**Rica to \*\*\*.**

### Translation
I have heard tell of a kind of tribunal called the French Academy: there is none in the world less respected, for they say that as soon as it has decided, the public overturns its decrees and imposes laws on it that it is obliged to follow.

Some time ago, to establish its authority, it issued a code of its judgments. That child of so many fathers was almost old when it was born; and though it was legitimate, a bastard that had already appeared nearly smothered it at birth.

Those who compose it have no other function than to chatter without ceasing. Praise comes to place itself almost of its own accord in their eternal babble; and as soon as they are initiated into its mysteries, the frenzy of panegyric seizes them and never leaves them.

That body has forty heads, all full of conceits, metaphors, and antitheses. So many mouths speak almost only in exclamations; its ears always want to be struck by cadence and harmony. As for the eyes, there is no question of them: it seems made for speaking and not for seeing. It is not firm on its feet, for time, which is its scourge, shakes it at every instant and destroys everything it has done. It was once said that its hands were greedy; I will tell you nothing of that, and leave it to those who know the matter better than I do to decide.

Those are oddities, \*\*\*, that one does not see in our Persia. We are not inclined to such singular and bizarre establishments; we always seek nature in our simple customs and our artless manners.

*At Paris, the 27th day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the main satirical targets of the letter: the Academy's weak authority, the absurdity of its dictionary project, its culture of mutual praise, and its rhetorical emptiness.

### Main differences
- **Public overturning decrees**
  - French: `le peuple casse ses arrêts`
  - Fresh translation: `the public overturns its decrees`
  - Davidson: `the people break it`
  - Davidson gets the sense, but the fresh version keeps more of the juridical image.

- **Child of many fathers**
  - French: `Cet enfant de tant de pères étoit presque vieux quand il naquit`
  - Fresh translation keeps the deliberately absurd birth image.
  - Davidson's `may be said to have been old when it was born` is very close.

- **`Jaser sans cesse`**
  - Fresh translation: `to chatter without ceasing`
  - Davidson: `to jabber perpetually`
  - Davidson is more aggressively comic here; the French is biting, but a touch less grotesque.

- **Figures / conceits**
  - French: `toutes remplies de figures, de métaphores et d'antithèses`
  - Fresh translation: `all full of conceits, metaphors, and antitheses`
  - Davidson: `chokeful of tropes, of metaphors, and of antitheses`
  - Davidson heightens the rhetoric; `figures` is broader and slightly less showy than `tropes`.

- **Seeking nature**
  - French: `nous cherchons toujours la nature dans nos coutumes simples et nos manières naïves`
  - Fresh translation: `we always seek nature in our simple customs and our artless manners`
  - Davidson: `we endeavour to shape our simple customs and artless manners in the mould of nature`
  - Davidson turns a simple claim into a more elaborate image.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: coolly mocking, imagistic, and institutionally satirical.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the legal and bodily metaphors while staying relatively plain.
- **Davidson**: often very good, but somewhat more flamboyant in diction.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXIII is a sharp satire on the French Academy as a pompous but powerless institution. Davidson preserves the broad critique, but the fresh translation keeps closer to Montesquieu's juridical framing and his plainer final contrast with Persian simplicity.
