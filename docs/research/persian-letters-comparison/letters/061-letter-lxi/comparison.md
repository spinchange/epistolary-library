## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXI**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
The other day I entered a famous church called Notre-Dame. While I was admiring that superb building, I had occasion to converse with an ecclesiastic whom curiosity had drawn there as it had me. Our conversation fell upon the tranquility of his profession. Most people, he told me, envy the happiness of our condition, and they are right; yet it has its inconveniences. We are not so far separated from the world that we are not summoned there on a thousand occasions, and there we have a very difficult role to sustain.

Worldly people are astonishing. They can endure neither our approval nor our censure. If we try to correct them, they find us ridiculous; if we approve them, they regard us as beneath our character. Nothing is so humiliating as to think one has scandalized even the impious. So we are obliged to maintain an equivocal conduct and to impose upon libertines, not by a decided character, but by the uncertainty in which we leave them about the way we receive their remarks. That requires much wit; this state of neutrality is difficult. Men of the world, who risk everything, abandon themselves to every sallie, and according to success either press it or drop it, succeed much better.

Nor is that all. That happy and tranquil state so much praised is not preserved for us in the world. As soon as we appear there, we are forced into disputation. We are made, for example, to prove the usefulness of prayer to a man who does not believe in God, or the necessity of fasting to another who has spent his life denying the immortality of the soul. The enterprise is laborious, and the laughers are not on our side. More than that: a certain desire to draw others into our opinions torments us continually and is, so to speak, attached to our profession. That is as ridiculous as if Europeans, in the interest of human nature, worked to whiten the faces of Africans. We disturb the state and torment ourselves in order to have points of religion accepted that are not fundamental. We resemble that conqueror of China who drove his subjects into general revolt because he wanted to force them to trim their hair or nails.

Even the zeal we have for making those entrusted to us fulfill the duties of our holy religion is often dangerous, and cannot be accompanied by too much prudence. An emperor named Theodosius had all the inhabitants of a city put to the sword, women and little children included. When he later presented himself to enter a church, a bishop named Ambrose had the doors shut against him as against a murderer and a sacrilegious man; in that he performed a heroic action. But when that emperor had afterward done the penance required by such a crime and, having been admitted into the church, went and placed himself among the priests, the same bishop made him leave. In that he committed the act of a fanatic and a madman. So true is it that one must mistrust one's zeal. What did it matter to religion or the state whether that prince had or had not a place among the priests?

*From Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Rebiab I, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the whole argumentative arc intact: the social awkwardness of clergy in polite society, their compulsion to argue nonessential points, and the final warning against excess zeal through the example of Ambrose and Theodosius.

### Main differences
- **Approval and censure**
  - Fresh translation keeps the balanced pair `approbation / censures` in more literal form.
  - Davidson is close but slightly more formalized.
- **Equivocal conduct**
  - French: `une conduite équivoque`
  - Fresh translation preserves the ambiguity.
  - Davidson's `an ambiguous method` is accurate but more abstract.
- **Whiten the faces of Africans**
  - Fresh translation retains the bodily absurdity of the simile.
  - Davidson's `change the Ethiopian's skin` is more generalized and more biblical in resonance.
- **Fanatic and madman**
  - French: `fanatique et d'un fou`
  - Fresh translation keeps both nouns bluntly.
  - Davidson reduces the line slightly by making it more polished.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: reflective, anticlerical without being irreligious, and neatly paradoxical.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the speaker's balance between sympathy for clergy and criticism of zeal.
- **Davidson**: strong and close, though a bit more sermon-like in cadence.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXI shows Montesquieu's preference for measured religion over militant zeal. Davidson conveys this well, but the fresh translation keeps the French's sharper turns of irony and contradiction.
