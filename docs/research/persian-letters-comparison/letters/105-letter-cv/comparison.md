## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CV**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
Not all the peoples of Europe are equally subject to their princes. For example, the impatient temper of the English scarcely leaves their king time to weigh down his authority. Submission and obedience are among the virtues on which they pride themselves least. They say some very extraordinary things on this subject. According to them, there is only one bond that can attach men, and that is gratitude. Husband and wife, father and son, are tied together only by the love they bear one another, or by the benefits they confer on one another; and those various motives of gratitude are the origin of all kingdoms and all societies.

But if a prince, far from making his subjects live happily, wants to crush and destroy them, the foundation of obedience disappears. Nothing binds them, nothing attaches them to him, and they return to their natural liberty. They maintain that all boundless power cannot be legitimate, because it can never have had a legitimate origin. For we cannot, they say, give another more power over us than we ourselves have. Now we do not have boundless power over ourselves: for example, we cannot take away our own lives. Therefore no one on earth, they conclude, has such power.

The crime of high treason, according to them, is nothing other than the crime the weaker commits against the stronger in disobeying him, no matter how that disobedience occurs. Thus the people of England, who found themselves stronger than one of their kings, declared it high treason for a prince to make war upon his subjects. They are therefore quite right when they say that the precept of their Alcoran ordering submission to the powers that be is not very hard to follow, since it is impossible not to observe it - especially as one is not compelled to submit to the most virtuous, but to the strongest.

The English say that one of their kings, after defeating and capturing a prince who had rebelled and contested the crown with him, wanted to reproach him for his infidelity and perfidy. `Only a moment ago,` said the unfortunate prince, `it was decided which of us two was the traitor.`

A usurper declares rebels all those who have not oppressed the fatherland as he has, and, believing that there is no law where he sees no judges, makes the caprices of chance and fortune revered as though they were decrees of heaven.

*Paris, the 20th day of the moon of Rebiab II, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the letter's broad constitutional argument: English political thinking treats obedience as conditional, denies the legitimacy of unlimited power, and exposes treason as a label often assigned by the stronger side.

### Main differences
- **Authority as weight**
  - French: `appesantir son autorité`
  - Fresh translation keeps the bodily image of authority weighing down on subjects.
  - Davidson's `make his authority felt` is smoother but less forceful.

- **Gratitude vs obligation**
  - French repeatedly uses `reconnoissance`
  - Fresh translation keeps `gratitude` throughout.
  - Davidson sometimes shifts toward `obligation`, which is broader and less exact.

- **Unlimited power and legitimacy**
  - Fresh translation keeps the French sequence tightly argumentative: no boundless power can be legitimate because it never could have had a legitimate origin.
  - Davidson is clear here, but slightly more abstract in cadence.

- **The `Alcoran` reference**
  - Fresh translation leaves the satirical `their Alcoran` within Usbek's own voice.
  - Davidson appends a note glossing this as the New Testament, which is useful editorially but external to the letter's rhetoric.

- **Closing political image**
  - French: `opprimé la patrie`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stronger phrase `oppressed the fatherland`.
  - Davidson's wording is close, but somewhat flatter.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: provocative, compressed, and ironic.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the concrete metaphors of weight, gratitude, and force.
- **Davidson**: lucid and vigorous, though somewhat more explanatory.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean file still shows visible OCR problems, including `TJSBEK` in the title line and `no matterwhat` in the treason paragraph.
- A trailing note glosses `their Koran` as the New Testament; that note should not be confused with the body of the letter.

## Working conclusion
Davidson preserves the logic of the letter well. A fresh translation is most useful where the French relies on physical and juridical metaphors - authority weighing down, gratitude binding, strength defining treason - that can otherwise be softened into abstract constitutional prose.
