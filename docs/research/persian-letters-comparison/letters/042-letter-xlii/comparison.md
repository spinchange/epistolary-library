## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLII**

**Pharan to Usbek, his sovereign lord**

### Translation
If you were here, magnificent lord, I would appear before you entirely covered with white paper, and there would still not be enough of it to write down all the insults your chief black eunuch, the wickedest of all men, has done me since your departure.

Under the pretext of a few jests that he claims I made about the misfortune of his condition, he is exercising on my head an inexhaustible vengeance; he has set against me the cruel steward of your gardens, who since your departure has forced me into insurmountable labors, in the midst of which I have thought a thousand times to lose my life without for a single moment losing my ardor to serve you. How many times have I said to myself: `I have a master full of gentleness, and I am the most unhappy slave on earth!'

I confess to you, magnificent lord, I did not believe myself destined for greater miseries, but that traitorous eunuch has wished to crown his wickedness. A few days ago, on his own private authority, he assigned me to the guard of your sacred women, that is to say to an execution that would be for me a thousand times crueler than death. Those who at birth had the misfortune to receive such treatment from their cruel parents perhaps console themselves with the thought that they have never known any other state than their own; but if I am made to descend from humanity and deprived of it, I would die of grief if I did not die from that barbarity.

I embrace your feet, sublime lord, in profound humility: let me feel the effects of that virtue so much revered, and let it not be said that by your order there is on earth one unhappy man more.

*From the gardens of Fatmé, the 7th day of the moon of Maharram, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the plea clearly: Pharan complains of abuse, forced labor, and the threatened mutilation that would make him a eunuch. He is generally faithful in sense, but he sometimes explains or dignifies phrases whose power in French lies in their stark, almost physical directness.

### Main differences
- **The white-paper hyperbole**
  - French: `je paroîtrois à ta vue tout couvert de papier blanc`
  - Fresh translation: `I would appear before you entirely covered with white paper`
  - Davidson: `I would appear to you clad from head to foot in white paper`
  - Davidson is vivid, though slightly fuller and more theatrical than the French.

- **The scope of vengeance**
  - French: `il exerce sur ma tête une vengeance inépuisable`
  - Fresh translation: `he is exercising on my head an inexhaustible vengeance`
  - Davidson: `he makes me the victim of an insatiable vengeance`
  - Davidson captures the sense, but the French's more bodily phrasing is sharper.

- **Forced labor and the near-loss of life**
  - French: `j'ai pensé mille fois laisser la vie`
  - Fresh translation: `I have thought a thousand times to lose my life`
  - Davidson: `I have a thousand times taken leave of life`
  - Davidson is elegant, but the French is starker.

- **`Une exécution`**
  - French: `à une exécution qui seroit pour moi mille fois plus cruelle que la mort`
  - Fresh translation: `to an execution that would be for me a thousand times crueler than death`
  - Davidson: `to a punishment which to me would be a thousand times more cruel than death`
  - `Punishment` is understandable, but `execution` preserves the violence and immediacy of the original.

- **Descending from humanity**
  - French: `qu'on me fasse descendre de l'humanité et qu'on m'en prive`
  - Fresh translation: `if I am made to descend from humanity and deprived of it`
  - Davidson: `were I to lose my place among men, and be deprived of that which makes me human`
  - Davidson explains the line well, but it is a freer paraphrase of a deliberately brutal formulation.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: desperate, abject, and rhetorically extreme; Pharan pleads by dramatizing humiliation in bodily terms.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve both the panic and the literal violence of the threat.
- **Davidson**: clear and emotionally legible, but somewhat more polished and interpretive.

## Working conclusion
Letter XLII reads well enough in Davidson, but the fresh translation better preserves the extremity of Pharan's language, especially where the French insists on the threatened loss not merely of status but of humanity itself.
