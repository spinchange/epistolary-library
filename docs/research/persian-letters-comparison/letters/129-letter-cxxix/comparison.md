### Translation
The other day I was crossing the Pont-Neuf with one of my friends. He met a man he knew, whom he told me was a geometer; and everything about him showed it, for he was sunk in deep abstraction. My friend had to tug at his sleeve for a long time and shake him to bring him back down to us, so preoccupied was he with a curve that had perhaps been tormenting him for more than eight days. They exchanged many civilities and passed on to one another some literary news. Their talk led them as far as the door of a coffeehouse, where I went in with them.

I noticed that our geometer was received there with eagerness by everybody, and that the coffeehouse servants made much more of him than of two musketeers sitting in a corner. As for him, he seemed pleased to be in such a place; for his face relaxed a little, and he began to laugh as if he had not the slightest tincture of geometry.

Yet his orderly mind measured everything said in the conversation. He resembled the man who, in a garden, cut off with his sword the heads of all the flowers that rose above the others. A martyr to his own exactness, he was offended by a flash of wit, just as delicate sight is offended by too bright a light. Nothing was indifferent to him, provided it was true; and so his conversation was singular. That day he had come in from the country with a man who had seen a superb château and magnificent gardens; he, however, had seen only a building sixty feet long by thirty-five wide, and an oblong grove of ten arpents. He would have greatly wished the rules of perspective to have been so well observed that the avenues appeared everywhere of the same width, and for that he would have provided an infallible method. He seemed very satisfied with a sundial of rather singular construction that he had managed to distinguish there; and he grew quite heated against a learned man standing near me who asked him whether the dial marked Babylonian hours. A newsmonger spoke of the bombardment of the castle of Fontarabia, and at once he gave us the properties of the line the bombs had described in the air; delighted to know that much, he wanted to remain entirely ignorant of the result. A man complained of having been ruined the previous winter by a flood. 'What you tell me is very agreeable,' the geometer said then. 'I see that I was not mistaken in the observation I made, and that at least two inches more water fell upon the earth than last year.'

A moment later he went out, and we followed him. Since he was walking rather fast and neglected to look in front of him, he ran straight into another man. They collided violently; and from the shock they rebounded, each to his own side, in inverse proportion to their speed and mass. When they had somewhat recovered from their bewilderment, the man, putting his hand to his forehead, said to the geometer: 'I am delighted you bumped into me, for I have great news to tell you: I have just given my Horace to the public.' 'What!' said the geometer. 'It has been there for two thousand years.' 'You do not understand me,' replied the other. 'It is a translation of that ancient author that I have just brought out; I have spent twenty years making translations.'

'What, sir,' said the geometer, 'for twenty years you have not been thinking! You speak for others, and they think for you!' 'Sir,' said the learned man, 'do you believe I have not rendered a great service to the public by making the reading of good authors familiar to it?' 'I do not quite say that. I esteem as much as anyone those sublime geniuses whom you disguise; but you will not resemble them at all, for if you go on translating forever, no one will ever translate you.

'Translations are like those copper coins that do indeed have the same value as a gold piece, and are even of greater use to the common people; but they are always weak and of bad alloy.

'You say you want to make those illustrious dead live again among us; and I grant that you do indeed give them a body: but you do not restore life to them; there is always a spirit lacking to animate them.

'Why do you not apply yourself instead to the search for so many fine truths that easy calculation reveals to us every day?' After this little piece of advice, they parted, I think, very displeased with one another.

*Paris, the last day of the moon of Rebiab II, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries over the broad comic contrast very well: the geometer is socially awkward, mentally exact, contemptuous of wit, and finally merciless to the translator who mistakes derivative labor for original genius.

### Main differences
- **`martyr de sa justesse`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the paradox directly: `a martyr to his own exactness`.
  - Davidson also keeps it, which is excellent; it is one of the sharpest phrases in the letter.

- **`travestissez`**
  - Fresh translation: `those sublime geniuses whom you disguise`
  - Davidson's `travestied` is strong and close, though the fresh draft makes the rebuke sound slightly less theatrical and more contemptuous.

- **`on ne vous traduira jamais`**
  - Fresh translation: `no one will ever translate you`
  - Davidson captures the joke well.
  - The line works because translation is treated not simply as service, but as proof of secondary rank.

- **Copper coin comparison**
  - French: `foibles et d'un mauvais aloi`
  - Fresh translation keeps both weakness and bad alloy.
  - Davidson's `base coin and always light` is vivid, but slightly freer.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: satirical, precise, and delighted by intellectual caricature.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the geometrical diction and the cruelty of the final exchange.
- **Davidson**: often excellent, but sometimes a little more novelistic in movement than Montesquieu's exact, needling prose.
