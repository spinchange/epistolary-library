## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXIV**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
We have been in Paris for a month now, and we have been in continual motion the whole time. It takes a great many matters before one is lodged, has found the people to whom one is addressed, and has supplied oneself with the necessary things, all of which are lacking at once.

Paris is as large as Isfahan. The houses are so high that one would think they were inhabited only by astrologers. You can well imagine that a city built in the air, with six or seven houses one on top of another, must be extremely populous, and that, when everyone has come down into the street, there must be a fine confusion there.

Perhaps you will not believe it, but in the month I have been here I have not yet seen anyone walk. There are no people in the world who get better use out of their machine than the French: they run, they fly. The slow carriages of Asia, the measured pace of our camels, would make them faint. As for me, who am not made for such a pace, and who often go on foot without changing my gait, I sometimes rage like a Christian. Let it pass that I am splashed from head to foot; I cannot forgive the elbow-blows I receive regularly and periodically. A man coming from behind and passing me makes me turn half around, and another crossing from the other side suddenly sets me back where the first had taken me. I have not gone a hundred steps before I am more broken than if I had gone ten leagues.

Do not think I can yet speak to you deeply about European customs and manners. I myself have only a slight idea of them, and have hardly had time to do more than be astonished.

The king of France is the most powerful prince in Europe. He has no gold mines like the king of Spain, his neighbor, but he has more wealth than that king because he draws it from the vanity of his subjects, which is more inexhaustible than mines. He has been seen undertaking and sustaining great wars with no other funds than titles of honor to sell, and by a prodigy of human pride his troops were paid, his strongholds provisioned, and his fleets equipped.

Besides, this king is a great magician. He exercises his empire over the minds of his subjects themselves: he makes them think as he wishes. If he has only a million écus in his treasury and he needs two, he has only to persuade them that one écu is worth two, and they believe it. If he has a difficult war to sustain and has no money, he has only to put it into their heads that a piece of paper is money, and they are immediately convinced. He even goes so far as to make them believe he cures all sorts of illnesses by touching them, so great is the force and power he has over minds.

What I tell you of this prince should not astonish you. There is another magician, stronger than he is, who is no less master of his mind than the king is of other people's. This magician is called the pope. Sometimes he makes the king believe that three are only one; that the bread one eats is not bread; or that the wine one drinks is not wine; and a thousand other things of that sort.

And, to keep him always in practice and never let him lose the habit of believing, from time to time he gives him certain articles of belief as exercises. Two years ago he sent him a great document that he called a constitution, and wanted to oblige, under heavy penalties, both prince and subjects to believe everything it contained. He succeeded with the prince, who submitted at once and set the example for his subjects; but some of them revolted and said they wanted to believe nothing of what was in that writing. The women have been the prime movers of the whole revolt, which divides the court, the kingdom, and all families. This constitution forbids them to read a book that all Christians say was brought down from heaven; it is properly speaking their Koran. The women, indignant at the outrage done to their sex, raise everyone against the constitution. They have put the men on their side, and in this matter the men want no privilege. Still, it must be admitted that this mufti reasons rather well; and, by great Ali, he must have been instructed in the principles of our holy law. Since women are of an inferior creation to ours, and our prophets tell us they will not enter paradise, why should they meddle with reading a book made only to teach the road to paradise?

I have heard extraordinary things told of the king, and I do not doubt that you will hesitate to believe them.

They say that while he was making war on his neighbors, who had all allied against him, he had within his kingdom an innumerable multitude of invisible enemies surrounding him. They add that he searched for them for more than thirty years, and that, despite the tireless care of certain dervishes who have his confidence, he has not been able to find a single one. They live with him; they are at his court, in his capital, in his armies, in his tribunals; and yet they say he will have the grief of dying without having found them. One would say they exist in general and are nothing in particular: it is a body, but with no members. No doubt heaven wishes to punish that prince for not having been moderate enough toward the enemies he defeated, since it gives him invisible ones whose genius and destiny stand above his own.

I shall continue to write to you and tell you things very far from the Persian character and genius. It is indeed the same earth that carries us both; but the men of the country where I live and those of the country where you are are very different men.

*From Paris, the 4th day of the second moon of Rebiab, 1712.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives a lively and largely faithful version of Rica's first long Paris satire. He preserves the movement from urban bewilderment to monetary magic, papal theology, the `constitution`, and the king's `invisible enemies`. His English is readable and energetic, but he often makes Rica's estranging perspective more idiomatic than the French.

### Main differences
- **Machine / body image**
  - French: `tirent mieux partie de leur machine`
  - Fresh translation: `get better use out of their machine`
  - Davidson: `hold more by their vehicles`
  - This is the clearest substantive mistranslation in the letter. Rica is talking about the human body, not about conveyances.

- **Built in the air**
  - French: `une ville bâtie en l'air`
  - Fresh translation keeps the hyperbolic image of stacked houses and aerial urbanism.
  - Davidson is close overall, but his phrasing is a little more explanatory and less abruptly comic.

- **Paper as money**
  - French: `un morceau de papier est de l'argent`
  - Fresh translation: `a piece of paper is money`
  - Davidson: `a piece of paper is coin of the realm`
  - Davidson domesticates the joke by adding a specifically British-sounding monetary phrase.

- **The `constitution` and the men who join the women**
  - French: `elles ont mis les hommes de leur parti, qui, dans cette occasion, ne veulent point avoir de privilége`
  - Fresh translation: `They have put the men on their side, and in this matter the men want no privilege.`
  - Davidson: `they have brought over to their side all the men who are not anxious about their privilege in the matter`
  - Davidson's line is harder to parse and weakens the joke that men suddenly renounce privilege when women's access to scripture is involved.

- **Invisible enemies as body without members**
  - French: `c'est un corps; mais point de membres`
  - Fresh translation preserves the bizarre political metaphor.
  - Davidson keeps the image, but he introduces it more smoothly, which slightly softens the strangeness.

- **Closing contrast between two countries**
  - French: `les hommes du pays où je vis, et ceux du pays où tu es, sont des hommes bien différents`
  - Fresh translation preserves the balanced antithesis.
  - Davidson's closing sentence is more awkward and less pointed.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: rapid, comic, satirical, and deliberately foreignizing in its logic.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps Rica's outsider perspective and the blunt literalness on which many of the jokes depend.
- **Davidson**: lively and often effective, but occasionally too idiomatic, especially where the satire works by making French institutions sound strange from a Persian point of view.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is readable, but it still contains footnote numerals and punctuation noise, especially in the section on the `Constitution`.
- Davidson's text also shows a slightly awkward final sentence that is probably recoverable but worth checking against the scan before quotation.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXIV is a strong comparison case. Davidson captures the main satirical targets, but the mistranslation of `machine` and his general tendency to smooth Rica's estranging language make a fresh translation especially useful here.
