## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LV**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
Among the peoples of Europe, the first quarter-hour of marriage smooths over all difficulties; the final favors always bear the same date as the nuptial blessing. Women here do not act like our Persian women, who sometimes dispute the ground for whole months. Nothing is so complete. If they lose nothing, it is because they have nothing to lose; but one always knows — shameful thing! — the moment of their defeat, and, without consulting the stars, one can predict to the very hour the birth of their children.

The French hardly ever speak of their wives: that is because they are afraid to speak of them before people who know them better than they do.

Among them there are men profoundly unhappy, whom nobody consoles: jealous husbands. There are some whom everybody hates: jealous husbands. There are some whom all men despise: once again, jealous husbands.

So there is no country where they are so scarce as among the French. Their tranquility is not founded on the trust they place in their wives; on the contrary, it is founded on the poor opinion they have of them. All the prudent precautions of Asians — the veils that cover women, the prisons in which they are confined, the vigilance of eunuchs — seem to them more likely to exercise female ingenuity than to wear it out. Here husbands take their lot in good part and regard infidelities as blows from an inevitable star. A husband who wished to possess his wife alone would be looked upon as a disturber of public joy, and as a madman who wanted to enjoy the light of the sun to the exclusion of other men.

Here, a husband who loves his wife is a man who lacks enough merit to make himself loved by another woman; who abuses the necessity created by the law to make up for the charms he lacks; who uses all his advantages to the detriment of an entire society; who appropriates to himself what had been given him only in trust; and who does all he can to overturn a tacit agreement that makes the happiness of both sexes. This title of husband of a pretty woman, so carefully concealed in Asia, is worn here without uneasiness: one feels able to create a diversion anywhere. A prince consoles himself for the loss of one place by taking another: while the Turk was taking Baghdad from us, were we not carrying off the fortress of Kandahar from the Mogul?

A man who, in general, tolerates his wife's infidelities is not disapproved; on the contrary, he is praised for his prudence: only particular cases are dishonoring.

Not that there are no virtuous ladies; one may even say that they are conspicuous. My guide always pointed them out to me; but they were all so ugly that one would have to be a saint not to hate virtue.

After what I have told you about the morals of this country, you can easily imagine that the French do not pride themselves much on constancy: they think it as ridiculous to swear to a woman that one will love her forever as to insist that one will always be in good health or always happy. When they promise a woman that they will love her always, they assume that she, for her part, promises always to remain lovable; and if she breaks her word, they no longer think themselves bound by theirs.

*At Paris, the 7th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson is quite faithful to the argument and sequencing of the letter. He keeps Rica's deliberately cynical anthropology of marriage, jealousy, and French sociability, and he reproduces the increasingly outrageous wit of the central paradoxes.

### Main differences
- **The opening sexual bluntness**
  - Fresh translation: `the first quarter-hour of marriage smooths over all difficulties`
  - Davidson: `the first quarter of an hour of marriage settles all difficulties`
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh version keeps a little more of the French's dry, social-observational tone.

- **The legal / fiduciary metaphor**
  - French: `ce qui ne lui avoit été donné qu'en engagement`
  - Fresh translation: `what had been given him only in trust`
  - Davidson: `that which he only holds in pawn`
  - `In pawn` is vivid, but more commercial and less socially contractual than the French.

- **Military language in gallantry**
  - French: `on se sent en état de faire diversion partout`
  - Fresh translation preserves the idea of being able to make a diversion elsewhere.
  - Davidson's `No one has ever to seek far for entertainment` captures the worldly implication but drops the military metaphor.

- **Bad opinion vs confidence**
  - French states pointedly that husbands are tranquil not because they trust their wives, but because they think poorly of them.
  - Davidson keeps this well, though the fresh translation keeps the sharper parallel between `trust` and `poor opinion`.

- **Constancy and conditions**
  - Both versions preserve the final logic that promises of eternal love are conditional on the woman remaining lovable.
  - Davidson is especially close here, with only minor Victorian smoothing.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: epigrammatic, ruthless, and coolly amused; the provocations are meant to sound socially analytical as much as obscene.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the hard wit and the legal / strategic metaphors that structure Rica's satire.
- **Davidson**: very readable and often accurate, but sometimes slightly expands or domesticates the sharper images.

## Working conclusion
Letter LV is one of the book's coldest jokes about marriage as a public rather than private institution. Davidson reproduces most of the sense very well, but the fresh translation keeps a little more of Montesquieu's military, legal, and satirical precision.
