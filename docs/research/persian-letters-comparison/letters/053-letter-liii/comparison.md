## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LIII**

**Zélis to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
No passion was ever stronger or livelier than that of Cosrou, the white eunuch, for my slave Zélide. He asks for her in marriage with such fury that I can no longer refuse him. And why should I resist, when her mother does not, and when Zélide herself seems pleased with the idea of this counterfeit marriage and the empty shadow being offered to her?

What can she want with that unfortunate man, who will have of a husband only jealousy; who will leave his coldness only to enter useless despair; who will always recall the memory of what he once was only to remind her of what he no longer is; who, always ready to give himself and never giving himself, will keep deceiving himself and her, and at every moment make her suffer all the miseries of his condition?

What then! to live forever among images and phantoms? To live only in imagination? To remain always beside pleasures and never within them? To languish in the arms of an unfortunate wretch and, instead of answering his sighs, answer only his regrets?

What contempt one must feel for a man made only to guard and never to possess. I seek love, and I do not see it.

I speak to you freely because you love my frankness, and because you prefer my open air and my feeling for pleasures to the feigned modesty of my companions.

I have heard you say a thousand times that eunuchs enjoy with women a kind of voluptuousness unknown to us; that nature compensates for its losses; that it has resources that repair the disadvantage of their condition; that one may indeed cease to be a man, but not to be sensitive; and that in such a state one is as though in a third sense, where one only changes pleasures, so to speak.

If that is true, I would find Zélide less to be pitied. It is something to live with people who are less unhappy.

Give me your orders on this point, and let me know whether you want the marriage to take place in the seraglio. Farewell.

*From the seraglio of Isfahan, the 5th day of the moon of Chalval, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson renders the basic erotic-philosophical complaint clearly: Zélis mocks the idea of marrying a eunuch, then recalls Usbek's own theories about eunuch sensuality and asks for instructions.

### Main differences
- **Counterfeit marriage / empty shadow**
  - French: `ce mariage imposteur, et l'ombre vaine`
  - Fresh translation keeps the harsh doubleness.
  - Davidson: `this mock union, and the empty shadow` is close and effective.
- **Ready to give himself**
  - French: `toujours prêt à se donner`
  - Fresh translation preserves the bodily offering implied.
  - Davidson turns the line toward `possess`, which narrows the sexual paradox.
- **Sensitive vs desire**
  - French: `non pas d'être sensible`
  - Fresh translation: `not to be sensitive`
  - Davidson: `not to feel desire`
  - Davidson intensifies and eroticizes the line more than the French does.
- **Third sense**
  - Fresh translation keeps the strange phrase `third sense`.
  - Davidson also keeps it, and rightly so.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: frank, erotic, and contemptuous, but also intellectually curious.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the provocative directness without reducing the letter to mere complaint.
- **Davidson**: vivid and elegant, though slightly more sensualized in places.

## Working conclusion
Letter LIII depends on the sharpness of its sexual paradoxes. Davidson is mostly close, but the French is slightly stranger and more analytical than his smoother Victorian rendering suggests.
