### Translation
I have seen people in whom virtue was so natural that it was not even felt as such. They devoted themselves to duty without bending themselves to it, and went toward it as if by instinct. Far from making their rare qualities stand out in their speech, it seemed that those qualities had never even reached their own awareness. Those are the people I love — not those virtuous persons who seem astonished to be virtuous, and who treat a good action as a prodigy whose story must amaze everyone.

If modesty is a necessary virtue in those to whom Heaven has given great talents, what is to be said of those insects who dare display a pride that would dishonor the greatest men?

Everywhere I see people talking endlessly about themselves. Their conversation is a mirror that always reflects their own impertinent face. They tell you the smallest things that have happened to them and want the interest they take in them to enlarge those things in your eyes. They have done everything, seen everything, said everything, thought everything. They are a universal model, an inexhaustible source of comparison, a spring of examples that never runs dry. Oh, how insipid praise is when it is reflected back toward the place from which it came.

A few days ago a man of this sort overwhelmed us for two hours with himself, his merit, and his talents; but since there is no perpetual motion in the world, he eventually stopped talking, and conversation returned to us.

A man who seemed rather gloomy began by complaining of the boredom spread through conversation. What! always fools painting themselves and drawing everything back to themselves? You are right, our speaker broke in at once: one has only to do as I do — I never praise myself. I have means, birth, I spend freely, my friends say I have some wit; but I never speak of any of that. If I have a few good qualities, the one I value most is my modesty.

I admired this impertinent fellow; and while he was speaking aloud, I said to myself in a low voice: Happy the man who has enough vanity never to speak well of himself, who fears those who listen to him, and never compromises his own merit with the pride of others.

*At Paris, the 20th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the essay-like structure of the letter well: natural virtue, attacks on vanity, and the final comic example of a boaster who prides himself on modesty.

### Main differences
- **Without bending to duty**
  - French: `sans s'y plier`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sense that duty costs them no strain.
  - Davidson: `without any compulsion`, which is accurate but less bodily.
- **Universal model**
  - French: `un modèle universel`
  - Fresh translation stays close.
  - Davidson also stays close here and is effective.
- **Perpetual motion joke**
  - French: `il n'y a point de mouvement perpétuel`
  - Fresh translation preserves the scientific-comic turn.
  - Davidson does too, though the OCR is damaged around the phrase.
- **Compromising merit with others' pride**
  - French: `ne compromet point son mérite avec l'orgueil des autres`
  - Fresh translation keeps the social balance of the idea.
  - Davidson turns it into `hurts the pride of others`, which narrows the line.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: moralizing, satirical, and lightly aphoristic.
- **Fresh translation**: aims for a plain, pointed English that preserves the neat turns of irony.
- **Davidson**: generally close, but a bit more sententious and polished.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text still shows OCR corruption around the `two hours` / `perpetual motion` transition.
- The overall argument remains clear despite that noise.

## Working conclusion
Letter L is structurally simple but stylistically neat. Davidson remains dependable, though the fresh translation preserves a little more of the French balance between satire and moral observation.
