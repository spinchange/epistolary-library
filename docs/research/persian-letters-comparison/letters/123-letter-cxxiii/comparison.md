## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXIII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
The mildness of government contributes wonderfully to the propagation of the species. All republics are a constant proof of it, and more than all others Switzerland and Holland, which are the two worst countries in Europe if one considers the nature of the soil, and which are nevertheless the most populated.

Nothing attracts foreigners more than liberty, and the opulence that always follows it. The one is sought for itself, and needs draw men into the countries where the other is found.

The species multiplies in a country where abundance provides for children without diminishing the subsistence of fathers.

The very equality of citizens, which ordinarily produces equality in fortunes, brings abundance and life into all the parts of the body politic and spreads it everywhere.

It is not the same in countries subject to arbitrary power. The prince, the courtiers, and a few private individuals possess all the wealth, while all the rest groan in extreme poverty.

If a man is poor and feels that he will make children poorer than himself, he will not marry; or if he does marry, he will fear having too many children, who may complete the disorder of his fortune and descend below their father's condition.

I admit that the rustic or peasant, once married, will people the land equally whether he is rich or poor; that consideration does not touch him. He always has a sure inheritance to leave his children, and that is his hoe; and nothing ever prevents him from blindly following the instinct of nature.

But what use is that number of children to a state if they languish in misery? They perish almost as they are born; they never prosper. Weak and feeble, they die one by one in a thousand ways, while whole groups are swept away by the frequent epidemic diseases that poverty and bad nourishment always produce. Those who escape reach manhood without possessing its strength, and languish for the rest of their lives.

Men are like plants, which never grow happily unless they are well cultivated: among miserable peoples the species declines, and sometimes even degenerates.

France can provide a great example of all this. In past wars, the fear all younger sons lived under of being enrolled in the militia obliged them to marry, and to do so at too tender an age and in the bosom of poverty. From so many marriages many children were born, and France still seeks them, for misery, famine, and disease have made them disappear.

And if, under so happy a sky and in so well-policed a kingdom as France, such observations can be made, what must it be in other states?

*Paris, the 23rd day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the overall argument of the letter very well: mild government, liberty, equality, wealth distribution, reproductive choice, rural persistence, and the wasting of children under poverty. His English is mostly close, but a few significant lexical shifts alter the political-economic emphasis.

### Main differences
- **Liberty and wealth reversed**
  - French: `l'une se fait rechercher par elle-même, et les besoins attirent dans les pays où l'on trouve l'autre`
  - Fresh translation keeps the likely sense that liberty is sought for itself, while needs draw people where opulence is found.
  - Davidson reverses the reference and says wealth is sought for itself while necessity draws people to liberty. That changes the logic of the sentence.

- **`hoyau`**
  - Fresh translation: `hoe`
  - Davidson: `plough`
  - `Hoyau` is closer to `hoe` or mattock than to `plough`, so Davidson slightly upgrades the peasant's implement.

- **`déranger sa fortune`**
  - Fresh translation: `complete the disorder of his fortune`
  - Davidson: `complete the ruin of his fortune`
  - Davidson intensifies the phrase. The French suggests disruption or derangement more than total ruin.

- **Children dying `en détail` / `en gros`**
  - Fresh translation preserves the pointed contrast between dying `one by one` and being swept away in masses by epidemics.
  - Davidson's `retail` and `wholesale` is vivid and actually quite strong, though more idiomatic and commercial in flavor.

- **`peuples misérables`**
  - Fresh translation: `miserable peoples`
  - Davidson: `poor people`
  - Davidson narrows a collective social condition into a more individualized phrase.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: political, demographic, and morally severe, but less flamboyant than some of the preceding letters.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the letter's clarity and its emphasis on government, equality, and material conditions.
- **Davidson**: often very close, though occasionally he shifts the balance of key abstractions.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXIII is one of the cleaner English comparisons in this set, but the liberty/opulence sentence is especially important because Davidson appears to reverse its logic. Elsewhere his version is generally strong, though slightly more dramatic in places like `ruin of his fortune`.
