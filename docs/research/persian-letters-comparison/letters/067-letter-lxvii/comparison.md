### Heading
**Letter LXVII**

**Ibben to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
Three ships have arrived here without bringing me any news from you. Are you ill, or do you take pleasure in making me anxious?

If you do not love me in a country where nothing binds you, what will it be like in the midst of Persia and in the bosom of your family? But perhaps I am mistaken: you are amiable enough to find friends everywhere. The heart is a citizen of every country: how could a well-formed soul keep from forming attachments? I confess it to you: I honor old friendships, but I am not sorry to make new ones everywhere.

In whatever country I have been, I have lived there as if I were to spend my whole life there. I have had the same eagerness for virtuous people, the same compassion — or rather the same tenderness — for the unfortunate, the same esteem for those whom prosperity has not blinded. That is my character, Usbek: wherever I find human beings, I choose myself friends.

There is here a Guebre who, after you, holds, I think, the first place in my heart. He is probity itself. Particular reasons compelled him to withdraw to this city, where he lives quietly on the proceeds of an honorable trade with a woman he loves. His life is marked everywhere by generous actions; and although he seeks an obscure life, there is more heroism in his heart than in that of the greatest monarchs.

I have spoken to him of you a thousand times and show him all your letters. I notice that it gives him pleasure, and I can already see that you have a friend who does not know you.

You will find here the principal adventures of his life. However reluctant he was to write them, he could not refuse them to my friendship, and I confide them to yours.

**History of Apheridon and Astarte**

I was born among the Guebres, in a religion that is perhaps the oldest in the world. I was so unfortunate that love came to me before reason. I was scarcely six years old, and I could not live except with my sister. My eyes were always fixed on her; and when she left me for a moment, she found them bathed in tears when she returned. Each day increased my love no less than my age. My father, astonished by so strong a sympathy, would gladly have wished to marry us together according to the ancient custom of the Guebres introduced by Cambyses. But fear of the Mohammedans, under whose yoke we live, keeps those of our nation from thinking of those holy alliances, which our religion orders rather than merely permits, and which are such artless images of the union already formed by nature.

My father therefore, seeing how dangerous it would be to follow both my inclination and his own, resolved to extinguish a flame he thought newly kindled but which was already at its final stage. He used a journey as a pretext and took me away with him, leaving my sister in the hands of one of her female relatives, for my mother had died two years before. I cannot tell you what despair that separation caused. I embraced my sister all bathed in tears; but I shed none myself, for grief had made me almost insensible. We arrived in Tiflis; and my father, having entrusted my education to one of our kinsmen, left me there and went back home.

Some time later I learned that, through the influence of one of his friends, he had had my sister admitted to the king's harem, where she served a sultana. Had I learned of her death, I would not have been more struck. Not only had I no hope of seeing her again, but her entry into the harem had made her a Mohammedan, and according to the prejudice of that religion she could no longer look on me except with horror. Yet, unable any longer to live in Tiflis, weary of myself and of life, I returned to Isfahan. My first words to my father were bitter. I reproached him for having placed his daughter in a place one can enter only by changing religion. You have drawn down upon your family, I said to him, the anger of God and of the Sun that enlightens you. You have done more than if you had defiled the Elements, since you have defiled the soul of your daughter, which is no less pure. I shall die of grief and love; but may my death be the only punishment God makes you feel. With those words I went out; and for two years I spent my life going to look at the walls of the harem and consider the place where my sister might be, exposing myself every day a thousand times over to being slaughtered by the eunuchs who patrol around those dreadful places.

At last my father died; and the sultana whom my sister served, seeing her grow more beautiful every day, became jealous and married her to a eunuch who desired her passionately. By that means my sister left the seraglio and took up a house in Isfahan with her eunuch.

For more than three months I could not speak to her, the eunuch, the most jealous of men, always putting me off under one pretext or another. At last I entered his harem, and he had her speak to me through a lattice. The eyes of a lynx could not have discovered her, so wrapped was she in garments and veils; I recognized her only by the sound of her voice. What emotion seized me when I found myself so near and yet so far from her! I restrained myself, for I was being watched. As for her, it seemed to me that she shed a few tears. Her husband tried to make some poor excuses, but I treated him like the lowest of slaves. He was deeply embarrassed when he saw that I was speaking to my sister in a language unknown to him: it was ancient Persian, our sacred tongue. What! my sister, I said to her, is it true that you have abandoned the religion of your fathers? I know that in entering the harem you had to profess Mohammedanism; but tell me, could your heart consent, as your mouth did, to abandon a religion that permits me to love you? And for whom do you abandon that religion, which ought to be so dear to us? For a wretch still branded by the chains he has worn, who, if he were a man, would be the lowest of all! My brother, she said, the man you speak of is my husband; I must honor him, unworthy as he seems to you; and I would be the lowest of women if... Ah, my sister, I said, you are a Guebre; he is not your husband, nor can he be. If you are faithful like your fathers, you must look on him only as a monster. Alas, she said, how distant that religion now seems to me! I had scarcely learned its precepts before I had to forget them. You can hear that the language I speak to you is no longer familiar to me, and that I have all the difficulty in the world expressing myself. But know that the memory of our childhood still enchants me; that since that time I have had only false joys; that not a day has passed in which I have not thought of you; that you had more share than you think in my marriage, and that I consented to it only in the hope of seeing you again. But that day, which has already cost me so much, will cost me still more. I see you beside yourself; my husband trembles with rage and jealousy; I shall see you no more; I am doubtless speaking to you for the last time in my life; and if that is so, my brother, it will not be a long life. At these words she softened, and finding herself unable to continue the conversation, she left me the most desolate of men.

Three or four days later I asked to see my sister again. The barbarous eunuch would gladly have prevented it; but, besides the fact that husbands of that sort do not have the same authority over their wives as other men do, he loved my sister so madly that he could refuse her nothing. I saw her once more in the same place and the same attire, accompanied by two slaves, which made me return to our private language. My sister, I said to her, why is it that I cannot see you without finding myself in a dreadful condition? The walls that keep you enclosed, those bolts and gratings, those miserable guards watching you, drive me to fury. How have you lost the sweet freedom your ancestors enjoyed? Your mother, who was so chaste, gave her husband no guarantee of her virtue except virtue itself. They lived happily in mutual trust; and the simplicity of their manners was a treasure to them a thousand times more precious than the false splendor you seem to enjoy in this sumptuous house. In losing your religion, you have lost your liberty, your happiness, and that precious equality which is the honor of your sex. But what is worse still is that you are not a wife, for you cannot be that, but the slave of a slave who has been degraded from humanity. Ah, my brother, she said, respect my husband, respect the religion I have embraced: according to that religion, I could not hear you or speak to you without sin. What! my sister, I said in agitation, do you then believe that religion to be true? Ah, she said, how advantageous it would be for me if it were not! I make too great a sacrifice for it not to believe it; and if my doubts... At those words she fell silent. Yes, your doubts, my sister, are well founded, whatever they may be. What can you expect from a religion that makes you unhappy in this world and leaves you no hope in the next? Consider that ours is the oldest in the world; that it has always flourished in Persia and has no origin other than that empire itself, whose beginnings are unknown; that Mohammedanism was introduced there only by chance; that that sect was established there not by persuasion but by conquest. If our native princes had not been weak, you would still see the worship of those ancient Magi reigning there. Transport yourself into those distant ages: everything will speak to you of Magism, and nothing of the Mohammedan sect, which many thousands of years later was not even in its infancy. But, she said, even if my religion were more modern than yours, it is at least purer, since it worships only God; whereas you still worship the Sun, the stars, fire, and even the elements. I see, my sister, that among the Muslims you have learned to slander our holy religion. We worship neither the heavenly bodies nor the elements, and our fathers never worshipped them. They never raised temples to them and never offered them sacrifices; they rendered them only a religious but subordinate reverence, as works and manifestations of the Divinity. But, my sister, in the name of the God who gives us light, receive this sacred book I bring you. It is the book of our lawgiver Zoroaster. Read it without prejudice; receive into your heart the rays of light that will shine on you as you read it; remember your fathers, who for so long honored the Sun in the holy city of Balkh; and finally remember me, who hope for peace, fortune, and life only from your change. I left her in transports and let her alone decide the greatest affair I could have in my life.

I returned two days later; I said nothing; I waited in silence for the sentence of my life or my death. You are loved, my brother, she said to me, and by a Guebre. I struggled for a long time: but, gods, how many difficulties love removes! How relieved I am! I no longer fear loving you too much; I can place no limits on my love, and its very excess is lawful. Ah, how well that suits the state of my heart! But you, who knew how to break the chains my mind had forged for itself, when will you break the ones that bind my hands? From this moment I give myself to you: show by the speed with which you accept me how dear that gift is to you. My brother, the first time I can embrace you, I think I shall die in your arms. I could never fully express the joy I felt at those sweet words. In a moment I believed myself, and truly saw myself, the happiest of men. I saw almost fulfilled all the desires I had formed in twenty-five years of life, and all the sorrows that had made it so painful melt away. But when I had grown somewhat accustomed to those sweet ideas, I found that I was not so near happiness as I had suddenly imagined, though I had overcome the greatest of all obstacles. We had to surprise the vigilance of her guards. I dared confide the secret of my life to no one: she and I would have to do everything ourselves. If I failed, I risked being impaled; but I saw no punishment crueler than failing. We agreed that she would send to ask me for a clock her father had left her, and that I would put inside it a file for sawing through the lattice of her window that looked onto the street, and a knotted rope for descending; that I would no longer see her from then on, but would go every night beneath that window and wait until she could carry out her plan. I spent fifteen whole nights without seeing anyone, because she had not found a favorable moment. At last, on the sixteenth, I heard a saw at work; from time to time the labor was interrupted, and in those intervals my fear was indescribable. Finally, after an hour of work, I saw her fastening the rope; she lowered herself and slid into my arms. I no longer felt the danger, and for a long while I remained there without moving. I led her outside the city, where I had a horse ready; I set her behind me on the croup, and we fled with all imaginable speed from a place that could have been so fatal to us. Before daybreak we arrived at the house of a Guebre who had retired to a lonely spot, living frugally by the work of his own hands. We did not think it prudent to stay with him; and on his advice we entered a thick forest and hid ourselves in the hollow of an old oak until the noise of our escape had died away. We lived together in that remote retreat, with no witnesses, telling each other endlessly that we would always love one another, waiting until some Guebre priest could perform the marriage ceremony prescribed by our sacred books. My sister, I said to her, how holy this union is! Nature had already united us; our holy law will unite us again. At last a priest came to calm our loving impatience. In the peasant's house he performed all the marriage rites; he blessed us and wished us a thousand times the vigor of Gustaspes and the holiness of Hohoraspes. Soon afterward we left Persia, where we were not safe, and withdrew into Georgia. We lived there for a year, each day more charmed with one another. But as my money was running out, and as I feared poverty for my sister — not for myself — I left her to seek some help from our relatives. Never was a farewell more tender. But my journey proved not only useless but disastrous: finding on one side all our goods confiscated, and on the other our relatives almost unable to help me, I brought back only exactly enough money for my return.

But what despair awaited me! I no longer found my sister. A few days before my arrival, Tartars had made an incursion into the town where she was; finding her beautiful, they carried her off and sold her to Jews traveling into Turkey, leaving behind only a little daughter she had given birth to some months earlier. I followed those Jews and caught up with them three leagues away. My prayers and tears were useless. They kept demanding thirty tomans and would not reduce the sum by a single coin. After appealing to everyone and imploring the protection of Turkish and Christian priests, I turned to an Armenian merchant. I sold him my daughter, and I sold myself as well, for thirty-five tomans. I went to the Jews, gave them their thirty tomans, and brought the other five to my sister, whom I had not yet seen. You are free, I said to her, my sister, and I can embrace you. Here are five tomans I bring you; I regret that they did not buy me for more. What! she said, you sold yourself? Yes, I told her. Ah, wretched man, what have you done? Was I not unfortunate enough without your making me still more so? Your freedom comforted me, and your slavery will put me in the grave. Ah, my brother, how cruel your love is! And my daughter? I do not see her. I sold her too, I said to her. We both burst into tears, and neither of us had the strength to say another word. At last I went to find my master, and my sister arrived there almost as soon as I did. She threw herself at his knees. I ask you, she said, for slavery as others ask you for freedom: take me; you will sell me for more than my husband. Then there took place a struggle that drew tears from my master's eyes. Unhappy man, she said, did you think I could accept my freedom at the cost of yours? Sir, you see before you two unfortunates who will die if you separate them. I give myself to you; pay for me; perhaps that money and my services may one day obtain from you what I do not dare ask. It is in your interest not to separate us; count on it, I hold his life in my hands. The Armenian was a gentle man, touched by our misfortunes. Serve me both faithfully and zealously, he said, and I promise that in a year I will give you your freedom. I see that neither of you deserves the unhappiness of your condition. If, when you are free, you are as happy as you deserve to be, if fortune smiles on you, I am certain you will make good the loss I suffer. We both embraced his knees and followed him on his journey. We relieved one another in the labors of slavery, and I was delighted whenever I could do the work that had fallen to my sister.

At the end of the year our master kept his word and freed us. We returned to Tiflis. There I found an old friend of my father's who practiced medicine successfully in that city; he lent me some money, with which I entered into trade. Some affairs then called me to Smyrna, where I settled. I have lived there for six years, and I enjoy the sweetest and most lovable society in the world. Union reigns in my family, and I would not exchange my condition for that of all the kings of the world. I have been fortunate enough to find again the Armenian merchant to whom I owe everything, and I have rendered him signal services.

*At Smyrna, the 27th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the full arc of the inset narrative effectively: childhood attachment, religious and political separation, reunion through clandestine speech, theological persuasion, escape, remarriage, renewed catastrophe, voluntary enslavement, and final restoration.

### Main differences
- **Nature and religious law**
  - French: `des images si naïves de l'union déjà formée par la nature`
  - Fresh translation: `such artless images of the union already formed by nature`
  - Davidson: `such innocent reflections of a union already formed by nature`
  - Davidson is close, but `reflections` is a little more abstract than the French image language.

- **False joys**
  - French: `je n'ai eu que de fausses joies`
  - Fresh translation: `I have had only false joys`
  - Davidson: `I have known only the mockery of happiness`
  - Davidson turns the phrase into a more elevated paraphrase.

- **Religion that permits love**
  - French: `une religion qui me permet de vous aimer`
  - Fresh translation keeps the shocking directness of `permits me to love you`.
  - Davidson is also close here, but the fresh translation keeps more of the unsoftened scandal of the line.

- **Slave of a slave**
  - French: `l'esclave d'un esclave`
  - Fresh translation: `the slave of a slave`
  - Davidson: the same phrase, but elsewhere softens the surrounding syntax.
  - This is one of the letter's crucial formulas, and it shows how blunt Montesquieu is when Apheridon attacks both harem marriage and religious conversion.

- **Subordinate reverence**
  - French: `un culte religieux, mais inférieur`
  - Fresh translation: `a religious but subordinate reverence`
  - Davidson: `such inferior reverence as is due`
  - Davidson is good here, though slightly more explanatory.

- **`De votre changement`**
  - Fresh translation: `from your change`
  - Davidson: `depend upon your conversion`
  - `Conversion` is interpretively clear, but the French is broader and somewhat more open-ended in wording.

- **Emotional register in the ending**
  - Davidson often heightens phrases into a more romantic Victorian diction: `mockery of happiness`, `A load has fallen from me`, `ye Gods!`, and similar expansions.
  - The fresh translation aims to keep the intensity while staying closer to Montesquieu's plainer rhetorical movement.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: passionate, argumentative, theological, and melodramatic all at once; it moves between confession, polemic, and romance.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve both the directness of the incest narrative and the argumentative precision of the religious debates.
- **Davidson**: often powerful and readable, but more ornamented in register and more Victorian in emotional coloring.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXVII is one of the most complex comparison cases in this range. Davidson preserves the narrative sequence and much of the force, but the fresh translation keeps closer to the French letter's mixture of scandalous directness, doctrinal argument, and comparatively plain narrative pressure.
