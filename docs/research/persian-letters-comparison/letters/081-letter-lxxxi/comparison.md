## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXXI**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
Since I have been in Europe, my dear Rhedi, I have seen many governments: it is not as in Asia, where the rules of politics are everywhere the same.

I have often thought to myself, to determine which of all governments was the most conformable to reason. It seemed to me that the most perfect is the one that reaches its end at the least cost; and thus the government that guides men in the way most suited to their bent and inclination is the most perfect.

If, in a mild government, the people are as submissive as in a severe one, the first is preferable, since it is more conformable to reason, and severity is a motive extraneous to it.

Take it from me, my dear Rhedi: in a state, punishments more or less cruel do not make people obey the laws more. In countries where punishments are moderate, they are feared as much as in those where they are tyrannical and dreadful.

Whether a government is mild or cruel, one always punishes by degrees; a greater or lesser punishment is inflicted for a greater or lesser crime. The imagination adapts itself to the customs of the country where one lives: eight days in prison, or a slight fine, strike the mind of a European brought up in a gentle country as much as the loss of an arm intimidates an Asian. They attach a certain degree of fear to a certain degree of punishment, and each person experiences it in his own way: the despair of infamy comes to torment a Frenchman who has just been condemned to a punishment that would not cost a Turk a quarter of an hour's sleep.

Besides, I do not see police, justice, and equity better observed in Turkey, in Persia, or under the Mogul than in the republics of Holland and Venice, and even in England; I do not see fewer crimes committed there, or men, frightened by the greatness of punishments, more submissive to the laws.

On the contrary, I notice in the midst of those very states a source of injustice and vexation.

I even find the prince, who is the law itself, less master there than anywhere else.

I see that, in those rigorous moments, there are always tumultuous movements in which no one is the leader; and that, once violent authority is scorned, no one retains enough power to bring it back;

That the very despair born of impunity confirms disorder, and makes it greater;

That, in those states, no little revolt forms, and there is never any interval between murmuring and sedition;

That great events there do not have to be prepared by great causes; on the contrary, the slightest accident produces a great revolution, often as unforeseen by those who make it as by those who suffer it.

When Osman, emperor of the Turks, was deposed, none of those who committed that outrage had thought of committing it; they were only asking, as suppliants, that justice be done for some grievance: a voice, never identified, came by chance out of the crowd; the name of Mustapha was pronounced, and immediately Mustapha was emperor.

*Paris, the 2nd day of the moon of Rebiab I, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the political reasoning of the letter closely. He preserves the central contrast between mild and severe governments, the relativity of punishment to local custom, and the claim that violent authority often produces instability rather than obedience. Most differences are matters of diction, though one difficult phrase seems to have been regularized rather than translated closely.

### Main differences
- **`à moins de frais`**
  - Fresh translation: `at the least cost`
  - Davidson: `with the least friction`
  - Davidson captures efficiency, but the French speaks more generally of expense or cost than of mechanical friction.

- **Severity as foreign motive**
  - French: `la sévérité est un motif étranger`
  - Fresh translation: `severity is a motive extraneous to it`
  - Davidson: `severity being a motive foreign to reason`
  - Davidson interprets the sentence well, though he makes explicit what the French leaves slightly more compact.

- **The moderate penalty example**
  - French: `une légère amende`
  - Fresh translation: `a slight fine`
  - Davidson: `a lighter punishment`
  - Davidson generalizes where the French gives a specific civic example.

- **Infamy and sleep**
  - French: `Le désespoir de l'infamie vient désoler un François`
  - Fresh translation keeps the emphasis on the despair of disgrace itself.
  - Davidson's `would overwhelm a Frenchman with infamy and despair` is strong, but a little more expansive than the original.

- **A difficult phrase regularized**
  - French: `Que le désespoir même de l'impunité confirme le désordre`
  - The phrase is syntactically difficult and may invite different readings.
  - Davidson renders it as `That the certainty of punishment itself strengthens and increases the disorder`, which is plausible as interpretation but not especially close to the wording.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: lucid political analysis with sudden compressed maxims.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the argumentative sequence plain, firm, and slightly aphoristic.
- **Davidson**: intelligent and readable, though more explanatory in spots where the French is terse.
