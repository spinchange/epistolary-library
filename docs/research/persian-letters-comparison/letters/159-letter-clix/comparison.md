## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CLIX**

**Solim to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
I complain, magnificent lord, and I pity you: never has a faithful servant sunk into the dreadful despair in which I am. Here are your misfortunes and mine; I write them to you only with trembling.

I swear, by all the prophets of heaven, that since you entrusted your wives to me I have watched over them night and day; that I have never for a single moment interrupted the course of my anxieties. I began my charge with punishments; and I suspended them without departing from my natural austerity.

But what am I saying? Why boast here of a fidelity that has been useless to you? Forget all my past services; look on me as a traitor; and punish me for all the crimes I could not prevent. Roxane, proud Roxane — O heaven! whom can one trust now? You suspected Zachi, and you were entirely secure about Roxane; but her fierce virtue was a cruel imposture; it was the veil of her perfidy. I surprised her in the arms of a young man who, as soon as he saw himself discovered, rushed at me; he struck me twice with a dagger; the eunuchs, running at the noise, surrounded him; he defended himself for a long time and wounded several of them; he even wanted to go back into the room, to die there, he said, before Roxane's eyes. But at last he yielded to numbers, and he fell at our feet.

I do not know, sublime lord, whether I shall wait for your severe orders: you have placed your vengeance in my hands; I must not let it languish.

*From the seraglio in Ispahan, the 8th day of the moon of Rebiab 1, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the main dramatic action of the letter: Solim's self-accusation, the revelation of Roxane's affair, the fight with the young man, and the movement toward immediate vengeance. The overall narrative remains clear and forceful.

### Main differences
- **Complaining and pitying**
  - French: `Je me plains ... et je te plains`
  - Fresh translation: `I complain ... and I pity you`
  - Davidson: `I lament for myself, and I lament for you`
  - Davidson makes both clauses parallel, but the French shifts from self-complaint to pity for Usbek.

- **Office and punishment**
  - French: `J'ai commencé mon ministère par les châtiments`
  - Fresh translation: `I began my charge with punishments`
  - Davidson: `When I assumed office I commenced with chastisement`
  - Davidson is close, though a little more administrative and formal.

- **The crucial name discrepancy**
  - French: `Tu soupçonnois Zachi`
  - Fresh translation: `You suspected Zachi`
  - Davidson: `You suspected Zelis`
  - This is a substantive discrepancy, not a mere stylistic variation. It should be checked against the raw scan, but the current clean French and English do not agree.

- **Attack sequence**
  - French: `dès qu'il s'est vu découvert, est venu sur moi`
  - Fresh translation: `as soon as he saw himself discovered, rushed at me`
  - Davidson: `when he saw himself discovered, ran at me`
  - Davidson is close here.

- **Final vengeance formula**
  - French: `je ne dois pas la faire languir`
  - Fresh translation: `I must not let it languish`
  - Davidson: `I ought not to defer it`
  - Davidson gives the sense, but the French has a more physical image of vengeance not being left to drag on.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: formal, panicked, and self-justifying; Solim writes as both servant and executioner.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the escalating panic while preserving the ceremonial rhetoric of address.
- **Davidson**: strong in narrative continuity, but a little smoother in places where the French is more abrupt or rhetorically uneven.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The clean English text has a visible line break disruption around `in the presence of / Roxana`, but the sentence is still recoverable.
- There is also a date discrepancy between the clean French text, which shows `1730`, and the clean English text, which shows `1720`; in sequence, `1720` is almost certainly the intended reading, so the French clean text likely preserves an OCR or transcription error.

## Working conclusion
Letter CLIX is broadly faithful in Davidson, but two issues stand out:
- a real name discrepancy: `Zachi` versus `Zelis`
- a probable date problem in the French clean text

Apart from those, Davidson tracks the letter closely, with mostly minor smoothing of rhetoric and imagery.
