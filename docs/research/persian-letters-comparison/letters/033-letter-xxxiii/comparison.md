## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXXIII**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
Wine is so expensive in Paris, because of the taxes laid upon it, that one would think they had undertaken to enforce there the precepts of the divine Koran, which forbids drinking it.

When I think of the disastrous effects of that liquor, I cannot keep myself from regarding it as the most dreadful gift nature has given to men. If anything has blighted the life and reputation of our monarchs, it has been their intemperance. It is the most poisoned source of their injustices and their cruelties.

I say it to the shame of men: the law forbids our princes the use of wine, and they drink it with an excess that degrades them beneath humanity itself. This use, on the contrary, is permitted to Christian princes, and one does not observe that it leads them into any fault. The human mind is contradiction itself. In a lawless debauch one rebels furiously against precepts; and the law made to render us more just often serves only to make us more guilty.

But when I disapprove of the use of this liquor, which makes one lose one's reason, I do not equally condemn those drinks that cheer it. It is the wisdom of the Orientals to seek remedies against sadness with as much care as against the most dangerous illnesses. When some misfortune happens to a European, he has no resource but the reading of a philosopher called Seneca. But Asians, more sensible than they and better physicians in this respect, take drinks capable of making a man cheerful and charming away the memory of his sorrows.

There is nothing so afflicting as consolations drawn from the necessity of evil, the uselessness of remedies, the fatality of destiny, the order of Providence, and the misery of the human condition. It is mockery to want to soften an ill by considering that one was born miserable. It is much better to draw the mind away from its reflections and to treat man as a feeling being instead of treating him as a rational one.

The soul, united with the body, is ceaselessly tyrannized by it. If the movement of the blood is too slow, if the spirits are not sufficiently refined, if they are not in sufficient quantity, we fall into dejection and sadness. But if we take drinks that can alter this disposition of our body, our soul again becomes capable of receiving impressions that cheer it, and it feels a secret pleasure in seeing its machine recover, so to speak, its motion and its life.

*From Paris, the 25th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the argument of the letter closely: the condemnation of wine, the comparison between Muslim and Christian princes, the defense of milder exhilarants, and the physiological theory of melancholy. His rendering is strong overall, though he sometimes domesticates the language or sharpens it in ways the French does not quite require.

### Main differences
- **Wine, not `strong drink`**
  - French: `le vin`
  - Fresh translation: `wine`
  - Davidson: `strong drink`
  - Davidson generalizes where the French is specific.

- **Blighted life and reputation**
  - French: `flétri la vie et la réputation`
  - Fresh translation keeps the paired damage to life and reputation.
  - Davidson's `soiled the lives and the good fame` is close, though a little more elevated.

- **Degraded beneath humanity itself**
  - French: `les dégrade de l'humanité même`
  - Fresh translation keeps the direct formulation.
  - Davidson's `degrades them beneath the lowest of mankind` is forceful, but it intensifies and rephrases the line.

- **Drinks that cheer reason**
  - French: `ces boissons qui l'égayent`
  - Fresh translation: `those drinks that cheer it`
  - Davidson: `those beverages which exhilarate the mind`
  - Davidson is elegant, though a little more abstract and Latinate.

- **Treat man as feeling rather than reasoning**
  - French: `traiter l'homme comme sensible, au lieu de le traiter comme raisonnable`
  - Fresh translation preserves the contrast.
  - Davidson's `a being capable of feeling, and not as a mere reasoner` is a good equivalent, but slightly fuller than the French.

- **The soul tyrannized by the body**
  - French: `en est sans cesse tyrannisée`
  - Fresh translation: `is ceaselessly tyrannized by it`
  - Davidson: `is a slave under a tyrant`
  - Davidson is vivid, but he metaphorically amplifies a line that is already strong in the original.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: moralizing, medical, and unexpectedly practical in its theory of melancholy.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep both the severity of the anti-wine argument and the calm confidence of the physiological explanation.
- **Davidson**: generally faithful and often effective, but somewhat more rhetorical in spots.
