**Letter CIX**

**Usbek to [***]**

### Translation
There is a kind of book unknown to us in Persia, and very fashionable here: journals. Idleness feels flattered by reading them: one is delighted to be able to skim thirty volumes in a quarter of an hour.

In most books, before the author has finished the usual compliments, readers are already at their wits' end: he ushers them half dead into a subject drowned in a sea of words. One man wants to immortalize himself in a duodecimo, another in a quarto; another, whose aspirations are loftier, aims at a folio; so he must stretch out his subject in proportion. He does so without mercy, counting for nothing the trouble of the poor reader, who exhausts himself reducing what the author took such pains to amplify.

I do not know, ***, what merit there is in composing such works: I could do as much myself, if I wanted to ruin my health and a bookseller.

The great fault of journalists is that they speak only of new books: as if truth were ever new. It seems to me that, until a man has read all the ancient books, he has no reason to prefer the new ones.

But when they impose on themselves the law of speaking only of works still hot from the forge, they impose another on themselves as well: that of being very tedious. They are careful not to criticize the books they excerpt, whatever reason they might have to do so; and indeed, what man is bold enough to want to make ten or twelve enemies every month?

Most authors resemble poets, who will endure a shower of blows from a stick without complaint, but who, little jealous of their shoulders, are so jealous of their works that they cannot bear the slightest criticism. One must therefore take great care not to attack them in so sensitive a place, and journalists know this very well. So they do exactly the opposite: they begin by praising the subject under discussion - first insipidity. From there they pass to praising the author - forced praise, since they are dealing with people still full of combativeness, always ready to demand satisfaction and to strike down a rash journalist with strokes of the pen.

*From Paris, the 5th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the basic satirical line very well: journals flatter lazy readers, reward swollen writing, and force reviewers into a regime of mutual praise because authors are vain and vindictive.

### Main differences
- **Idleness and speed-reading**
  - French: `La paresse se sent flattée`
  - Fresh translation keeps the idea that idleness itself is being complimented by journals.
  - Davidson is close here.

- **Sea of words**
  - French: `une matière noyée au milieu d'une mer de paroles`
  - Fresh translation preserves the hyperbolic image of the subject drowned in verbiage.
  - Davidson also keeps the image well.

- **Bookseller vs publisher**
  - French: `ruiner ma santé et un libraire`
  - Fresh translation keeps the more exact `bookseller`.
  - Davidson's `publisher` is acceptable, but less literal.

- **Fresh from the forge**
  - French: `ouvrages encore tout chauds de la forge`
  - Fresh translation keeps the forge metaphor.
  - Davidson turns this into `hot from the press`, which is idiomatic but loses the French image.

- **Demanding satisfaction**
  - French: `tout prêts à se faire faire raison`
  - Fresh translation preserves the sense of authors poised to demand redress or satisfaction.
  - Davidson's phrasing is more generalized and loses some of the dueling or quarrelsome flavor.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dry, malicious, and elegantly compressed.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the mockery of literary vanity sharp without over-modernizing it.
- **Davidson**: lively and often close, though a little more polished and less pointed in a few images.

## Working conclusion
Davidson is broadly faithful in Letter CIX. The main gains from a fresh translation lie in preserving Montesquieu's exact metaphors - especially the `forge`, the `bookseller`, and the belligerent sensitivity of authors under review.
