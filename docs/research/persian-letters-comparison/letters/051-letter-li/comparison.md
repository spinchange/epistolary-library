## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LI**

**Nargum, Persian envoy in Muscovy, to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
I have been written from Isfahan that you have left Persia and are now in Paris. Why must I learn news of you from others rather than from yourself?

The orders of the king of kings have kept me for five years in this country, where I have concluded several important negotiations. You know that the czar is the only Christian prince whose interests are mixed with those of Persia, because, like us, he is the enemy of the Turks.

His empire is larger than ours, for from Moscow to the furthest post of his states on the Chinese side they count two thousand leagues. He is absolute master of the life and goods of his subjects, who are all slaves except for four families. The lieutenant of the prophets, the king of kings, who has heaven for a footstool, does not exercise his power in a more formidable way.

To see the dreadful climate of Muscovy, one would never believe exile from it could be a punishment; yet as soon as a great man falls into disgrace, he is relegated to Siberia. As the law of our Prophet forbids us wine, the law of their prince forbids it to the Muscovites.

They have a way of receiving guests that is not at all Persian. As soon as a stranger enters a house, the husband presents his wife to him; the stranger kisses her, and this passes for a politeness done to the husband.

Although fathers usually stipulate in their daughters' marriage contracts that the husband shall not whip them, one can hardly believe how much Muscovite women like to be beaten. They cannot understand that they possess their husband's heart if he does not beat them properly; the opposite conduct is, in their eyes, an unforgivable sign of indifference. Here is a letter that one of them wrote recently to her mother:

My dear mother, I am the unhappiest woman in the world. I have done everything to make my husband love me, and I have never succeeded. Yesterday I had a thousand things to do in the house; I went out and stayed away the whole day. On my return I thought he would beat me soundly; but he did not say a single word. My sister is treated quite differently: her husband thrashes her every day; she cannot so much as look at a man without his knocking her down on the spot. They love one another dearly too, and live in the best understanding in the world. That is what makes her so proud; but I will not let her despise me much longer. I have resolved to make my husband love me, whatever it costs. I will anger him so well that he will have to give me marks of affection. It shall not be said that I am not beaten and that I live in the house without anyone thinking of me. At the least flick he gives me, I will cry out with all my strength, so that people think it is serious; and if some neighbor came to my rescue, I believe I would strangle him. I beg you, my dear mother, kindly to represent to my husband that he treats me in a shameful way. My father, who was such an honest man, did not behave like that; and I remember that, when I was a little girl, it sometimes seemed to me that he loved you too much. I embrace you, my dear mother.

Muscovites are not allowed to leave the empire, even for travel. Thus, separated from other nations by the laws of the country, they have kept their ancient customs with all the more attachment because they never thought it possible to have others.

But the prince who now reigns has wanted to change everything. He has had great quarrels with them over their beards; the clergy and monks have fought no less stubbornly on behalf of their ignorance. He is intent on making the arts flourish, and neglects nothing that might carry the glory of his nation, until now forgotten and almost known only to itself, through Europe and Asia. Restless and perpetually agitated, he wanders through his vast states, leaving everywhere marks of his natural severity. He quits them as though they could not contain him, and goes to seek in Europe other provinces and new kingdoms.

I embrace you, my dear Usbek: I beg you to send me news of yourself.

*From Moscow, the 2nd day of the moon of Chalval, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the diplomatic overview of Muscovy, the comic ethnography of hospitality and wife-beating, and the brief portrait of Peter the Great. The letter's structure and political emphasis remain clear.

### Main differences
- **Distance across the empire**
  - French: `deux mille lieues`
  - Fresh translation: `two thousand leagues`
  - Davidson: `a thousand leagues`
  - This is a substantive discrepancy, not just stylistic variation.
- **Mixed interests**
  - French: `les intérêts soient mêlés`
  - Fresh translation: `interests are mixed with those of Persia`
  - Davidson's `allied` is smoother, but less exact.
- **Marks of affection**
  - In the embedded letter, French keeps the grotesque logic of beating as proof of love.
  - Davidson captures the comic point, though with somewhat more polished phrasing.
- **Natural severity**
  - French: `sa sévérité naturelle`
  - Fresh translation keeps the phrase sharp.
  - Davidson's `savage nature` intensifies it beyond the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: diplomatic in frame, satirical in anecdote, and sharp in its portrait of autocracy.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the shifts between political report and absurd social custom.
- **Davidson**: highly readable, but with a few stronger interpretive pushes than the French requires.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text preserves the whole letter but includes footnote debris and pagination fragments.

## Working conclusion
Letter LI is broadly well served by Davidson, but it includes at least one notable factual divergence (`two thousand` vs `a thousand`) and a few interpretive sharpenings. The fresh translation keeps the ethnographic satire slightly straighter.
