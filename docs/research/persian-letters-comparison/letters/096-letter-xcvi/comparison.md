## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCVI**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
Magistrates must render justice from citizen to citizen; each people must render it for itself from one people to another. In this second distribution of justice, one cannot employ other maxims than in the first.

From people to people there is rarely any need of a third party to judge, because the subjects of dispute are almost always clear and easy to settle. The interests of two nations are ordinarily so separate that one need only love justice to find it; one can hardly be prejudiced in one's own cause.

It is not the same with disputes between private persons. Since they live in society, their interests are so mingled and confused, and there are so many different kinds of them, that a third party is needed to disentangle what the parties' greed tries to obscure.

There are only two sorts of just wars: those waged to repel an enemy who attacks, and those undertaken to aid an ally who is attacked.

It would not be just to make war for a prince's private quarrels, unless the case were grave enough to deserve the death of the prince or of the people who committed the offense. Thus a prince cannot make war because some honor due to him has been refused, or because there has been some discourteous proceeding toward his ambassadors, and other such things, any more than a private person can kill someone who refuses him precedence. The reason is that, since a declaration of war must be an act of justice in which the penalty must always be proportioned to the fault, one must see whether the party on whom war is declared deserves death. For to make war on someone is to wish to punish him with death.

In public law, the most severe act of justice is war, since its end is the destruction of society.

Reprisals are of the second degree. It is a law that tribunals themselves have not been able to avoid: measuring punishment by crime.

A third act of justice is to deprive a prince of the advantages he can derive from us, always proportioning the penalty to the offense.

The fourth act of justice, and the one that ought to be most frequent, is renouncing the alliance of the people of whom one has cause to complain. That punishment answers to banishment in the tribunals, which cuts the guilty off from society. Thus a prince whose alliance we renounce is thereby cut off from our society, and is no longer one of our members.

No greater insult can be offered a prince than to renounce his alliance, and no greater honor than to contract it. Among men there is nothing more glorious, and indeed more useful, than to see others always attentive to our preservation.

But for alliance truly to bind us, it must be just. Thus an alliance made by two nations in order to oppress a third is illegitimate, and one may violate it without crime.

It is not even consistent with a prince's honor and dignity to ally himself with a tyrant. It is said that a monarch of Egypt had the king of Samos warned about his cruelty and tyranny, and summoned him to correct himself; when he did not, the Egyptian king sent word that he renounced his friendship and alliance.

Conquest gives no right by itself. When the people continues to exist, it is a pledge for peace and for reparation of the wrong; and if the people is destroyed or scattered, it is the monument of a tyranny.

Treaties of peace are so sacred among men that they seem to be the voice of nature reclaiming her rights. They are all legitimate when their conditions are such that both peoples can preserve themselves; otherwise, the one of the two societies that must perish, deprived by peace of its natural defense, may seek that defense in war.

For nature, which established different degrees of strength and weakness among men, has also often made weakness equal to strength through despair.

*Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1716.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the whole argument of the letter: international justice should follow the same proportional reasoning as domestic justice, and war is legitimate only under narrow conditions.

### Main differences
- **`de peuple à peuple`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the repeated `people to people` structure because the French insists on an analogy between bodies politic and private persons.
  - Davidson's `nation and nation` is natural, but a shade more modern and state-centered.

- **War as death penalty**
  - French: `faire la guerre à quelqu'un, c'est vouloir le punir de mort`
  - Fresh translation: `to make war on someone is to wish to punish him with death`
  - Davidson is close, but the French lands with especially stark juridical severity.

- **Alliance and banishment**
  - French compares renouncing alliance to banishment with unusual literalness.
  - Davidson preserves the point, though the fresh draft keeps the wording tighter and more institutional.

- **Extra closing sentence in Davidson**
  - The English clean text ends with `This, my dear Rhedi, is what I call international law...`
  - That line does not appear in the French clean witness here.
  - It should be checked against the raw excerpt before any final editorial use.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: systematic, legalistic, and severe.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the juridical analogy and sentence-by-sentence argument.
- **Davidson**: very serviceable, but slightly more discursive and modern in terminology.

## Working conclusion
Letter XCVI shows Davidson at his best in conveying argumentative structure, but it also shows the importance of checking the French for additions, modernizing terminology, and the exact legal force of key claims.
