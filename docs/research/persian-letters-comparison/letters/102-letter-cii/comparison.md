## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CII**

**Usbek to \*\*\***

### Translation
People here are always speaking of the constitution. The other day I entered a house where I first saw a fat man with a ruddy complexion saying in a loud voice: "I have issued my charge; I will not answer everything you say; but read it, this charge, and you will see that I have resolved all your doubts there. I had to sweat a great deal to produce it," he said, putting his hand to his forehead; "I needed all my learning, and I had to read a great many Latin authors."

"I believe it," said a man who happened to be there, "for it is a fine work; and I defy that Jesuit who comes to see you so often to make a better one."

"Well then, read it," he replied, "and in a quarter of an hour you will be better instructed on these matters than if I had talked to you for two hours."

That was how he avoided entering into conversation and exposing his self-importance. But when he saw himself pressed, he was forced to leave his entrenchments, and he began, in theological fashion, to utter a quantity of nonsense, backed by a dervish who returned it to him very respectfully. When two men who were there denied one of his principles, he said at once: "That is certain; we have judged it so, and we are infallible judges."

"And how," I said to him then, "are you infallible judges?"

"Do you not see," he replied, "that the Holy Spirit enlightens us?"

"That is fortunate," I answered him, "for from the way you have spoken all day, I can see that you are badly in need of enlightenment."

*Paris, the 18th day of the moon of Rebiab I, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the anticlerical joke effectively: a puffed-up churchman hides behind his published charge, invokes infallibility, and is punctured by Usbek's final retort.

### Main differences
- **`mandement`**
  - Davidson's `charge` is reasonable in ecclesiastical context.
  - The French remains more specifically about a formal pastoral or episcopal pronouncement, so some institutional nuance is lost.

- **`sa suffisance`**
  - Fresh translation: `self-importance`
  - Davidson: `incompetence`
  - The French targets conceit first. Incompetence is part of the joke, but it is not the named vice.

- **`dire théologiquement force sottises`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the adverbial irony: he begins, `in theological fashion`, to say a great many stupid things.
  - Davidson's `a mass of theological nonsense` is strong, but a little less pointed in structure.

- **Final rebuke**
  - Davidson is close in overall sense.
  - The fresh draft keeps the direct link between the man's whole day's speech and his supposed need for divine illumination.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brief, theatrical, and barbed.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the dialogue sharp and the satire on clerical pretension clear.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, with only a few interpretive shifts.

## Working conclusion
Davidson handles the scene well. The main value of the fresh draft is in restoring the satirical target from mere stupidity to pomposity backed by institutional claims to infallibility.
