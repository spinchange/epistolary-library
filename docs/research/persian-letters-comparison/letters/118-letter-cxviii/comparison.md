## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXVIII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
The prohibition of divorce is not the only cause of the depopulation of Christian countries: the great number of eunuchs they have among them is no less considerable a cause.

I mean priests and dervishes of both sexes, who vow themselves to an eternal continence: among Christians this is virtue par excellence. I do not understand them in this, since I do not know what to make of a virtue from which nothing results.

I find that their doctors plainly contradict themselves when they say that marriage is holy, and that celibacy, which is opposed to it, is holier still; without even considering that, in matters of precepts and fundamental dogmas, the good is always what is best.

The number of people making profession of celibacy is prodigious. Formerly fathers condemned their children to it from the cradle; today they devote themselves to it at the age of fourteen, which comes to nearly the same thing.

This trade of continence has destroyed more men than plagues and the bloodiest wars ever have. In every religious house one sees an eternal family, where no one is born, and which maintains itself at the expense of all the others. These houses are always open, like so many gulfs in which future generations are buried.

This policy is very different from that of the Romans, who established penal laws against those who refused the laws of marriage and wished to enjoy a liberty so contrary to the public good.

I speak here only of Catholic countries. In the Protestant religion everyone has the right to make children; it tolerates neither priests nor dervishes. And if, in the establishment of that religion, which brought everything back to the earliest times, its founders had not been accused constantly of intemperance, one cannot doubt that, after making the practice of marriage universal, they would have further softened its yoke and finished by removing every barrier that separates, in this matter, the Nazarene from Mohammed.

But however that may be, it is certain that religion gives Protestants an immense advantage over Catholics.

I dare say it: in the present condition of Europe, it is impossible for the Catholic religion to survive there for five hundred years.

Before the decline of Spanish power, Catholics were much stronger than Protestants. The latter have little by little arrived at an equilibrium, and today the balance is beginning to tip to their side. That superiority will increase every day: Protestants will become richer and more powerful, and Catholics weaker.

Protestant countries ought to be, and in fact are, more populated than Catholic ones. From this it follows, first, that their revenues are greater, because they increase in proportion to the number of those who pay them; second, that the land is better cultivated there; and finally, that commerce flourishes more, because more people there have fortunes to make, and with more needs there are more resources to meet them. When there are only enough people for the cultivation of the land, commerce must perish; and when there are only enough for the maintenance of commerce, cultivation of the land must fail. That is to say, both must collapse at the same time, because one is never pursued except at the expense of the other.

As for Catholic countries, not only is the cultivation of the land neglected there, but industry itself is pernicious: it consists only in learning five or six words of a dead language. As soon as a man has that stock on hand, he need no longer concern himself with his fortune: he finds in the cloister a tranquil life, which in the world would have cost him sweat and trouble.

That is not all. The dervishes hold in their hands almost all the wealth of the state. It is a society of avaricious people, always taking and never giving: they constantly accumulate revenues in order to acquire capital. So much wealth falls, so to speak, into paralysis; no circulation, no commerce, no arts, no manufactures.

There is no Protestant prince who raises from his people ten times more taxes than the pope draws from his subjects; and yet the latter are miserable, while the others live in opulence. Commerce gives life to everything among the first, and monasticism carries death everywhere among the second.

*Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson retains the broad anti-clerical thrust of the letter and its contrast between Protestant and Catholic Europe. He is generally faithful in sequence and argument, but several of his lexical choices soften Montesquieu's satire or replace his more cutting formulations with smoother abstractions.

### Main differences
- **`vertu par excellence`**
  - Fresh translation: `virtue par excellence`
  - Davidson: `the virtue of virtues`
  - Davidson makes the phrase more ornate. The French is emphatic, but less decorative.

- **`le bien est toujours le mieux`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the compact proverb-like force: in doctrine, the good is always what is best.
  - Davidson's `the expedient is always the best` shifts the statement toward practicality rather than moral-theological consistency.

- **`métier de continence`**
  - Fresh translation: `This trade of continence`
  - Davidson: `The practice of continence`
  - `Trade` preserves the mockery; `practice` is more neutral.

- **`famille éternelle, où il ne naît personne`**
  - Fresh translation: `an eternal family, where no one is born`
  - Davidson: `an unending family, where nobody is born`
  - Davidson is close, though the fresh version keeps the stronger paradoxical note of `eternal family`.

- **Tax ratio**
  - French: `dix fois plus d'impôts`
  - Fresh translation preserves `ten times more taxes`.
  - Davidson reduces this to `much heavier taxes`, losing a specific quantitative claim.

- **Monasticism vs celibacy**
  - French: `le monachisme porte la mort partout`
  - Fresh translation: `monasticism carries death everywhere`
  - Davidson substitutes `celibacy`, which broadens and alters the target of the sentence.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: aggressively polemical, anti-monastic, and openly predictive in its political theology.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the pamphlet-like energy and the sarcasm directed at clerical celibacy.
- **Davidson**: mostly faithful, but often a little less biting and less concrete.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is usable, but some minor OCR defects remain, including `bom` for `born` and occasional spacing artifacts.
- The larger comparative issues here are lexical and ideological rather than OCR-driven.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXVIII is important because Davidson's version is close enough to be useful, yet it repeatedly softens terms that matter: `trade of continence`, the explicit `ten times`, and `monasticism` rather than generic `celibacy`. Those distinctions shape the force of Montesquieu's anti-Catholic argument.
