## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXVI**

**Usbek to his friend Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The laws in Europe are furious against those who kill themselves: they are made to die, so to speak, a second time; they are dragged dishonorably through the streets; they are branded with infamy; their goods are confiscated.

It seems to me, Ibben, that these laws are very unjust. When I am crushed by grief, misery, and contempt, why does anyone wish to prevent me from ending my sufferings, and cruelly deprive me of a remedy that lies in my own hands?

Why should anyone want me to labor for a society to which I consent no longer to belong? Why should I, against my will, remain bound by an agreement made without me? Society is founded on mutual advantage; but when it becomes burdensome to me, what prevents me from renouncing it? Life was given to me as a favor; I may therefore return it when it is no longer one: the cause ceases, therefore the effect should cease as well.

Does the prince wish me to be his subject when I reap none of the benefits of subjection? Can my fellow citizens demand this iniquitous sharing of their advantage and my despair? Does God, unlike all benefactors, wish to condemn me to receive graces that overwhelm me?

I am obliged to follow the laws when I live under the laws: but when I no longer live under them, can they still bind me?

But, it will be said, you disturb the order of providence. God joined your soul to your body, and you separate it from there: you therefore oppose his designs and resist him.

What does that mean? Do I disturb the order of providence when I alter the modifications of matter, and make square a ball that the first laws of motion, that is to say the laws of creation and preservation, had made round? No, without doubt: I merely use the right that has been given me; and in this sense I may disturb all nature at my pleasure, without anyone being able to say that I oppose providence.

When my soul is separated from my body, will there be less order and less arrangement in the universe? Do you think that this new combination will be less perfect, and less dependent on general laws? that the world will have lost something by it? and that the works of God will be less great, or rather less immense?

Do you think that my body, become an ear of wheat, a worm, a patch of grass, will be changed into a work of nature less worthy of her, and that my soul, freed from all that was earthly in it, will become less sublime?

All these ideas, my dear Ibben, have no other source than our pride: we do not feel our smallness; and, however much one protests, we want to be counted in the universe, to cut a figure there, and to be an important object in it. We imagine that the annihilation of a being as perfect as ourselves would degrade all nature; and we cannot conceive that one man more or less in the world - what am I saying? all men together, a hundred million heads like ours - are only a fine, delicate atom which God perceives only because of the immensity of his knowledge.

*Paris, the 15th day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives a clear and forceful version of Usbek's anti-prohibition argument about suicide, and he preserves the sequence from civil contract to metaphysics to the critique of human pride. The English text is often strong, but in a few places it either softens the French or departs from it quite sharply.

### Main differences
- **Opening violence of the law**
  - French: `Les lois sont furieuses en Europe contre ceux qui se tuent eux-mêmes`
  - Fresh translation: `The laws in Europe are furious against those who kill themselves`
  - Davidson: `European law is dead against suicide`
  - Davidson's idiom is brisk, but the French is more violent and personified.

- **Consent to leave society**
  - French: `une société, dont je consens de n'être plus`
  - Fresh translation: `a society to which I consent no longer to belong`
  - Davidson: `a society to which I refuse to belong`
  - Davidson strengthens the willfulness slightly; the French is colder and more contractual.

- **`grâces qui m'accablent`**
  - Fresh translation: `graces that overwhelm me`
  - Davidson: `favours which are a burden to me`
  - Davidson is intelligible, but `accablent` is heavier than mere inconvenience.

- **Matter and form**
  - French: `les modifications de la matière`
  - Fresh translation: `the modifications of matter`
  - Davidson: `the qualities of matter`
  - `Qualities` is looser and less philosophical.

- **`un épi de blé`**
  - Fresh translation: `an ear of wheat`
  - Davidson: `a blade of grass`
  - That is a real image shift.

- **Major numerical / imagistic drift at the end**
  - French: `cent millions de têtes comme la nôtre`
  - Fresh translation: `a hundred million heads like ours`
  - Davidson: `a hundred millions of worlds like ours`
  - This is the most striking discrepancy in the letter and may reflect OCR or editorial corruption in the English witness.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: provocative, coolly logical, and philosophically deflationary.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep the juridical structure of the argument while preserving the abruptness of Usbek's rhetorical questions.
- **Davidson**: rhetorically effective, but occasionally more idiomatic and less exact in the philosophical diction.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXVI shows Davidson at his best when handling argument, but it also shows why line-level comparison matters: several philosophical terms are softened, and the closing image is altered enough to change the scale and texture of the passage.
