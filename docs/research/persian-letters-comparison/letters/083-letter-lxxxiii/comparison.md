## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXXIII**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
Although the French talk a great deal, there is nevertheless among them a kind of taciturn dervishes called Carthusians: people say that they cut out their tongues on entering the convent; and one would very much wish that all the other dervishes likewise cut away everything their profession renders useless to them.

Speaking of silent people, there are some far more singular than those, and who possess a truly extraordinary talent. They are the ones who know how to speak without saying anything, and who can keep a conversation going for two hours without it being possible to expose them, to plagiarize them, or to remember a word of what they said.

Such people are adored by women; but not nearly so much as others, who have received from nature the agreeable talent of smiling at the right moment - that is to say, at every moment - and who spread the grace of a gracious approval over everything women say.

But they are at the height of wit when they know how to hear subtlety in everything, and find a thousand little ingenious touches in the most ordinary things.

I know others who have done very well for themselves by introducing inanimate things into conversation and making their embroidered coat, their blond wig, their snuffbox, their cane, and their gloves speak there. It is good to begin out in the street to make oneself heard by the noise of one's carriage, and of the hammer that strikes rudely at the door: this preface predisposes people in favor of the rest of the discourse; and when the exordium is fine, it makes all the follies that come afterward bearable, though by good luck they arrive too late.

I promise you that these little talents, which are valued at nothing among us, serve very well here those fortunate enough to possess them, and that a man of good sense hardly shines before such people.

*Paris, the 6th day of the moon of Rebiab II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson communicates the central social satire very well: Parisian society rewards empty fluency, strategic smiling, and the theatrical display of objects and arrival. His version is lively and often close, but it tends to explain jokes that the French leaves more compact.

### Main differences
- **Speaking without saying anything**
  - French: `savent parler sans rien dire`
  - Fresh translation: `know how to speak without saying anything`
  - Davidson: `know how to talk without saying anything`
  - This is very close; the larger difference appears in the rest of the sentence.

- **`sans qu'il soit possible de les déceler, d'être leur plagiaire`**
  - Fresh translation: `without it being possible to expose them, to plagiarize them`
  - Davidson: `without its being possible to discover their meaning, to rehearse their talk`
  - Davidson paraphrases rather than translating the sharper social joke about their emptiness being impossible to copy.

- **Smiling at the right moment**
  - French: `sourire à propos, c'est-à-dire à chaque instant`
  - Fresh translation keeps the comic definition intact.
  - Davidson is close here, but slightly more polished in `the proper time`.

- **Making objects speak**
  - French: `faire parler leur habit brodé, leur perruque blonde, leur tabatière...`
  - Fresh translation keeps the personification.
  - Davidson's `make a long story about` is understandable, but less vivid.

- **Arrival as rhetorical prologue**
  - French: `le bruit du carrosse, et du marteau qui frappe rudement la porte`
  - Fresh translation preserves the coach and hammer as part of the performance.
  - Davidson is close, though a bit more elegant than the deliberately clattering French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: bright, malicious, and socially miniature; every sentence pricks salon vanity.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the compact wit and object-centered comedy.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, but somewhat more explanatory and less epigrammatic.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXXIII is a strong match in overall sense, but the fresh translation keeps better hold of Montesquieu's sharper jokes - especially the idea that certain social performers are not merely meaningless but uncopiably empty.
