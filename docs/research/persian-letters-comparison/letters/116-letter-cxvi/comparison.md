## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXVI**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
The Romans had no fewer slaves than we do; indeed, they had more. But they made better use of them.

Far from preventing the multiplication of those slaves by violent means, they encouraged it with all their power. As much as they could, they joined them to one another in a kind of marriage; by that means they filled their houses with servants of every sex and every age, and the state with an innumerable people.

Those children, who in the long run became a master's wealth, were born around him without number. He alone was charged with their nourishment and upbringing; their fathers, free of that burden, followed only the inclination of nature and multiplied without fearing too large a family.

I have told you that, among us, all slaves are occupied in guarding our women, and in nothing else; that, with regard to the state, they remain in a perpetual lethargy. As a result, the cultivation of the arts and of the land has to be restricted to a few free men, a few heads of households, and even they apply themselves to it as little as they can.

It was not the same among the Romans. The republic made use of this nation of slaves to immense advantage. Each of them had his peculium, which he possessed under the conditions his master imposed on him; with that little property he worked and turned himself toward whatever his industry led him to. One went into banking; another into maritime trade; one sold goods retail; another applied himself to some mechanical craft, or else leased and cultivated land. There was not one of them who did not devote all his power to making that peculium profitable, since it procured him both ease in present servitude and the hope of future liberty. That made for a laborious people and gave life to the arts and to industry.

These slaves, grown rich through care and labor, had themselves freed and became citizens. The republic continually repaired itself, and received new families into its bosom as the old ones disappeared.

In my following letters I may perhaps have occasion to prove to you that the more men there are in a state, the more commerce flourishes there. I shall also prove just as easily that the more commerce flourishes there, the more the number of men increases: those two things support and favor one another necessarily.

If that is so, how greatly must this prodigious number of slaves, always at work, have grown and increased! Industry and abundance brought them forth, and they in turn brought forth abundance and industry.

*Paris, the 16th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson is very close to the French in this letter. He keeps the economic argument clearly in view: Roman slavery, however abhorrent, is presented here as socially reproductive and commercially productive, unlike the idle eunuch-heavy slavery of the East described in the preceding letter.

### Main differences
- **`espèces de mariages`**
  - Fresh translation: `a kind of marriage`
  - Davidson: `marriages, of a kind`
  - Davidson is faithful here; the difference is mostly rhythmic.

- **`pécule` / `peculium`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the more specific Roman-social term `peculium`, glossed in context as a little property.
  - Davidson uses `savings` and `hoard`, which are intelligible but less precise historically.

- **Arts and land**
  - French: `la culture des arts et des terres`
  - Fresh translation keeps the pairing of arts and land.
  - Davidson collapses this more generally into `industry and agriculture`, which is accurate in sense but less literal.

- **Republic repairing itself**
  - French: `La république se réparoit sans cesse`
  - Fresh translation: `The republic continually repaired itself`
  - Davidson: `The republic was continually built up`
  - Davidson clarifies the meaning, but the French image of self-repair is worth retaining.

- **Reciprocity of abundance and industry**
  - Fresh translation keeps the mirrored structure: `Industry and abundance brought them forth, and they in turn brought forth abundance and industry.`
  - Davidson is close, but slightly less pointed in the final chiasmic effect.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, economic, and schematic; this is one of the more explicitly demographic-commercial letters in the sequence.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the compact argumentative movement and the Roman institutional vocabulary.
- **Davidson**: quite faithful overall, with only modest smoothing.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXVI is one of the closer Davidson renderings in this run. The main thing to preserve in the fresh translation is the technical, almost economic texture of the French, especially around `peculium`, self-renewing citizenship, and the reciprocal link between population and commerce.
