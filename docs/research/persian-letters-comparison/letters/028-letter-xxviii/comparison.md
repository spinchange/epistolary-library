## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXVIII**

**Rica to ∗∗∗.**

### Translation
I saw yesterday something rather singular, though it happens every day in Paris.

Toward the end of the afternoon all the people gather together and go to play a sort of scene that I have heard called comedy. The great movement is on a platform that they call the stage. On the two sides one sees, in little enclosures called boxes, men and women who play silent scenes together, much like those in use in our Persia.

At one moment it is an afflicted lover expressing her languor; at another, one with lively eyes and a passionate air devours her lover with her gaze, while he looks at her in the same way. All the passions are painted on faces and expressed with an eloquence all the more vivid for being mute. There the actors appear only half-bodied, and ordinarily have a muff, out of modesty, to hide their arms. Below is a troop of people standing up who mock those who are above on the stage, and the latter in turn laugh at those who are below.

But the ones who take the most trouble are some young men, taken for that purpose at an early age so as to endure the fatigue. They are obliged to be everywhere. They pass through places known only to themselves, climb with astonishing agility from level to level, and are above, below, in every box. They dive, so to speak; one loses sight of them, they reappear; often they leave the place of the scene and go play in another. One even sees some who, by a prodigy one would not have dared hope from their crutches, walk and move like the others. Finally people make their way to rooms where a particular comedy is played. They begin with bows and continue with embraces. They say that the slightest acquaintance gives a man the right to smother another. It seems that the place inspires tenderness. Indeed, they say that the princesses who reign there are not cruel; and, except for two or three hours each day when they are rather savage, one may say that the rest of the time they are tractable, and that this frenzy leaves them easily.

Everything I tell you here takes place almost in the same way in another place called the Opera. The whole difference is that they speak at one and sing at the other. A friend of mine took me the other day into the box where one of the principal actresses was undressing. We made each other's acquaintance so successfully that the next day I received this letter from her:

"Sir,

"I am the unhappiest girl in the world. I have always been the most virtuous actress at the Opera. Seven or eight months ago I was in the box where you saw me yesterday. As I was dressing as a priestess of Diana, a young abbé came to see me there; and, with no respect for my white robe, my veil, and my headband, he ravished me of my innocence. However much I exaggerate the sacrifice I made him, he only laughs, and insists that he found me very profane. Meanwhile I am so pregnant that I no longer dare appear on the stage; for, in matters of honor, I am of an inconceivable delicacy, and I always maintain that for a well-born girl it is easier to lose virtue than modesty. With such delicacy, you can well imagine that this young abbé would never have succeeded if he had not promised to marry me. So legitimate a motive made me pass over the usual little formalities and begin with what I ought to have ended with. But since his infidelity has dishonored me, I no longer wish to live at the Opera, where, between you and me, they scarcely give me enough to live on. For now that I am advancing in age and losing on the side of charms, my pension, which remains the same, seems to diminish every day. I have learned from a man in your retinue that in your country they set an infinite value on a good dancer, and that, if I were in Isfahan, my fortune would at once be made. If you would grant me your protection and take me with you to that country, you would have the advantage of doing good to a girl who, by her virtue and her conduct, would not prove unworthy of your kindness. I am..."

*From Paris, the 2nd day of the moon of Chalval, 1712.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives an amusing and generally effective version of Rica's extended theater joke. He preserves the outsider's misunderstanding of Parisian playhouses and the satirical letter from the actress. Even so, he often nudges the French toward smoother English comedy and occasionally over-interprets the visual metaphors.

### Main differences
- **People `go to play` comedy**
  - French: `Tout le peuple s'assemble ... et va jouer une espèce de scène`
  - Fresh translation keeps Rica's comic misunderstanding that the assembled public themselves `go to play` a scene.
  - Davidson's `all the people gather together and play at a sort of dramatic game` is workable, but a bit tidier and less odd.

- **The movement on the stage and in the boxes**
  - French: `Le grand mouvement est sur une estrade` and the spectators in the boxes `jouent ensemble des scènes muettes`
  - Fresh translation keeps the literal stage/box structure and Rica's confusion between actors and audience.
  - Davidson is lively here, but he sometimes clarifies the joke too much by making the theatrical scene more legible than Rica means it to be.

- **Half-bodies and the muff**
  - French: `les acteurs ne paroissent qu'à demi-corps, et ont ordinairement un manchon`
  - Fresh translation keeps the strange visual phrasing.
  - Davidson's `The actresses here display only half their bodies` is vivid, but it is slightly more sensational than the French.

- **Young men everywhere in the house**
  - French: `Ils sont obligés d'être partout`
  - Fresh translation preserves the busy, almost acrobatic description.
  - Davidson is close, though his `certain people` loses the more specific `young men` of the French.

- **The actress's `virtue` and pregnancy**
  - French: `je suis si grosse`
  - Fresh translation: `I am so pregnant`
  - Davidson paraphrases the idea as `my pregnancy is so apparent`, which is accurate but less bluntly comic.

- **Beginning with what she should have ended with**
  - French: `commencer par où j'aurois dû finir`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sharpness of the punchline.
  - Davidson is close, but his version is a little more polished and less abrupt.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: arch, deadpan, and visually comic, with the Persian observer deliberately misreading social theater as literal theater.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the literalness of Rica's framing and the shameless self-exoneration of the actress's letter.
- **Davidson**: often witty and effective, but more inclined to make the joke read as polished English comedy rather than as estrangement comedy.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXVIII is a strong example of why the project benefits from fresh translation. Davidson catches the satirical setup, but a more literal rendering better preserves Rica's deliberate misunderstandings of public spectacle, flirtation, and theatrical virtue.
