## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXVII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
So far we have spoken of Mohammedan countries and sought the reason why they are less populated than those once subject to Roman rule. Let us now examine what has produced the same effect among Christians.

Divorce was permitted in the pagan religion, and it was forbidden to Christians. This change, which at first seemed of such slight consequence, gradually had terrible effects, such as one can scarcely believe.

Not only was all the sweetness of marriage taken away, but its very purpose was struck at. In trying to tighten its bonds, they loosened them; and instead of uniting hearts, as was claimed, they separated them forever.

Into an act so free, and one in which the heart ought to have so large a part, they introduced constraint, necessity, and the very fatality of destiny. Disgusts, caprices, and the unsociableness of tempers were counted for nothing. They wanted to fix the heart, that is to say, the most variable and inconstant thing in nature. They bound without recall and without hope people who were worn out by one another and almost always badly matched, and they did as those tyrants did who had living men tied to dead bodies.

Nothing contributed more to mutual attachment than the possibility of divorce. A husband and wife were inclined to bear domestic troubles patiently, knowing that they were masters of bringing them to an end; and they often kept that power in their hands all their lives without using it, solely because they were free to do so.

It is not the same with Christians, whose present sufferings drive them to despair of the future. In the miseries of marriage they see only duration and, so to speak, eternity. From that come disgust, discord, and contempt; and that is so much lost to posterity. Hardly three years of marriage pass before its essential purpose is neglected; then thirty years of coldness are spent together. Private separations arise, as strong as public ones and perhaps more harmful. Each lives and remains on his own side, and all of this is to the prejudice of future generations. Soon a man, disgusted by an everlasting wife, gives himself over to prostitutes: a shameful commerce, so contrary to society, which without fulfilling the object of marriage represents at most only its pleasures.

If, of two people thus bound together, one is unfit for nature's design and for the propagation of the species, whether by temperament or by age, that one buries the other along with it, and makes it as useless as it is itself.

One must not be surprised, then, to see among Christians so many marriages yield so small a number of citizens. Divorce has been abolished; ill-matched marriages are no longer repaired; women no longer pass, as among the Romans, successively through the hands of several husbands, who drew from them along the way the best use that could possibly be made of them.

I dare say it: if, in a republic like Lacedaemon, where citizens were continually constrained by singular and subtle laws, and where there was only one family, the republic, it had been established that husbands should change wives every year, an innumerable people would have been born.

It is rather difficult to make well understood the reason that led Christians to abolish divorce. Marriage, among all the nations of the world, is a contract capable of every sort of agreement, and only those agreements that might weaken its purpose ought to have been excluded from it. But Christians do not regard it from that point of view; indeed, they have great difficulty saying what it is. They do not make it consist in sensual pleasure; on the contrary, as I have already told you, it seems they want to banish that from it as much as they can. But for them it is an image, a figure, and something mysterious, which I do not understand.

*Paris, the 19th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the structure of the letter closely and keeps its central paradox clear: the attempt to make marriage indissoluble ends by damaging marriage itself and reducing population. His English is generally accurate, though he often relaxes Montesquieu's sharper syntax into more explanatory prose.

### Main differences
- **Purpose of marriage**
  - French: `on donna atteinte à sa fin`
  - Fresh translation: `its very purpose was struck at`
  - Davidson: `attacked its very aim`
  - Davidson is faithful here; the main difference is stylistic rather than semantic.

- **Fixing the heart**
  - Fresh translation keeps the strong sequence: `They wanted to fix the heart, that is to say, the most variable and inconstant thing in nature.`
  - Davidson is close, but spreads the idea into smoother English and slightly reduces the abruptness of Montesquieu's mockery.

- **Coldness and private separations**
  - French: `on passe ensemble trente ans de froideur`
  - Fresh translation preserves the blunt image of `thirty years of coldness`.
  - Davidson conveys the sense well, but in a somewhat more essayistic cadence.

- **`commerce honteux`**
  - Fresh translation: `a shameful commerce`
  - Davidson: `a commerce shameful and opposed to society`
  - Davidson remains close, but the fresh translation keeps the French's concise moral condemnation.

- **Roman remarriage image**
  - French: `dans le chemin, le meilleur parti qu'il étoit possible`
  - Fresh translation keeps the slightly cynical sense that Roman husbands made the best use of women `along the way`.
  - Davidson is serviceable, but a bit flatter in that startlingly instrumental line.

- **Mystery of marriage**
  - Fresh translation ends with `an image, a figure, and something mysterious`.
  - Davidson's `a symbol, a type, and something mysterious` is elegant, but slightly more ecclesiastical in register.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: biting, paradoxical, and anti-sacramental in tone; it uses social theory and satire together.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the severity of the argument without losing the swift movement of the prose.
- **Davidson**: accurate in outline, but a little more polished and less barbed.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXVII is a strong example of Davidson's basic reliability at the level of argument and sequence. The fresh translation mainly needs to keep Montesquieu's harder satirical turns intact, especially the language of fixing the heart, thirty years of coldness, and the mystery Christians struggle to define.
