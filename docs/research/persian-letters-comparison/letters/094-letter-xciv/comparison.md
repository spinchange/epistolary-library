## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCIV**

**Usbek to his brother, santon at the monastery of Casbin**

### Translation
I humble myself before you, sacred santon, and I prostrate myself. I regard the traces of your feet as the apple of my eyes. Your holiness is so great that one would think you had the heart of our holy Prophet. Your austerities astonish heaven itself. The angels have looked at you from the summit of glory and said: How is he still on earth, since his spirit is with us and flies around the throne borne upon the clouds?

And how should I not honor you, I who have learned from our doctors that dervishes, even infidel ones, always possess a character of sanctity that makes them respectable to true believers, and that God has chosen in every corner of the earth souls purer than others, separating them from the impious world so that their mortifications and fervent prayers might suspend his wrath, ready to fall upon so many rebellious peoples?

Christians tell marvels of their first santons, who fled in their thousands to the dreadful deserts of the Thebaid and had Paul, Anthony, and Pachomius for leaders. If what is said of them is true, their lives were as full of prodigies as those of our most sacred imams. They sometimes spent ten entire years without seeing a single man. But they lived night and day with demons; those evil spirits tormented them without ceasing; they found them in bed and found them at table; there was never any refuge from them. If all this is true, venerable santon, one would have to admit that no one ever lived in worse company.

Sensible Christians regard all these stories as a very natural allegory, which can help us feel the misery of the human condition. In vain do we seek a tranquil state in the desert; temptations always follow us. Our passions, figured by demons, still do not leave us. Those monsters of the heart, those illusions of the mind, those vain phantoms of error and falsehood, always show themselves to seduce us, and attack us even in fasts and hair-shirts - that is to say, in our very strength.

As for me, venerable santon, I know that God's envoy has chained Satan and cast him into the abyss. He purified the earth, once filled with that empire, and made it worthy of the dwelling of angels and prophets.

*Paris, the 9th day of the moon of Chahban, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the letter's progression from extravagant reverence, to comic commentary on Christian ascetics, to allegorical reading, and finally to an Islamic profession of certainty.

### Main differences
- **Opening bodily reverence**
  - French: `Je m'humilie devant toi` / `je me prosterne` / `les vestiges de tes pieds`
  - Fresh translation keeps the physical gestures of abasement and the image of the saint's footprints.
  - Davidson's `I humble myself in the dust before you` is forceful, but it slightly enlarges the imagery instead of following the French wording closely.

- **Demons and bad company**
  - French: `personne n'auroit jamais vécu en plus mauvaise compagnie`
  - Fresh translation: `no one ever lived in worse company`
  - Davidson: `nobody ever lived in more disagreeable company`
  - `Disagreeable` softens a line whose wit is drier and a little harsher in French.

- **Allegory of temptation**
  - French: `jusque dans notre force même`
  - Fresh translation: `in our very strength`
  - Davidson: `even in our strongholds`
  - Davidson's image is clever, but the French means that temptation reaches us precisely where we think we are strongest.

- **Final prophetic certainty**
  - French contrasts the old empire of Satan with the purified earth made fit for angels and prophets.
  - Davidson is broadly accurate here, but the fresh draft keeps the prophetic cadence more directly.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: mock-devout, witty, then serious again.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve that oscillation without flattening it.
- **Davidson**: elegant and often strong, but occasionally more decorous than the French joke permits.

### Notable OCR / edition notes

## Working conclusion
Davidson's Letter XCIV is broadly faithful, but a fresh translation better preserves the bodily devotional imagery, the mordant joke about the desert fathers' demons, and the exact force of the allegory about temptation.
