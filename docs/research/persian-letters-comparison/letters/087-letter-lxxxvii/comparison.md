### Heading
**Letter LXXXVII**

**Rica to \*\*\*.**

### Translation
Here it seems as though families govern themselves. The husband has only a shadow of authority over his wife, the father over his children, the master over his slaves. Justice meddles in all their disputes; and be sure that it is always against the jealous husband, the peevish father, and the inconvenient master.

The other day I went to the place where justice is administered. Before arriving there, one must pass under the arms of an infinite number of young women sellers, who call out in deceptive voices. At first the spectacle is cheerful enough; but it becomes gloomy when one enters the great halls, where one sees only people whose dress is graver even than their faces. At last one reaches the sacred place where all the secrets of families are revealed and the most hidden actions brought into broad daylight.

There, a modest girl comes to confess the torments of a virginity kept too long, her struggles, and her painful resistance. She is so little proud of her victory that she is always threatening a near defeat; and, so that her father may no longer remain ignorant of her needs, she exposes them before the whole people.

Then a shameless woman comes to display the wrongs she herself has done her husband as a reason to be separated from him.

With equal modesty, another comes to say that she is tired of bearing the title of wife without enjoying it. She reveals the mysteries hidden in the marriage night. She wants herself delivered to the gaze of the most skillful experts, and a sentence that restores to her all the rights of virginity. Some even dare challenge their husbands, and demand in public a contest that witnesses make so difficult - a trial as degrading for the wife who sustains it as for the husband who succumbs.

An infinite number of abducted or seduced girls make men seem much worse than they are. Love makes this tribunal resound: one hears there only of enraged fathers, abused daughters, faithless lovers, and unhappy husbands.

By the law observed there, every child born during marriage is presumed to belong to the husband. He may have excellent reasons to believe otherwise; the law believes for him, and relieves him of inquiry and scruple.

In this tribunal, votes are taken by the majority. But experience, they say, has shown it would be better to gather them by the minority; and that is only natural, since there are very few just minds, and everybody agrees there are an infinity of false ones.

*Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows Rica's satirical tour of the Paris courts well. He preserves the sequence of family cases, the sexual and legal comedy, and the closing jab at majority judgment.

### Main differences
- **Domestic authority**
  - Fresh translation keeps `père chagrin` as `peevish father` and `maître incommode` as `inconvenient master`.
  - Davidson's `sorrowing father` and `exasperated master` are weaker fits for the social satire.

- **Marketplace threshold**
  - French: `passer sous les armes`
  - Fresh translation keeps the literal image of passing `under the arms` of the sellers.
  - Davidson's `run the gauntlet` is vivid but less exact.

- **Legal-sexual diction**
  - The French repeatedly uses procedural verbs such as `expose`, `révéler`, and `rétablisse`.
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh translation keeps the colder courtroom texture more consistently.

- **Final maxim**
  - French contrasts `esprits justes` with `esprits faux`.
  - Davidson's `ill-balanced ones` is plausible, but it weakens the opposition between sound judgment and false minds.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: bawdy, judicial, and satirically precise.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve how legal language is applied to intimate embarrassment.
- **Davidson**: lively and readable, though a bit more idiomatic and less procedural.
