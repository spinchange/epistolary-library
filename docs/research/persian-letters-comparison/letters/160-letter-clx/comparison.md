## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CLX**

**Solim to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
I have made up my mind: your misfortunes are about to disappear; I am going to punish.

I already feel a secret joy; my soul and yours are about to be appeased: we shall exterminate crime, and innocence will grow pale.

O you who seem made only to know nothing of your senses and to be outraged even by your own desires; eternal victims of shame and modesty — if only I could make you enter this unhappy seraglio in great floods, so that I might see you astonished by all the blood I am going to spill there!

*From the seraglio in Ispahan, the 8th day of the moon of Rebiab 1, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the letter's basic meaning and its bloodthirsty momentum. The clipped opening and the fantasy of collective slaughter both come through clearly.

### Main differences
- **Decision and punishment**
  - French: `J'ai pris mon parti ... je vais punir`
  - Fresh translation: `I have made up my mind ... I am going to punish`
  - Davidson: `I have made up my mind ... I am going to punish`
  - This is essentially exact.

- **Innocence growing pale**
  - French: `l'innocence va pâlir`
  - Fresh translation: `innocence will grow pale`
  - Davidson: `make innocence turn pale`
  - Davidson slightly recasts the syntax, but the image is preserved.

- **Knowing nothing of the senses**
  - French: `qui semblez n'être faites que pour ignorer tous vos sens`
  - Fresh translation: `who seem made only to know nothing of your senses`
  - Davidson: `who seem made only to be unconscious of your own feelings`
  - Davidson interprets the phrase rather than translating it closely. The French is stranger, harsher, and more bodily.

- **Victims versus slaves**
  - French: `éternelles victimes de la honte et de la pudeur`
  - Fresh translation: `eternal victims of shame and modesty`
  - Davidson: `everlasting slaves of shame and modesty`
  - `Slaves` intensifies and redirects the image; `victims` keeps the French closer.

- **Entering in floods**
  - French: `entrer à grands flots`
  - Fresh translation: `enter ... in great floods`
  - Davidson: `bring you in crowds`
  - Davidson captures multiplicity, but loses the more violent rush implied by `à grands flots`.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: feverish, theatrical, and intoxicated by impending punishment.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the extreme rhetoric without smoothing away its strangeness.
- **Davidson**: effective and vivid, but slightly more normalizing in the apostrophe to the women.

## Working conclusion
Letter CLX is close in Davidson at the level of action and rhetoric, but the differences are still revealing:
- Davidson normalizes the strange sensual language of `ignorer tous vos sens`
- he replaces `victims` with `slaves`
- and he softens `à grands flots` into the more ordinary `in crowds`
