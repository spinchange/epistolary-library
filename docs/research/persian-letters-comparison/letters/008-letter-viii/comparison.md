## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter VIII**

**Usbek to his friend Rustan, in Isfahan**

### Translation
Your letter reached me at Erzurum, where I am. I had guessed well enough that my departure would cause talk; I gave myself no concern over it. What would you have me follow: the prudence of my enemies, or my own?

I appeared at court from my earliest youth. I can say it: my heart was not corrupted there. I even formed a great design; I dared to be virtuous there. As soon as I recognized vice, I withdrew from it; later I approached it again only to unmask it. I carried truth to the foot of the throne. There I spoke a language unknown until then. I disconcerted flattery and astonished at once both the worshippers and the idol.

But when I saw that my sincerity had made me enemies; that I had drawn on myself the jealousy of ministers without gaining the prince's favor; that in a corrupt court I was sustained only by a frail virtue, I resolved to leave it. I pretended a great attachment to the sciences, and by dint of pretending it I truly acquired one. I no longer took part in public affairs and withdrew to a country house. But even that course had its disadvantages: I was still exposed to the malice of my enemies, and I had almost deprived myself of the means of defending against them. Certain secret warnings made me think seriously of myself. I resolved to exile myself from my country, and my retirement from court itself supplied me with a plausible pretext. I went to the king; I showed him the desire I had to instruct myself in the sciences of the West; I hinted that he might draw some utility from my travels. I found grace in his eyes, I departed, and I stole a victim from my enemies.

There, Rustan, is the true motive for my journey. Let Isfahan talk; defend me only before those who love me. Leave my enemies their malicious interpretations. I am only too happy if that is the only harm they can do me.

People are talking about me now. Perhaps I shall be forgotten only too quickly, and my friends ... No, Rustan, I do not want to surrender to that sad thought. I shall always remain dear to them; I count on their faithfulness as on yours.

*From Erzurum, the 20th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the autobiographical and political content of the letter in good order: Usbek as honest courtier, reluctant exile, and self-conscious truth-teller. The main differences lie in register and rhetorical emphasis.

### Main differences
- **Prudence, not maxims**
  - French: `la prudence de mes ennemis, ou la mienne?`
  - Fresh translation keeps the contrast as `their prudence or mine`.
  - Davidson: `the petty maxims that guide my enemies, or the dictates of my own free soul`
  - That is much freer and considerably more rhetorical than the French.

- **Great design**
  - French: `je formai même un grand dessein`
  - Fresh translation: `I even formed a great design`
  - Davidson reduces the phrase into smoother narrative English, losing some of Usbek's grand self-presentation.

- **Grace before the king**
  - French: `je trouvai grâce devant ses yeux`
  - Fresh translation: `I found grace in his eyes`
  - Davidson: `I found favour in the king's sight`
  - Davidson is accurate, but the biblical resonance is more explicit in English than in the French sentence flow.

- **A victim stolen away**
  - French: `je dérobai une victime à mes ennemis`
  - Fresh translation: `I stole a victim from my enemies`
  - Davidson: `snatched from my enemies their expected victim`
  - Davidson clarifies the sense; the French is more abrupt and slightly darker.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: defensive, proud, and politically theatrical; Usbek casts himself as a solitary truth-teller.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the self-dramatizing edge without turning it into declamatory prose.
- **Davidson**: effective, but more embellished and self-consciously noble in the opening paragraph.

## Working conclusion
Letter VIII is a good example of Davidson's tendency to dignify Usbek further than the French already does. The sense survives, but the modern reader should note how much rhetorical polish has been added.
