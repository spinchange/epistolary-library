## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter C**

**Rica to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
I find the caprices of fashion among the French astonishing. They have forgotten how they were dressed this summer; they know even less how they will be dressed this winter. But above all, one cannot imagine what it costs a husband to keep his wife in fashion.

What good would it do me to give you an exact description of their dress and ornaments? A new fashion would come and destroy my whole work, just as it destroys that of their craftsmen; and before you received my letter, everything would have changed.

A woman who leaves Paris to spend six months in the country comes back as antique as if she had been forgotten there for thirty years. The son no longer recognizes his mother's portrait, so foreign does the dress in which she is painted seem to him. He imagines it is some American woman represented there, or that the painter meant to express one of his own fantasies.

Sometimes hairstyles rise little by little, and then a revolution brings them down all at once. There was a time when their immense height placed a woman's face in the middle of herself; at another, it was the feet that occupied that place, with heels making a pedestal that held them in the air. Who could believe it? Architects have often been obliged to raise, lower, and widen their doors according as women's adornments demanded it of them; and the rules of their art were enslaved to those fancies. Sometimes one sees on a face a prodigious number of beauty patches, and they all disappear the next day. Formerly women had waists and teeth; today that is no longer the question. In this changeable nation, whatever the critic may say, daughters are made differently from their mothers.

It is with manners and ways of life as with fashions: the French change their morals according to the age of their king. The monarch could even succeed in making the nation grave, if he undertook it. The prince stamps the character of his own mind upon the court, the court upon the city, the city upon the provinces. The soul of the sovereign is a mold that gives form to all the others.

*Paris, the 8th day of the moon of Saphar, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the satirical inventory of French fashion very well, from rapid change in dress to architectural consequences and the final point that manners themselves take shape from the king.

### Main differences
- **Craftsmen vs dressmakers**
  - French: `leurs ouvriers`
  - Fresh translation keeps the broader `craftsmen`.
  - Davidson narrows this to `dressmakers`, which misses the later point about architecture and all the trades fashion mobilizes.

- **`antique`**
  - Fresh translation keeps `antique`, which is the French joke.
  - Davidson's `out of date` is accurate but less pointed and less visual.

- **Artistic caprice**
  - French: `quelqu'une de ses fantaisies`
  - Davidson's `a fancy of his own` is good, but the fresh draft keeps the slightly sharper sense of artistic caprice.

- **Morals vs lifestyle**
  - French: `les François changent de moeurs selon l'âge de leur roi`
  - Fresh translation keeps `morals`.
  - Davidson's `customs and style of living` is serviceable, but `moeurs` is stronger and more collective.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: light, visually comic, and socially observant.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the quick shifts from dress to architecture to national character.
- **Davidson**: lively and often close, though slightly more explanatory in places.

## Working conclusion
Davidson's translation is good in substance, but the cleaned English witness is damaged. A fresh draft is especially useful here because the letter depends on fast-moving visual jokes that are easy to blunt through smoothing or OCR interruption.
