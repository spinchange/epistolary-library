## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXXIX**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
Liberty and equality reign in Paris. Birth, virtue, even military merit, however brilliant, do not lift a man out of the crowd in which he is confounded. Jealousy of rank is unknown there. They say that the first man in Paris is the one with the best horses on his carriage.

A great lord is a man who sees the king, speaks to ministers, has ancestors, debts, and pensions. If, besides all that, he can hide his idleness beneath an air of business or a feigned devotion to pleasure, he believes himself the happiest of men.

In Persia, the only great men are those to whom the monarch gives some share in government. Here, there are people who are great by birth, but without influence. Kings do as skilled craftsmen do, who always use the simplest machines for their work.

Favor is the great divinity of the French. The minister is the high priest who offers many victims to it. Those around it are not dressed in white: now sacrificers, now sacrificed, they devote themselves to their idol together with all the people.

*Paris, the 9th day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the letter's satirical movement from Parisian equality, to the definition of a grand seigneur, to the cult of favor as a political religion. Where the surviving clean text breaks off, the continuation can still be recovered from the spillover at the start of Letter XC.

### Main differences
- **`mérite même de la guerre`**
  - Fresh translation: `military merit`
  - Davidson: `military fame`
  - Davidson is serviceable, but the French is closer to military distinction or merit.

- **`sans crédit`**
  - Fresh translation: `without influence`
  - Davidson: `not esteemed`
  - `Crédit` here is specifically social and political standing, not mere esteem.

- **Machine image**
  - French compares kings to craftsmen who use the simplest machines.
  - Davidson is close, but my draft keeps the analogy a little more directly.

- **Sacrificial ending**
  - Fresh translation retains `favor` as a divinity and the courtiers as alternately sacrificers and sacrifices.
  - Davidson's phrasing is effective, but the pagan-sacrificial metaphor lands more sharply when kept plain.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, ironical, and compact.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the sentence-level sharpness and political bite.
- **Davidson**: fluent, though partially obscured here by damaged transmission.
