## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXV**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
You are looking for the reason why the earth is less populated than it once was; and if you attend to it closely, you will see that the great difference comes from the change that has taken place in morals and manners.

Since the Christian religion and the Mohammedan religion divided the Roman world between them, things have greatly changed: those two religions are far from being as favorable to the propagation of the species as the religion of those former masters of the universe.

In that earlier religion, polygamy was forbidden; and in that it had a very great advantage over the Mohammedan religion. Divorce was permitted in it; and that gave it another advantage, no less considerable, over Christianity.

I find nothing so contradictory as that plurality of wives permitted by the holy Koran, and the command to satisfy them laid down by the same book. `See to your wives,` says the Prophet, `because you are as necessary to them as their garments, and they are as necessary to you as your garments.` That is a precept that makes the life of a true Muslim a very laborious one. A man who has the four wives established by law, and as many concubines and slave girls besides, must he not be crushed beneath so many garments?

`Your wives are your fields,` says the Prophet again; `go therefore to your fields: do good for your souls, and one day you will find it again.`

I regard a good Muslim as an athlete, destined to fight without rest, but who soon, weakened and overwhelmed by his first exertions, languishes on the very field of victory and finds himself, so to speak, buried beneath his own triumphs.

Nature always acts slowly and, so to speak, sparingly: its operations are never violent. Even in its productions it requires temperance; it proceeds only by rule and measure. If one forces it, it soon falls into weakness; it uses all the strength left to it merely to preserve itself, losing altogether its productive virtue and its generative power.

It is into this state of exhaustion that this great number of women always puts us, women more suited to wear us out than to satisfy us. Among us it is very common to see a man in an immense seraglio with a very small number of children; and even those children are for the most part weak and sickly, carrying in themselves their father's languor.

That is not all. These women, obliged to a forced continence, need people to guard them, and these can only be eunuchs: religion, jealousy, and reason itself allow no others to approach them. These guardians must be very numerous, both to maintain order within amid the wars these women wage incessantly against one another, and to prevent any attempt from without. Thus a man who has ten wives or concubines needs at least as many eunuchs to guard them. But what a loss for society in this great number of men dead from birth! What depopulation must result from it!

The female slaves who are in the seraglio to serve, along with the eunuchs, this great number of women almost always grow old there in an afflicting virginity. They cannot marry while they remain there, and once their mistresses have grown used to them, they almost never part with them.

That is how a single man alone occupies so many subjects of both sexes for his pleasures, causes them to die to the state, and renders them useless to the propagation of the species.

Constantinople and Ispahan are the capitals of the two greatest empires in the world: everything ought to flow there, and people, drawn in a thousand ways, ought to come there from every side. Yet they perish by themselves, and they would soon be destroyed if the sovereigns did not, almost every century, bring entire nations there to repeople them.

I shall exhaust this subject in another letter.

*Paris, the 13th day of the moon of Chahban, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the argumentative line of the letter well: anti-polygamy satire, physiological exhaustion, the social cost of eunuchs and enclosed women, and the final claim that great eastern capitals survive only through repeated replenishment. His version is generally accurate, but he often softens Montesquieu's harder social vocabulary or substitutes more literary English.

### Main differences
- **`moeurs` as manners / morals**
  - Fresh translation: `the change that has taken place in morals and manners`
  - Davidson: `a moral change`
  - Davidson captures part of the idea, but `moeurs` is broader than morality alone; it includes customs and social habits.

- **Qur'anic clothing image**
  - Fresh translation keeps the metaphor of spouses as `garments` and the comic burden of `so many garments`.
  - Davidson translates the passage fluently, but his `visit your wives` phrasing is a little more decorous than the French's direct practical command.

- **`vos labourages`**
  - Fresh translation: `your fields`
  - Davidson: `your tilth` and `devote yourselves therefore to your labour`
  - Davidson leans into archaism. The French image is agricultural but more straightforward.

- **Dead from birth**
  - French: `ce grand nombre d'hommes morts dès leur naissance`
  - Fresh translation: `this great number of men dead from birth`
  - Davidson: `men practically dead from their birth`
  - `Practically` weakens the brutal force of the original.

- **Women as causes of exhaustion**
  - French: `plus propres à nous épuiser qu'à nous satisfaire`
  - Fresh translation preserves the blunt contrast: they exhaust more than satisfy.
  - Davidson is close, but his register is slightly more polished and less caustic.

- **Subjects of both sexes**
  - French: `tant de sujets de l'un et l'autre sexe`
  - Fresh translation keeps the language of subjects, emphasizing political loss.
  - Davidson's `persons of both sexes` is accurate in sense but less political in force.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: satirical, coldly demographic, and openly polemical about polygamy, eunuchs, and seraglio life.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve both the satire and the administrative harshness of the argument.
- **Davidson**: clear and readable, but often a little smoother and less severe than the French.

### OCR / cleanup notes

- Those errors do not obscure the overall comparison, but they should be cleaned in a later pass.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXV is broadly well represented by Davidson, but the sharper edges of the French matter here. The letter is not only making a moral argument; it is making a demographic and political one, and terms like `moeurs`, `subjects`, and `dead from birth` should stay as hard as they are in the original.
