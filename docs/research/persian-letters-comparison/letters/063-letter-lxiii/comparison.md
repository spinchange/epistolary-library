## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXIII**

**Rica to Usbek**

### Translation
I think you mean to spend your life in the country. At first I only lost you for two or three days, and now it has been fifteen since I saw you. True, you are in a charming house, you find there society that suits you, and you reason there at your ease. That is more than enough to make you forget the whole universe.

As for me, I lead much the same life you saw me lead before: I move through the world and try to know it. My mind is gradually losing what remained in it of Asia and is bending without effort to European manners. I am no longer so astonished to see in a house five or six women with five or six men; and I find that arrangement not badly imagined.

I can say it plainly: I have only known women since I came here. I have learned more about them in one month than I would have in thirty years in a seraglio.

Among us all characters are uniform because they are forced. One does not see people as they are, but as one compels them to be. In that slavery of heart and mind, one hears only fear, which has but a single language, and not nature, which expresses itself so differently and appears under so many forms.

Dissimulation, that art so practiced and so necessary among us, is unknown here. Everything speaks, everything is seen, everything is heard. The heart shows itself like the face. In manners, in virtue, and even in vice, one always perceives something naive.

To please women, one must have a certain talent different from that which pleases them still more. It consists in a kind of playfulness in the mind that amuses them because it seems every instant to promise them what can only be delivered at too long intervals.

That playfulness, naturally made for dressing rooms, seems to have ended by forming the general character of the nation. They trifle in council, they trifle at the head of an army, they trifle with an ambassador. Occupations appear ridiculous only in proportion to the seriousness put into them. A doctor would cease to be ridiculous if his clothes were less gloomy and if he killed his patients playfully.

*At Paris, the 10th day of the moon of Rebiab I, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries over Rica's light social observation well: Usbek's retreat to the country, Rica's growing acclimatization to European society, his contrast between Persian fear and French openness, and the final national portrait of cultivated frivolity.

### Main differences
- **Not badly imagined**
  - French: `cela n'est pas mal imaginé`
  - Fresh translation preserves the sly understatement.
  - Davidson: `not altogether a bad idea`, which is close but more idiomatic.
- **Fear has only one language**
  - Fresh translation keeps the metaphor exactly.
  - Davidson expands it into `a dull routine of words`, which adds interpretation.
- **Naive in vice**
  - French: `dans le vice même, on aperçoit toujours quelque chose de naïf`
  - Fresh translation keeps that startling phrase.
  - Davidson also keeps it fairly well.
- **Playfulness / badinage**
  - Fresh translation uses `playfulness` and repeats it.
  - Davidson disperses the term into `playfulness of mind` and then `gaiety`, slightly weakening the conceptual thread.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: amused, sociological, and pointed in its contrast between Persian coercion and French ease.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the quick shifts from intimacy to social theory.
- **Davidson**: fluent and graceful, if somewhat more polished than the French aphorisms.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXIII is one of Rica's most revealing notes on cultural adaptation. Davidson is good, but the fresh translation keeps the French's concise conceptual vocabulary — especially around fear, dissimulation, and `badinage` — more visible.
