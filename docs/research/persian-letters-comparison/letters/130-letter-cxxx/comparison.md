### Translation
In this letter I shall speak to you of a certain nation called the newsmongers, who gather in a magnificent garden, where their idleness is always busy. They are very useless to the state, and fifty years of their talk have no different effect from what a silence just as long might have produced; yet they think themselves important because they amuse themselves with magnificent projects and handle great interests.

The basis of their conversations is a frivolous and ridiculous curiosity: there is no cabinet so mysterious that they do not claim to penetrate it; they cannot bear to be ignorant of anything; they know how many wives our august sultan has, how many children he fathers every year; and, though they spend nothing on spies, they are informed of the measures he is taking to humble the emperor of the Turks and the emperor of the Moguls.

Hardly have they exhausted the present when they hurl themselves into the future; and, walking ahead of Providence, they anticipate it in all men's actions. They take a general by the hand; and, after praising him for a thousand foolish things he has not done, they prepare a thousand more that he will not do.

They make armies fly like cranes and walls fall like cardboard; they have bridges over every river, secret roads through every mountain, immense magazines in burning sands: only common sense is lacking.

A man with whom I lodge received this letter from a newsmonger. Since it seemed singular to me, I kept it; here it is:

'Sir,

'I am seldom mistaken in my conjectures about the affairs of the times. On 1 January 1711, I predicted that the emperor Joseph would die in the course of the year. It is true that, as he was in very good health, I thought I would be laughed at if I explained myself too clearly; that made me use somewhat enigmatic terms. But people who know how to reason understood me perfectly well. On 17 April of the same year, he died of smallpox.

'As soon as war was declared between the emperor and the Turks, I went looking for our gentlemen in every corner of the Tuileries; I gathered them near the basin, and predicted that Belgrade would be besieged and taken. I was fortunate enough for my prediction to be fulfilled. It is true that, toward the middle of the siege, I wagered a hundred pistoles that it would be taken on 18 August; it was not taken until the next day. Can one lose at such a fine game?

'When I saw the Spanish fleet disembark in Sardinia, I judged that it would conquer the island. I said so, and it proved true. Swollen with that success, I added that this victorious fleet would go and land at Final in order to conquer the Milanese. As I met resistance in getting this idea accepted, I decided to uphold it gloriously: I wagered fifty pistoles, and lost them again; for that devil Alberoni, despite the faith of treaties, sent his fleet to Sicily and deceived, all at once, two great politicians, the duke of Savoy and me.

'All this has so disconcerted me, sir, that I have resolved always to predict and never to wager. Formerly, at the Tuileries, we knew nothing of betting, and the late Count of L. scarcely tolerated it; but, since a troop of fops has mixed itself in among us, we no longer know where we stand. Hardly have we opened our mouths to tell a piece of news when one of these young men proposes to bet against it.

'The other day, as I was opening my manuscript and setting my spectacles on my nose, one of those braggarts, seizing precisely the interval between my first word and my second, said to me: I bet a hundred pistoles no. I pretended not to notice that extravagance; and, resuming in a louder voice, I said: Marshal *** having learned... That is false, he said to me, your news is always absurd; there is no common sense in any of it. I beg you, sir, to do me the pleasure of lending me thirty pistoles; for I confess these bets have greatly thrown me into disorder. I send you copies of two letters I have written to the minister. I am, etc.'

LETTER FROM A NEWSMONGER TO THE MINISTER.

'My lord,

'I am the most zealous subject the king has ever had. It was I who obliged one of my friends to carry out the plan I had formed for a book demonstrating that Louis the Great was the greatest of all the princes who have deserved the name Great. For a long time I have been working on another book that will do even more honor to our nation, if Your Greatness will grant me a privilege. My design is to prove that, since the beginning of the monarchy, the French have never been beaten, and that what historians have said until now about our reverses are sheer impostures. I am obliged to set them right on many occasions; and I dare flatter myself that I shine especially in criticism. I am, my lord, etc.'

'My lord,

'Since the loss we have suffered in Count L., we beg you to have the goodness to allow us to elect a president. Disorder is entering our conferences, and affairs of state are not discussed there with the same deliberation as before; our young people live absolutely without regard for the old, and among themselves without discipline: it is the very council of Rehoboam, where the young impose upon the elders. In vain we represent to them that we had been peaceful possessors of the Tuileries twenty years before they were born; I think they will end by driving us out, and that, forced to leave these places where we have so often summoned up the shades of our French heroes, we shall have to go hold our conferences in the King's Garden or in some more remote place. I am...'

*Paris, the 7th day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the main satirical architecture of the letter: the newsmongers are idle political gossips who mistake speculation for action, and the inset letters turn them into ridiculous memorialists of their own importance.

### Main differences
- **`nouvellistes`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the more literal `newsmongers`.
  - Davidson's `Quidnuncs` is clever and historically apt in English, but also more domesticated and slightly more whimsical than the French.

- **`leur oisiveté est toujours occupée`**
  - Fresh translation preserves the paradox: `their idleness is always busy`.
  - Davidson's `always indolently busy` is strong, though a little more explanatory.

- **`marchant au-devant de la providence`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the mock-blasphemous image of walking ahead of Providence.
  - Davidson's `stealing a march on Providence` is vivid and excellent, but more idiomatic and less literal.

- **`petits-maîtres`**
  - Fresh translation uses `fops`.
  - Davidson's clean text is marred here by OCR corruption (`pitits-maltres`), which obscures the point unless checked against the French.

- **The two letters to the minister**
  - The French satire depends on self-importance, administrative petitioning, and national vanity all being voiced in the same deadpan style.
  - Davidson captures much of this, but the fresh draft keeps the bureaucratic flatness slightly harder.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: mock-ethnographic, self-amused, and politically satirical.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps both the observational wit and the absurd self-seriousness of the newsmongers' own prose.
- **Davidson**: often very effective, but more Anglicized in diction and more vulnerable here to OCR damage.
