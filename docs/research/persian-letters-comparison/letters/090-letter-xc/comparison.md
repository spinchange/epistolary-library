## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XC**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The desire for glory is no different from that instinct all creatures have for self-preservation. It seems that we enlarge our being when we can carry it into the memory of others: it is a new life that we acquire, and it becomes as precious to us as the one we received from heaven.

But as all men are not equally attached to life, they are not equally sensitive to glory. This noble passion is always engraved in their hearts; but imagination and education modify it in a thousand ways.

That difference, which one finds from man to man, is felt still more strongly from people to people.

One may lay it down as a maxim that in every state the desire for glory increases with the liberty of subjects and diminishes with it: glory is never the companion of servitude.

A sensible man was saying to me the other day: `In many respects people are freer in France than in Persia, and for that reason glory is more loved here. This happy notion makes a Frenchman do with pleasure and taste what your sultan obtains from his subjects only by continually setting punishments and rewards before their eyes.

Among us, the prince is jealous of the honor of the lowest of his subjects. There are respected tribunals for maintaining it. It is the sacred treasure of the nation, and the only one the sovereign is not master of, because he could not be without injuring his own interests. So if a subject finds his honor wounded by his prince, whether through some preference or the slightest mark of contempt, he leaves the court, his office, his service, and retires home.

The difference between French troops and yours is that the latter, being made up of slaves naturally cowardly, overcome fear of death only through fear of punishment, and that produces in the soul a new kind of terror that leaves it almost stupid. The former, by contrast, present themselves to blows with delight, and banish fear by a satisfaction stronger than fear itself.

But the sanctuary of honor, reputation, and virtue seems to be established above all in republics and in countries where one may pronounce the word fatherland. In Rome, Athens, and Lacedaemon, honor alone rewarded the most signal services. An oak or laurel crown, a statue, a eulogy - that was an immense reward for a battle won or a city taken.

There, a man who had done a fine action found himself sufficiently rewarded by that action itself. He could not look upon one of his compatriots without feeling the pleasure of being that man's benefactor; he counted his services by the number of his fellow citizens. Any man can do good to one other man; but to contribute to the happiness of an entire society is to resemble the gods.

But must not this noble emulation be wholly extinguished in the hearts of your Persians, among whom office and dignity are only attributes of the sovereign's fancy? Reputation and virtue are treated there as imaginary if they are not accompanied by the prince's favor, with which they are born and die alike. A man with public esteem is never sure of not being dishonored tomorrow. Today he is a general of the army; perhaps tomorrow the prince will make him his cook, and he will have no praise left to hope for except that he made a good stew.`

*Paris, the 15th day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves both the philosophical opening on glory and the embedded speech contrasting French honor with Persian servility. The broad argument survives very well once the spillover from Letter LXXXIX is separated out.

### Main differences
- **`avec plaisir et avec goût`**
  - Fresh translation: `with pleasure and taste`
  - Davidson: `with pleasure and inclination`
  - Davidson loses the social nuance of `goût`.

- **Honor as treasure**
  - French: `le trésor sacré de la nation`
  - Fresh translation keeps `sacred treasure`.
  - Davidson is close, but slightly smoother and less ceremonial.

- **Military psychology**
  - Fresh translation keeps the contrast between a terror that stupefies and a satisfaction that overcomes fear.
  - Davidson is accurate, but a touch more diffuse.

- **Final culinary insult**
  - Both versions keep the cook image.
  - My draft preserves the abrupt descent from generalship to kitchen service in plainer language.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: meditative at first, then sharply political and rhetorical.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the transition into reported speech and the civic vocabulary of honor exact.
- **Davidson**: strong overall, though slightly more oratorical than the French.
