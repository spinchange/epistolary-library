## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXXVIII**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
It is a great question among men whether it is more advantageous to take liberty away from women or to leave it to them. It seems to me that there are many reasons on both sides. If Europeans say there is no generosity in making unhappy the persons one loves, our Asians reply that there is baseness in men when they renounce the empire nature has given them over women. If one tells them that large numbers of women shut up together are burdensome, they answer that ten women who obey are less troublesome than one who does not. And if they object in turn that Europeans cannot be happy with women who are unfaithful to them, they are answered that this fidelity of which they boast so much does not prevent the disgust that always follows satisfied passions; that our women are too much our own; that so tranquil a possession leaves us nothing to desire or to fear; that a little coquetry is a salt that stings and prevents corruption. Perhaps a wiser man than I would be embarrassed to decide the question: for if the Asians do very well to seek means fitted to calm their anxieties, the Europeans do very well too in not having any.

`After all,' they say, `even if we were unhappy as husbands, we would always find a way to compensate ourselves as lovers. For a man to have reason to complain of his wife's infidelity, there would have to be only three persons in the world; he will always still find his mark when there are four.'

It is another question whether natural law subjects women to men. `No,' a very gallant philosopher said to me the other day, `nature has never dictated such a law; the empire we have over them is a genuine tyranny; they have let us take it only because they have more gentleness than we do, and consequently more humanity and reason. Those advantages, which would no doubt have given them superiority if we had been rational, have made them lose it because we are not.'

Now, if it is true that we have over women only a tyrannical power, it is no less true that they have over us a natural empire, that of beauty, which nothing resists. Ours is not found in every country; but the empire of beauty is universal. Why then should we have a privilege? Is it because we are stronger? But that is a real injustice. We use every sort of means to break their spirit; strengths would be equal if education were too; let us test them in those talents that education has not weakened, and we shall see whether we are so strong.

It must be admitted, though this shocks our customs, that among the most civilized peoples women have always had authority over their husbands; it was established by law among the Egyptians in honor of Isis, and among the Babylonians in honor of Semiramis. It was said of the Romans that they commanded all nations, but obeyed their wives. I say nothing of the Sauromatians, who were truly in servitude to the sex; they were too barbarous for their example to be cited.

You see, my dear Ibben, that I have acquired the taste of this country, where people like to uphold extraordinary opinions and reduce everything to paradox. The Prophet has decided the question and settled the rights of the one sex and the other. `Women,' he says, `must honor their husbands; their husbands must honor them; but they have the advantage of one degree over them.'

*From Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Gemmadi 2, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the argumentative shape of the letter well: the exchange of European and Asian positions, the gallant philosopher's intervention, and the final return to prophetic authority. His version is smooth and readable, but it often domesticates the paradoxes and sharpens the aphorisms into more finished English than the French demands.

### Main differences
- **A `great question`, not an `all-important` one**
  - French: `C'est une grande question`
  - Fresh translation: `It is a great question`
  - Davidson: `It is an all-important question`
  - Davidson intensifies the claim at the outset.

- **`Empire` over women**
  - French: `l'empire que la nature leur a donné`
  - Fresh translation: `the empire nature has given them`
  - Davidson: `the dominion which nature has given them`
  - `Dominion` is reasonable, but keeping `empire` helps preserve the political metaphor that runs through the letter.

- **The joke about `three` and `four` persons**
  - French: `ils seront toujours à but quand il y en aura quatre`
  - Fresh translation: `he will always still find his mark when there are four`
  - Davidson: `odd may be made even, as long as a fourth can be found`
  - Davidson turns a sharp, slightly cynical metaphor into a more idiomatic English proverb.

- **`Douceur`**
  - French: `elles ont plus de douceur que nous`
  - Fresh translation: `they have more gentleness than we do`
  - Davidson: `they are more tenderhearted`
  - `Tenderhearted` narrows and sentimentalizes the broader French term.

- **Education and equality of strength**
  - French: `les forces seroient égales, si l'éducation l'étoit aussi`
  - Fresh translation: `strengths would be equal if education were too`
  - Davidson: `Our powers would be found equal if we were educated alike`
  - Davidson is close in sense, though slightly fuller and more explanatory.

- **The Prophet's final ruling**
  - French: `ils ont l'avantage d'un degré sur elles`
  - Fresh translation: `they have the advantage of one degree over them`
  - Davidson: `men are a degree higher in the scale of creation than women`
  - Davidson expands the line into a metaphysical hierarchy that is not stated so explicitly in the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: witty, paradoxical, and exploratory; it stages argument rather than simply delivering doctrine.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the balance between intellectual play, social observation, and the final appeal to authority.
- **Davidson**: clear and attractive, but often a little more polished and sententiously English than the French conversation-like movement.
