## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXI**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
Countries inhabited by peoples living in a savage state are ordinarily thinly populated because of the aversion almost all of them have for labor and the cultivation of the earth. That unhappy aversion is so strong that, when they utter an imprecation against one of their enemies, they wish him nothing worse than to be reduced to plowing a field, believing that only hunting and fishing are noble exercises worthy of them.

But since there are often years in which hunting and fishing yield very little, they are laid waste by frequent famines; and besides, there is no country so abundant in game and fish that it can provide subsistence for a great people, because animals always flee places that are too densely inhabited.

Besides, villages of such peoples, numbering two or three hundred inhabitants and isolated from one another, with interests as separate as those of two empires, cannot sustain themselves, because they do not have the resource of great states, whose parts answer one another and come to one another's aid.

Among such peoples there is another custom no less harmful than the first: the cruel practice by which women procure abortion so that pregnancy may not make them disagreeable to their husbands.

Here there are terrible laws against that disorder, carried to the point of fury. Any girl who has not declared her pregnancy to the magistrate is punished with death if her child dies: modesty and shame, even accidents themselves, never excuse her.

*Paris, the 9th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives a clear version of this short letter, preserving its sequence from labor-aversion to famine, political fragmentation, abortion, and punitive law. The main differences are less about large omissions than about wording and the handling of a few socially charged or legally precise expressions.

### Main differences
- **`les sauvages`**
  - Fresh translation renders the term more cautiously as `peoples living in a savage state`.
  - Davidson uses the direct noun `savages`.
  - The French is of course blunt, but a modern fresh translation can keep the historical sense without reproducing the noun as unmarked modern usage.

- **Subsistence and scale**
  - Fresh translation keeps the point that no land rich in game and fish can support `a great people`.
  - Davidson's `a numerous people` is close, but slightly more general.

- **Political mutuality**
  - French: `dont toutes les parties se répondent et se secourent mutuellement`
  - Fresh translation preserves the image of the parts of a state answering and helping one another.
  - Davidson simplifies this into members being `in accord`, which loses some of the structural metaphor.

- **Pregnancy declaration law**
  - French: `Toute fille qui n'a point été déclarer sa grossesse au magistrat`
  - Fresh translation: `Any girl who has not declared her pregnancy to the magistrate`
  - Davidson: `Every unmarried woman`
  - `Unmarried` is an interpretive addition not present in the French wording we have here.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compressed, severe, and quasi-ethnographic in posture.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to stay faithful while handling especially loaded terminology with some care.
- **Davidson**: clear and readable, though sometimes more interpretive than literal.
