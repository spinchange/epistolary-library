### Translation
A man of wit is usually difficult in company. He chooses few people; he grows bored with that great number whom he is pleased to call bad company; and it is impossible for him not to let his distaste be felt a little: so many enemies.

Sure of pleasing whenever he wishes, he very often neglects to do so.

He is inclined to criticism, because he sees more things than other people and feels them more keenly.

He almost always ruins his fortune, because his mind supplies him with a greater number of means for doing so.

He fails in his undertakings because he risks too much. His sight, always directed far away, makes him see objects that are too distant. Besides, at the birth of a project, he is less struck by the difficulties that come from the thing itself than by the remedies, which come from him and which he draws from his own resources.

He neglects the small details on which, however, the success of almost all great affairs depends.

The mediocre man, by contrast, tries to take advantage of everything: he knows well that he has nothing to lose through negligence.

Universal approval is more often given to the mediocre man. People are delighted to give to the latter; they are enchanted to take away from the former. While envy falls upon the one and forgives him nothing, everything is supplied in favor of the other: vanity declares for him.

But if the man of wit has so many disadvantages, what shall we say of the hard condition of the learned?

I never think of it without recalling a letter from one of them to one of his friends. Here it is:

“Sir,

“I am a man who spends every night looking, through telescopes thirty feet long, at those great bodies that roll above our heads; and when I want relaxation, I take my little microscopes and observe a mite or a moth.

“I am not rich, and I have only one room. I do not even dare light a fire there, because I keep my thermometer in it, and any foreign heat would make it rise. Last winter I thought I would die of cold; and although my thermometer, which was at the lowest degree, warned me that my hands were about to freeze, I did not stir; and I have the consolation of being exactly informed about the slightest changes in the weather throughout the whole past year.

“I mix very little with others; and of all the people I see, I know none of them. But there is a man in Stockholm, another in Leipzig, another in London, whom I have never seen, and whom I shall doubtless never see, with whom I keep such exact correspondence that I do not let a single post go by without writing to them.

“But although I know no one in my neighborhood, I am in such bad repute there that in the end I shall be obliged to leave it. Five years ago I was rudely insulted by one of my women neighbors for dissecting a dog that she claimed belonged to her. A butcher’s wife who happened to be there joined in; and while the first overwhelmed me with insults, the second beat me with stones, together with Doctor ***, who was with me and received a terrible blow on the frontal and occipital bone, so that the seat of his reason was badly shaken.

“Since that time, as soon as any dog strays to the end of the street, it is immediately decided that it has passed through my hands. A good bourgeoise who had lost a little dog which, she said, she loved more than her children, came the other day to faint away in my room; and, not finding it, she summoned me before the magistrate. I believe I shall never be delivered from the importunate malice of those women who, with their yelping voices, keep deafening me with the funeral oration of all the automata that have died these last ten years.

“I am, etc.”

All learned men were once accused of magic. I am not surprised at it. Each person said to himself: I have carried natural talents as far as they can go; yet a certain learned man has advantages over me: there must certainly be some devilry in it.

Now that accusations of that sort have fallen into discredit, another course has been taken; and a learned man can hardly avoid the reproach of irreligion or heresy. He may be acquitted by the people; the wound is made, and it will never close properly. It always remains a sore spot. An adversary will come thirty years later and say to him modestly: God forbid that I should say the charge against you is true! but you were obliged to defend yourself. Thus they turn his very justification against him.

If he writes some history, and has nobility in his mind and some rectitude in his heart, they stir up a thousand persecutions against him. They will go and rouse the magistrate against him over some event that took place a thousand years ago. And they would have his pen be captive if it is not venal.

Happier, however, than those cowardly men who abandon their faith for a mediocre pension; who, if one takes all their impostures in detail, do not sell them even for an obol; who overturn the constitution of the empire, diminish the rights of one power, increase those of another, give to princes, take from peoples, revive obsolete rights, flatter the passions that are in credit in their own time and the vices that are on the throne, imposing on posterity all the more indignantly because it has fewer means of destroying their testimony.

But it is not enough for an author to have endured all these insults; it is not enough for him to have been in continual anxiety over the success of his work. At last that work, which cost him so much, comes into the light. It attracts quarrels from every side. And how could he avoid them? He held an opinion; he supported it in his writings; he did not know that a man two hundred leagues away had said the exact opposite. Yet war is declared.

Even if he could hope to obtain some esteem! No. At best he is valued only by those who have applied themselves to the same branch of learning as he has. A philosopher has sovereign contempt for a man whose head is burdened with facts; and in his turn he is regarded as a visionary by the man with a good memory.

As for those who make a profession of proud ignorance, they would like the whole human race to be buried in the oblivion in which they themselves will be buried.

A man who lacks some talent makes up for it by despising it: he removes the obstacle he found between merit and himself; and by that means he places himself on the same level as the man whose labors he fears.

Finally, to an equivocal reputation one must add the privation of pleasures and the loss of health.

*Paris, the 20th day of the moon of Chahban, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the long satirical complaint well at the level of structure and argument. His version remains readable and often vivid, but it repeatedly heightens the social and intellectual register, especially by turning broadly `learned` figures into more specialized `scientific` ones.

### Main differences
- **`Homme d'esprit` vs `man of genius`**
  - French: `Un homme d'esprit`
  - Fresh translation: `A man of wit`
  - Davidson: `A man of genius`
  - Davidson makes the opening category grander and more romantic than the French phrase requires.

- **The world of the `savant`**
  - French: `les savants` / `un savant`
  - Fresh translation keeps `the learned` / `a learned man`.
  - Davidson consistently prefers `scientific man`, which narrows the satire toward science rather than learned life more broadly.

- **The grotesque dog passage**
  - French: `l'oraison funèbre de tous les automates qui sont morts depuis dix ans`
  - Fresh translation: `the funeral oration of all the automata that have died these last ten years`
  - Davidson: `the funeral oration of all the beasts that have died during the last six years`
  - This is a notable shift in both imagery and fact: `automates` becomes `beasts`, and `ten years` becomes `six years`.

- **Pen captive vs pen bought**
  - French: `on voudra que sa plume soit captive, si elle n'est pas vénale`
  - Fresh translation: `they would have his pen be captive if it is not venal`
  - Davidson: `if his pen is not to be bought they would have it restrained`
  - Davidson is clear, but the French has a starker antithesis between captivity and venality.

- **Final sentence on the cost of authorship**
  - French: `il faut joindre ... la privation des plaisirs et la perte de la santé`
  - Fresh translation stays close to the austere list.
  - Davidson smooths it into `abstain from all pleasure and sacrifice his health`, which is faithful in sense but more polished in cadence.

- **Date discrepancy**
  - French date: `le 20 de la lune de Chahban 1720`
  - Davidson: `the 26th of the moon of Chahban, 1720`
  - This looks like a real discrepancy worth flagging.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: sprawling, bitter, witty, and aggressively social in its satire.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the length and accumulation of the complaint without turning it into Victorian eloquence.
- **Davidson**: strong on readability, but often enlarges the scale of the speaker from `witty/learned` to `genius/scientific` and occasionally tidies the grotesque details.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLV is broadly faithful in Davidson, but the comparison is valuable because it shows several recurring patterns:
- rhetorical elevation (`man of genius`)
- narrowing of `savants` into `scientific men`
- occasional factual or lexical drift in comic details (`automata` / `ten years`)
- a smoother, more literary English cadence than the French's sharper satirical prose
