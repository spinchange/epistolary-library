## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LVII**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
The libertines here maintain an infinite number of women of pleasure, and the devout an innumerable number of dervishes. These dervishes make three vows: obedience, poverty, and chastity. It is said that the first is the best observed of all; as for the second, I can answer for it that it is not observed at all; I leave you to judge the third.

But however rich these dervishes may be, they never give up the condition of poor men; our glorious Sultan would sooner renounce his magnificent and sublime titles. They are right, for this title of poor men keeps them from being poor.

Doctors, and some of these dervishes who are called confessors, are always here either too highly esteemed or too deeply despised; yet they say that heirs get on better with doctors than with confessors.

The other day I went into a convent of these dervishes. One of them, made venerable by his white hair, received me very politely; and, after showing me the whole house, took me into the garden, where we began to talk.

"Father," I said to him, "what office do you hold in the community?"

"Sir," he answered, with an expression of great satisfaction at my question, "I am a casuist."

"A casuist?" I replied. "Since I have been in France, I have not heard this office mentioned."

"What! you do not know what a casuist is? Very well, listen: I shall give you an idea of it that will leave nothing to be desired. There are two kinds of sins: mortal sins, which absolutely exclude one from paradise; and venial sins, which do indeed offend God, but do not anger him to the point of depriving us of blessedness. Now all our art consists in carefully distinguishing these two kinds of sins. For, with the exception of a few libertines, all Christians want to gain paradise; but there is scarcely anyone who does not want to gain it as cheaply as possible. Once one knows the mortal sins well, one tries not to commit those, and that settles the matter. There are men who do not aspire to such a high perfection; and as they have no ambition, they do not care about the first places. Thus they enter paradise by the narrowest margin they can; provided they get there, that is enough for them: their aim is to do neither more nor less. These are people who snatch heaven rather than obtain it, and who say to God: Lord, I have fulfilled the conditions to the letter; you cannot help keeping your promises. Since I have done no more than you required, I release you from giving me more than you promised."

"So you see, sir, we are necessary men. But that is not all; you shall see something further. The deed does not make the crime; it is the knowledge of the person who commits it. He who does something wrong while he can still believe it not to be wrong is safe in conscience. And as there are an infinite number of equivocal actions, a casuist can give them a degree of goodness they do not possess merely by qualifying them as such; and provided he can persuade people that they contain no poison, he removes it from them entirely.

"I am telling you the secret of a profession in which I have grown old; I am showing you its refinements. Everything can be given a turn, even the things that seem least susceptible of it."

"Father," I said, "this is all very well; but how do you settle matters with Heaven? If the Great Sophy had at his court a man who did with regard to him what you do against your God, who distinguished among his commands, and who taught his subjects in what cases they must obey them and in what others they might violate them, he would have him impaled on the spot." At that I saluted my dervish and left him without waiting for his reply.

*At Paris, the 23rd day of the moon of Maharram, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the overall satire effectively: monastic vows are mocked, casuistry is explained as a technology for minimizing moral cost, and Usbek ends by condemning the whole enterprise in political terms.

### Main differences
- **Women of pleasure / courtesans**
  - Fresh translation: `women of pleasure`
  - Davidson: `courtesans`
  - Davidson is clear, but a little more decorous and socially specific than the broader French `filles de joie`.

- **Paradise `at the narrowest margin`**
  - French: `ils entrent en paradis le plus juste qu'ils peuvent`
  - Fresh translation: `they enter paradise by the narrowest margin they can`
  - Davidson: `they would enter Paradise as easily as possible`
  - This is a meaningful difference. The French stresses bare sufficiency, not ease.

- **Release from extra reward**
  - Fresh translation: `I release you from giving me more than you promised`
  - Davidson: `I expect no more from you than you have promised`
  - Davidson smooths the joke; the French is more insolent and contractual.

- **Knowledge and guilt**
  - Both versions preserve the core principle that the moral status of an act depends on the agent's knowledge.
  - The fresh translation stays somewhat closer to the lapidary French syntax.

- **`Poison` in equivocal actions**
  - French: `pourvu qu'il puisse persuader qu'elles n'ont pas de venin`
  - Fresh translation keeps the concrete metaphor of `poison`
  - Davidson turns this into `harmlessness` and `deadliness`, which is sensible but less vivid.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: anti-clerical, dryly comic, and increasingly savage as the casuist becomes more explicit.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the juridical and transactional language that makes the casuist's self-description so chillingly funny.
- **Davidson**: strong on the large movement of the passage, though sometimes a little smoother and more genteel than the French sting.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is usable, but it contains a visible typo in `a cheap a rate as s possible`.
- Capitalization in the title line is irregular in the OCR: `USBEK TO RheDI, AT VENICE.`
- Otherwise the letter is comparatively stable in both languages.

## Working conclusion
Letter LVII is one of Montesquieu's clearest satires of moral casuistry. Davidson preserves the architecture of the joke, but the fresh translation keeps closer to the French's sharper legalism, especially where salvation is treated as something one obtains by exact minimum compliance.
