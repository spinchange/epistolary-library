## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXXVI**

**Usbek to Mirza, at Ispahan**

### Translation
You know, Mirza, that some ministers of Shah Soliman once formed the design of forcing all the Armenians of Persia either to leave the kingdom or to become Muslims, thinking that our empire would remain polluted so long as it kept these infidels in its bosom.

Persian greatness would have been finished if blind devotion had been listened to on that occasion.

No one knows how the scheme failed. Neither those who proposed it nor those who rejected it understood its consequences. Chance performed the office of reason and policy, and saved the empire from a danger greater than the loss of three battles and the taking of two cities.

By proscribing the Armenians, people meant in a single day to destroy all the merchants, and almost all the artisans, in the kingdom. I am sure that the great Shah Abbas would sooner have had both his arms cut off than sign such an order; and that, by sending to the Mogol and the other kings of India his most industrious subjects, he would have thought he was giving them half his states.

The persecutions our zealous Muslims have inflicted on the Guebres have forced them to pass in crowds into India, and have deprived Persia of that laborious nation, so devoted to cultivation, which alone by its work could overcome the sterility of our lands.

Devotion had only one second blow left to strike: to ruin industry. Once that was done, the empire would have fallen of itself, and with it, by necessary consequence, the very religion that men wished to make so flourishing.

If one reasons without prejudice, Mirza, I do not know whether it may not be a good thing for a state to have several religions.

It is observed that those who live under tolerated religions ordinarily make themselves more useful to their country than those who live under the dominant religion. Kept away from honors, and able to distinguish themselves only by wealth, they are led to acquire it through labor and to embrace the most painful occupations in society.

Besides, since all religions contain precepts useful to society, it is good that they should be observed zealously. And what is more capable of animating that zeal than their multiplicity?

They are rivals that forgive one another nothing. Jealousy descends to private persons: each remains on guard, and fears doing anything that would dishonor his party and expose it to the contempt and unforgivable censures of the opposing side.

Thus it has always been noticed that a new sect introduced into a state is the surest means of correcting all the abuses of the old one.

People may say as much as they like that it is not in a prince's interest to permit several religions in his state. If all the sects in the world came together there, it would do him no harm, because there is none that does not prescribe obedience and preach submission.

I admit that histories are full of religious wars. But one must take care here: it is not the multiplicity of religions that produced those wars, but the spirit of intolerance animating the religion that believed itself dominant.

That is the spirit of proselytism, which the Jews took from the Egyptians, and which passed from them, like an epidemic and popular disease, to Muslims and Christians.

It is finally that spirit of vertigo whose progress can only be regarded as a total eclipse of human reason.

For after all, even if there were no inhumanity in afflicting the consciences of others, and even if none of the thousand evil effects that spring from it followed, one would still have to be mad to think of it. Whoever wants me to change religion surely does so only because he himself would not change his if someone tried to force him; he finds it strange, then, that I will not do a thing he would not do himself, perhaps not even for the empire of the world.

*Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Gemmadi I, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the line of argument well: economic damage from persecution, the usefulness of tolerated minorities, the rivalry of sects, and the claim that religious wars arise from intolerance rather than plurality itself.

### Main differences
- **Economic bluntness**
  - French says persecuting Armenians would destroy `all the merchants, and almost all the artisans` of the kingdom.
  - Fresh translation keeps the abruptness of that social and fiscal catastrophe.
  - Davidson preserves the sense, but with slightly less force.

- **Guebres and agriculture**
  - French stresses that this nation alone could, by labor, overcome the sterility of Persian lands.
  - Davidson is close, but my draft keeps the agricultural emphasis more sharply.

- **`On a beau dire`**
  - Fresh translation: `People may say as much as they like...`
  - Davidson: `It is sophistry to say...`
  - Davidson becomes more polemical than the French.

- **Final turn against coercion**
  - Davidson's close is good.
  - The French is especially effective because it turns the coercer's logic back on him in near-conversational form.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: argumentative, anti-fanatical, and politically concrete.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the political economy and civic logic in the foreground.
- **Davidson**: often faithful, but occasionally more abstract or more emphatic than the French.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXXVI is one of the clearest cases for fresh translation work, because Montesquieu's argument is not only about tolerance in principle: it is also about labor, commerce, agriculture, and state capacity. Davidson keeps most of that, but a fresh draft restores some of the sharper political economy.
