## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXVII**

**Ibben to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
My dear Usbek, it seems to me that, for a true Muslim, misfortunes are less punishments than warnings. Those are very precious days that lead us to expiate our offenses. It is the time of prosperity that ought to be shortened. What use are all these impatiences except to show that we would like to be happy independently of him who gives felicities, because he is felicity itself?

If a being is composed of two beings, and if the necessity of preserving their union marks greater submission to the Creator's commands, one may make of it a religious law; if this necessity of preserving the union is a better guarantee of human actions, one may make of it a civil law.

*Smyrna, the last day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson transmits the substance of Ibben's brief reply clearly: misfortune should be read as warning rather than punishment, and the duty to preserve life can be justified both religiously and civilly. The main issue here is less interpretive drift than the severe corruption of the English clean text.

### Main differences
- **Punishments vs warnings / threats**
  - French: `les malheurs sont moins des châtiments que des menaces`
  - Fresh translation: `misfortunes are less punishments than warnings`
  - Davidson: `misfortunes are not so much punishments as warnings`
  - Davidson is essentially right here; the only nuance is that `menaces` retains a sharper sense of threat.

- **`jours bien précieux`**
  - Fresh translation: `very precious days`
  - Davidson: `priceless days`
  - `Priceless` is elegant, though somewhat more elevated than the plain French phrase.

- **`les félicités`**
  - Fresh translation: `felicities`
  - Davidson regularizes the sentence into `happiness`.
  - The French repetition on `félicité` / `félicités` is more audible than in Davidson's smoother wording.

- **Normative force in the final sentence**
  - French: `on en a pu faire une loi religieuse ... une loi civile`
  - Fresh translation: `one may make of it a religious law ... a civil law`
  - Davidson: `it should be made a religious law ... it should be made a civil law`
  - Davidson strengthens the claim from possibility or justification to recommendation.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compact, corrective, and pious in a severe register.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the aphoristic brevity and the conceptual parallel between religious and civil law.
- **Davidson**: generally faithful in sense, but smoother and more explicitly prescriptive.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text is badly contaminated in the opening sentence by a stray fragment: `ferable, however, as it is an anticlimax...`.
- That intrusion appears to come from note matter carried over from the preceding letter.
- This letter is short enough that the corruption is easy to identify, but the raw file should still be preferred for any exact quotation.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXVII is comparatively straightforward, and Davidson's rendering is close; the real obstacle here is OCR contamination rather than deep translational divergence.
