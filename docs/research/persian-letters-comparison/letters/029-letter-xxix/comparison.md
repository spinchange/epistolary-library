## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXIX**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The pope is the head of the Christians. He is an old idol that they incense out of habit. He was once formidable even to princes themselves, for he deposed them as easily as our magnificent sultans depose the kings of Irimette and Georgia. But he is no longer feared. He calls himself the successor of one of the first Christians, whom they call Saint Peter; and it is certainly a rich succession, for he has immense treasures and a great country under his dominion.

The bishops are men of law who are subordinate to him, and under his authority they have two very different functions. When they are assembled, they make, like him, articles of faith; when they are by themselves, they have scarcely any other function than to dispense people from fulfilling the law. For you should know that the Christian religion is burdened with an infinity of very difficult practices; and, as it has been judged less easy to fulfill one's duties than to have bishops who dispense one from them, the latter course has been adopted for the public good. Thus, if one does not wish to keep Ramadan, if one does not wish to submit to the formalities of marriage, if one wants to break one's vows, if one wants to marry against the prohibitions of the law, sometimes even if one wants to go back on one's oath, one goes to the bishop or the pope, who immediately grants the dispensation.

The bishops do not make articles of faith on their own initiative. There is an infinite number of doctors, most of them dervishes, who stir up among themselves a thousand new questions concerning religion. They are left to dispute for a long time, and the war lasts until a decision comes and ends it.

So I can assure you that there has never been a kingdom in which there have been so many civil wars as in that of Christ.

Those who bring forward some new proposition are at first called heretics. Each heresy has its own name, which serves for those engaged in it as a sort of rallying word. But no one is a heretic unless he wants to be. One need only split the difference in half, and provide a distinction to those who accuse one of heresy; and, whatever the distinction may be, intelligible or not, it makes a man white as snow, and he may call himself orthodox.

What I tell you applies to France and Germany. For I have heard that in Spain and Portugal there are certain dervishes who do not understand raillery and who burn a man like straw. When one falls into the hands of those people, happy the man who has always prayed to God with little wooden beads in his hand, who has worn on his person two bits of cloth tied to two ribbons, and who has at some time been in a province called Galicia. Without that, a poor devil is in a very awkward position. He might swear like a pagan that he is orthodox, and they still might not agree about the qualities and burn him as a heretic. He might offer all the distinctions he pleased: no distinctions; he would be ashes before anyone had even thought of listening to him.

Other judges presume an accused man innocent; these always presume him guilty. In doubt, they make it a rule to decide on the side of severity, apparently because they think men wicked. Yet, on the other hand, they have so good an opinion of them that they never judge them capable of lying; for they accept the testimony of mortal enemies, women of bad life, and those who practice an infamous trade. In their sentence they pay a little compliment to those clothed in a shirt of sulfur, telling them that they are very sorry to see them so badly dressed, that they are gentle men, that they abhor bloodshed, and that they are in despair at having condemned them; but, to console themselves, they confiscate all the goods of these wretches for their own profit.

Happy the land inhabited by the children of the prophets! Such melancholy spectacles are unknown there. The holy religion that the angels brought there defends itself by its truth alone; it has no need of such violent means in order to maintain itself.

*From Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Chalval, 1712.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows Rica's anti-clerical satire quite closely. He preserves the broad sequence of papal authority, dispensations, doctrinal conflict, inquisitorial cruelty, and the final contrast with Islamic toleration. The main differences concern lexical precision and the degree to which the French keeps its satire juridical and administrative.

### Main differences
- **Old idol and ritual habit**
  - French: `C'est une vieille idole qu'on encense par habitude`
  - Fresh translation keeps the bluntness of `old idol` and `incense out of habit`.
  - Davidson's `kept venerable by custom` is elegant, but it softens the idolatry joke.

- **Bishops as `men of law`**
  - French: `Les évêques sont des gens de loi`
  - Fresh translation: `The bishops are men of law`
  - Davidson: `The bishops are the administrators under his rule`
  - Davidson loses the specifically legal-juridical coloring of the French.

- **On their own initiative**
  - French: `de leur propre mouvement`
  - Fresh translation: `on their own initiative`
  - Davidson: `for their own government`
  - This is a genuine mistranslation. The French is about who initiates doctrinal articles, not about governing themselves.

- **No one is a heretic unless he wants to be**
  - French: `n'est hérétique qui ne veut`
  - Fresh translation keeps the aphoristic bite.
  - Davidson's `no one need be a heretic against his will` is close, though a touch more expansive.

- **Giving a distinction**
  - French: `donner une distinction`
  - Fresh translation preserves the scholastic maneuver.
  - Davidson's `allege some scholastic subtlety` explains the point, but it interprets rather than translates.

- **The inquisitors' view of testimony**
  - French: `ils ne les jugent jamais capables de mentir`
  - Fresh translation keeps the irony sharp.
  - Davidson conveys it well, though with a slightly fuller explanatory rhythm.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dry, juridical, anti-clerical, and increasingly savage in its irony.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep both the administrative satire and the sudden brutality of the Spanish and Portuguese example.
- **Davidson**: generally strong, but sometimes too smooth where Montesquieu wants pointedly legal or scholastic language.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXIX is a solid comparison letter. Davidson preserves the overall satire well, but a fresh translation better keeps the French's legal vocabulary, doctrinal sarcasm, and the abrupt cruelty of the inquisitorial sections.
