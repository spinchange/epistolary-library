## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXXIV**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
If there is a God, my dear Rhedi, he must necessarily be just; for if he were not, he would be the worst and most imperfect of all beings.

Justice is a relation of fitness that truly exists between two things. That relation is always the same, whatever being considers it, whether God, an angel, or finally a man.

It is true that men do not always see these relations; and even when they do see them, they often turn away. Their own interest is always what they see most clearly. Justice raises its voice; but in the tumult of passions it is barely heard.

Men can commit injustices because it serves their interest, and because they would rather satisfy themselves than others. They always act by a return upon themselves. No one is wicked for nothing: there must be some determining reason, and that reason is always a reason of interest.

But it is impossible that God should ever do anything unjust. Once one supposes that he sees justice, one must also suppose that he follows it; for, needing nothing and being sufficient to himself, he would be the most wicked of all beings if he were wicked without any interest.

Thus, even if there were no God, we should still have to love justice; that is, we should strive to resemble that being of whom we have so noble an idea, and who, if he existed, would necessarily be just. Even if free from the yoke of religion, we ought not to be free from that of equity.

That, Rhedi, is what has made me think that justice is eternal and does not depend on human conventions; and even if it did depend on them, that would be a terrible truth from which one would have to hide oneself.

We are surrounded by men stronger than we are; they can harm us in a thousand ways, and three quarters of the time with impunity. What peace it is for us to know that in the heart of all these men there is an inner principle fighting on our behalf and shielding us from their enterprises.

Without that, we would live in continual fear. We would pass among men as among lions, and would never be sure for a moment of our life, our goods, or our honor.

All these thoughts arm me against those doctors who represent God as a being who tyrannically exercises his power; who make him act in ways we ourselves would not wish to act for fear of offending him; who load him with all the imperfections he punishes in us; and who, in their contradictory opinions, now portray him as wicked, now as one who hates and punishes evil.

When a man examines himself, what satisfaction to find that he has a just heart. That pleasure, severe though it is, ought to transport him: he sees his being as much above those who do not have it as he sees himself above tigers and bears. Yes, Rhedi, if I were sure always and inviolably to follow that equity I keep before my eyes, I would think myself the first of men.

*Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Gemmadi I, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson's version is close in overall sense and sequence. He preserves the movement from natural justice, to divine justice, to the inward dignity of a just heart, and he remains readable throughout.

### Main differences
- **Justice vs righteousness**
  - French consistently argues in terms of `justice` and `équité`.
  - Fresh translation keeps `justice` and `equity` so the letter stays ethical, juridical, and philosophical at once.
  - Davidson repeatedly prefers `righteousness`, which shifts the register toward devotional English.

- **`rapport de convenance`**
  - Fresh translation: `a relation of fitness`
  - Davidson: `a true relation`
  - Davidson is close, but the French stresses proportion or fittingness more explicitly.

- **Interest as motive**
  - French: `cette raison est toujours une raison d'intérêt`
  - Fresh translation preserves the repetition of `reason` and `interest`.
  - Davidson's `interested one` is accurate but smoother and less insistent.

- **The security supplied by justice**
  - French imagines social life without justice as life among lions.
  - Davidson keeps the image well, though my draft stays a little closer to the plain force of `notre vie, notre bien, ni notre honneur`.

- **Final self-estimate**
  - French: `je me croirois le premier des hommes`
  - Fresh translation: `I would think myself the first of men`
  - Davidson: `I would believe myself the best of men`
  - `The first of men` is prouder and more exact than `the best of men`.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: lucid, severe, and morally ambitious.
- **Fresh translation**: aims for clear modern philosophical English without adding sermonizing coloration.
- **Davidson**: stately and effective, but somewhat more devotional in register.

## Working conclusion
Davidson is broadly faithful in substance, but he slightly Christianizes the letter's vocabulary by preferring `righteousness` where Montesquieu is more abstractly judicial and philosophical. A fresh translation helps keep the conceptual backbone of the letter sharper.
