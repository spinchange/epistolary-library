## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XVII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
I cannot, divine mollak, calm my impatience. I cannot wait for your sublime answer. I have doubts, and they must be settled. I feel my reason going astray; bring it back into the right path. Come enlighten me, source of light. With your divine pen strike down the difficulties I am about to put before you. Make me pity myself, and blush at the question I am going to ask.

Why is it that our lawgiver deprives us of pork and of all the meats he calls unclean? Why does he forbid us to touch a dead body, and, to purify our soul, command us constantly to wash the body? It seems to me that things are in themselves neither pure nor impure. I cannot conceive any quality inherent in a thing that could make it so. Mud seems dirty to us only because it offends our sight or some other sense; in itself it is no more dirty than gold or diamonds. The idea of defilement contracted by touching a corpse comes to us only from a certain natural repugnance. If the bodies of those who do not wash offended neither smell nor sight, how could anyone imagine that they were impure?

So the senses, divine mollak, ought to be the only judges of the purity or impurity of things. But since objects do not affect all men in the same way, and what gives one man an agreeable sensation gives another a disgusting one, it follows that the testimony of the senses cannot serve here as a rule, unless one says that each man may decide the point at will and distinguish, for himself, pure things from impure ones.

But would not that itself, sacred mollak, overturn the distinctions established by our divine Prophet, and the fundamental points of the law written by the hands of angels?

*From Erzurum, the 20th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives a clear and generally faithful version of Usbek's skeptical argument about ritual purity. He follows the sequence of reasoning closely, but now and then trades the French's plain examples for more literary or philosophical wording.

### Main differences
- **Impatience and self-abasement**
  - French: `fais-moi pitié de moi-même`
  - Fresh translation: `Make me pity myself`
  - Davidson: `enable me to commiserate myself`
  - Davidson is close in sense, but the French is simpler and more abrupt.

- **Reason going astray**
  - French: `je sens que ma raison s'égare`
  - Fresh translation: `I feel my reason going astray`
  - Davidson: `I feel that my reason has gone astray`
  - Davidson preserves the meaning, though the French's present movement feels slightly more immediate.

- **Mud, not `filthiness of filth`**
  - French: `La boue ne nous paroît sale que parce qu'elle blesse notre vue`
  - Fresh translation: `Mud seems dirty to us only because it offends our sight`
  - Davidson: `The filthiness of filth consists in its offending our sight`
  - Davidson turns a concrete example into an abstract formulation.

- **Testimony of the senses**
  - French: `le témoignage des sens`
  - Fresh translation: `the testimony of the senses`
  - Davidson: `the witness of the senses`
  - The difference is minor, but the French remains slightly more juridical in tone.

- **At will / according to fancy**
  - French: `chacun peut à sa fantaisie décider ce point`
  - Fresh translation: `each man may decide the point at will`
  - Davidson: `each according to his fancy`
  - Davidson is close, though the French keeps a more formal argumentative structure.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: respectful in frame, but unmistakably skeptical and analytical.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the ceremonial deference alongside the rational critique.
- **Davidson**: lucid and mostly faithful, with only mild elevation of register.

## Working conclusion
Letter XVII is one of the cleaner early philosophical letters to compare. Davidson transmits the argument well, but his most notable tendency is to replace the French's plain examples and legalistic phrasing with slightly more polished English.
