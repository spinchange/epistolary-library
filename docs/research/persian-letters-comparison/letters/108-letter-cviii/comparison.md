## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CVIII**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
I have seen the young monarch; his life is very precious to his subjects; it is no less so to all Europe, because of the great troubles his death might produce. But kings are like the gods; and while they live, one must believe them immortal. His countenance is majestic but charming: a fine education seems to join with a happy natural disposition and already promises a great prince.

They say one can never know the character of the kings of the West until they have passed through the two great tests: their mistress and their confessor. Soon we shall see both at work trying to seize this one's mind; and for that purpose great battles will be fought. For under a young prince these two powers are always rivals; but they reconcile themselves and unite under an old one. Under a young prince, the dervish has a very difficult role to sustain: the king's strength makes his weakness; but the other triumphs equally through his weakness and his strength.

When I arrived in France, I found the late king wholly governed by women; and yet, at the age he had reached, I think he was the monarch on earth who had the least need of them. One day I heard a woman say: `Something must be done for that young colonel; I know his valor; I shall speak to the minister of him.` Another said: `It is astonishing that that young abbé has been overlooked; he must be made a bishop: he is a man of birth, and I could answer for his morals.` Yet you must not imagine that the women speaking this way were favorites of the prince; they perhaps had not spoken to him twice in their lives - though that is a very easy thing to manage with European princes. The point is that there is no one who holds any post at court, in Paris, or in the provinces, who does not have some woman through whose hands pass all the favors, and sometimes the injustices, he can do. All these women have connections with one another and form a kind of republic whose members, always active, aid and serve one another. It is like a new state within the state; and the man who is at court, in Paris, or in the provinces, who sees ministers, magistrates, and prelates at work, if he does not know the women who govern them, is like a man who sees a machine functioning but knows none of its springs.

Do you think, Ibben, that a woman decides to become a minister's mistress in order to sleep with him? What an idea! It is so that she may present him with five or six petitions every morning; and the goodness of their nature shows itself in the eagerness with which they do good to an infinity of unfortunate people who procure them a hundred thousand livres of income.

In Persia they complain that the kingdom is governed by two or three women: in France it is much worse, for women in general govern, and they take not only the whole of authority in the mass, but even divide it out among themselves in detail.

*From Paris, the last day of the moon of Chalval, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson carries the main satire well: the young king's future is shaped by the struggle between mistress and confessor, while actual political favors circulate through a female patronage network that operates as a state within the state.

### Main differences
- **Mistress and confessor as `tests`**
  - French: `les deux grandes épreuves, de leur maîtresse, et de leur confesseur`
  - Fresh translation keeps the sharper idea that these are the two ordeals by which Western kings are known.
  - Davidson's `ordeals of selecting their mistress and their confessor` is readable, but slightly more explanatory.

- **The king's strength as weakness**
  - Fresh translation stays close to the paradox `la force du roi fait sa foiblesse`.
  - Davidson is close in wording, but the sentence is badly interrupted by OCR debris in the clean text.

- **Patronage through women**
  - French: `il n'y a personne ... qui n'ait une femme par les mains de laquelle passent toutes les grâces`
  - Fresh translation preserves the stronger image of favors literally passing through women's hands.
  - Davidson's `acquainted with some woman` softens the mechanism.

- **Why a mistress?**
  - French sarcasm: `pour coucher avec lui? Quelle idée!`
  - Fresh translation keeps the blunt cynicism of `in order to sleep with him?`
  - Davidson's `for love of him?` softens the satire.

- **Authority shared `in detail`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the final contrast between taking power `in the mass` and dividing it out `in detail`.
  - Davidson is good here, but slightly more polished than the French.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: courtly, satirical, and mechanically precise in the way it maps hidden patronage.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve both the wit and the cold explanatory tone.
- **Davidson**: effective overall, but somewhat softened in the sharper sexual and administrative sarcasm.

## Working conclusion
Davidson preserves the broad political satire of Letter CVIII, but a fresh translation is useful for the harsher edges of Montesquieu's analysis: women do not merely influence power, they route it, retail it, and profit from it.
