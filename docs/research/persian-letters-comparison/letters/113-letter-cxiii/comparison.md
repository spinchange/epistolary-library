## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXIII**

**Rhedi to Usbek, at Paris**

### Translation
During my stay in Europe I read ancient and modern historians; I compare all times; I take pleasure in seeing them pass, so to speak, before me; and I dwell above all on those great changes that have made one age so different from another, and the earth so unlike itself.

Perhaps you have not paid attention to something that surprises me every day. How is the world so thinly peopled, compared with what it once was? How could nature have lost that prodigious fecundity of the earliest times? Is she already in her old age, and falling into languor?

I spent more than a year in Italy, where I saw nothing but the wreck of that ancient Italy once so famous. Although everyone lives in the cities, they are utterly deserted and depopulated: they seem to survive only to mark the sites of those powerful cities of which history has spoken so much.

There are people who claim that the city of Rome alone once contained more people than the greatest kingdom of Europe has today. There were Roman citizens who owned ten and even twenty thousand slaves, not counting those who worked on country estates; and since four or five hundred thousand citizens were counted there, one cannot fix the number of its inhabitants without the imagination revolting.

Sicily once had powerful kingdoms and numerous peoples, which have since disappeared: that island has nothing considerable left but its volcanoes.

Greece is so deserted that it does not contain the hundredth part of its ancient inhabitants.

Spain, once so full, now shows only uninhabited countryside; and France is nothing compared with that ancient Gaul of which Caesar speaks.

The countries of the North are very thinly peopled; and their peoples are far from being, as formerly, obliged to divide themselves and send out colonies and whole nations like swarms to seek new dwellings.

Poland and Turkey in Europe scarcely have peoples any longer.

One could not find in America the two-hundredth part of the men who formed such great empires there.

Asia is hardly in better condition. That Asia Minor which contained so many powerful monarchies and such a prodigious number of great cities now has only two or three of them left. As for greater Asia, the part subjected to the Turk is no fuller; and as for that under the dominion of our kings, if it is compared with the flourishing state it was once in, one will see that it possesses only a very small part of the inhabitants who were numberless there in the time of Xerxes and Darius.

As for the small states around those great empires, they are truly deserts: such are the kingdoms of Irimette, Circassia, and Guriel. All those princes, with vast states, can barely count fifty thousand subjects.

Egypt has not been spared any more than the other countries.

In short, I survey the earth and find nothing but dilapidation: I think I see it emerging from the ravages of plague and famine.

Africa has always been so little known that one cannot speak of it as precisely as the other parts of the world; but if one attends only to the Mediterranean coasts, known at all times, one sees that it has declined enormously from what it was when it was a Roman province. Today its princes are so weak that they are the smallest powers in the world.

After a calculation as exact as one can make in such matters, I have found that there are scarcely the fiftieth part of the men on earth who were here in Caesar's time. What is astonishing is that it is depopulating every day; and if this continues, in ten centuries it will be nothing but a desert.

There, my dear Usbek, is the most terrible catastrophe that has ever happened in the world; but it has scarcely been noticed, because it came about imperceptibly and in the course of a great many centuries. That marks an inner vice, a secret and hidden poison, a disease of languor afflicting human nature.

*From Venice, the 10th day of the moon of Rhegeb, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the overall argument of the letter: Rhedi imagines the whole globe to be undergoing long-term depopulation and supports that thesis with a sweeping tour through Italy, Greece, Spain, Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

### Main differences
- **Opening surprise and footnote intrusion**
  - The English clean text is badly broken near the start of the second paragraph by a stray note from the previous letter.
  - The French progression of thought is still clear, but Davidson's printed text should be checked against a cleaner witness there.

- **America: `deux-centième partie`**
  - French: `la deux-centième partie`
  - Fresh translation keeps the striking claim that America has not even a two-hundredth part of its former population.
  - Davidson changes this to `the fiftieth part`, which is a major numerical drift.

- **Africa under Rome**
  - French: `lorsqu'elle étoit province romaine`
  - Fresh translation keeps the narrower historical reference to Africa as a Roman province.
  - Davidson expands this to `under the Carthaginians and the Romans`, which is not what the sentence says.

- **The earth's total population**
  - French: `à peine ... la cinquantième partie des hommes qui y étoient du temps de César`
  - Fresh translation keeps the much more extreme `fiftieth part`.
  - Davidson changes this to `one tenth part`, another substantial factual difference.

- **`délabrement` and `langueur`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the vocabulary of `dilapidation` and `languor`, which fits the medicalized vision of civilizational decay.
  - Davidson often prefers a slightly smoother nineteenth-century idiom.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: speculative, sweeping, and rhetorically grave; it turns demographic fantasy into a kind of global diagnosis.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the cumulative, almost obsessive movement of the survey and the medical imagery at the end.
- **Davidson**: readable and often effective, but much less reliable here on numerical particulars.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text has a severe footnote intrusion near the start of the second paragraph and a line-break split in the Spain passage (`unin- / habited`).
- More importantly, this letter contains multiple substantive numerical differences that are not mere OCR noise.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXIII is one of the more important comparison cases in this batch. Davidson transmits the general idea, but he introduces at least two major numerical shifts (`1/200` -> `1/50`, `1/50` -> `1/10`) and broadens one historical reference to Africa. This is exactly the kind of letter where a fresh translation materially improves scholarly reliability.
