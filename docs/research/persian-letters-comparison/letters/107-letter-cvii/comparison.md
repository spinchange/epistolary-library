## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CVII**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
Either you do not think what you say, or you act better than you think. You left your country to instruct yourself, and you despise all instruction; you come to form yourself in a country where the fine arts are cultivated, and you regard them as harmful. Shall I tell you, Rhedi? I am more in agreement with you than you are with yourself.

Have you reflected properly on the barbarous and unhappy condition into which the loss of the arts would drag us? It is not necessary to imagine it; one can see it. There are still peoples on earth among whom a moderately well-trained monkey could live with honor; he would be roughly on a level with the other inhabitants: no one would find his wit peculiar or his character bizarre; he would pass like anyone else, and might even be distinguished for his prettiness.

You say that the founders of empires have almost all been ignorant of the arts. I do not deny that barbarous peoples may, like impetuous torrents, have spread across the earth and covered the best-policed kingdoms with their savage armies. But observe: they learned the arts, or had the conquered peoples practice them; without that, their power would have passed like the sound of thunder and storms.

You fear, you say, that some more cruel means of destruction than the ones in use may be invented. No: if a fatal invention came to light, it would soon be prohibited by the law of nations, and the unanimous consent of nations would bury that discovery. It is not in the interest of princes to make conquests by such means: they must seek subjects, not lands.

You complain of the invention of gunpowder and bombs; you think it strange that there are no longer any impregnable places: that is to say, you think it strange that wars are ended more quickly today than they were formerly.

You must have noticed, in reading histories, that since the invention of gunpowder battles are much less bloody than they once were, because there is almost no hand-to-hand fighting anymore.

And even if there were some particular case in which an art had been harmful, should it for that reason be rejected? Do you think, Rhedi, that the religion our holy Prophet brought down from heaven is harmful because one day it will serve to confound perfidious Christians?

You think that the arts soften peoples, and thus cause the fall of empires. You speak of the ruin of the ancient Persians, which resulted from their softness; but that example is far from decisive, since the Greeks, who subjugated them, cultivated the arts with infinitely more care than they did.

When people say that the arts make men effeminate, they are certainly not speaking of the people who apply themselves to them, since such people are never idle - and idleness, of all vices, is the one that most weakens courage.

So the question concerns only those who enjoy their products. But in a civilized country, since those who enjoy the conveniences of one art are obliged to cultivate another, unless they are willing to be reduced to shameful poverty, it follows that idleness and softness are incompatible with the arts.

Paris is perhaps the most sensual city in the world, and the one in which pleasure is refined the most; but it is perhaps also the city where life is hardest. For one man to live deliciously, a hundred others must labor without rest. A woman takes it into her head that she must appear at an assembly in a certain adornment; from that moment fifty artisans no longer sleep, and no longer have leisure to drink or eat: she commands, and she is obeyed more promptly than our monarch would be, because self-interest is the greatest monarch on earth.

This ardor for work, this passion for enriching oneself, passes from rank to rank, from artisans up to great men. No one likes to be poorer than the person he has just seen immediately below him. In Paris you see a man who has enough to live on until Judgment Day working without ceasing and running the risk of shortening his life in order to amass, as he says, enough to live on.

The same spirit seizes the whole nation; one sees nothing there but work and industry: where, then, is this effeminate people you speak of so much?

Suppose, Rhedi, that a kingdom allowed only the arts absolutely necessary for the cultivation of the land, though these are already numerous, and banished all those that serve only pleasure or fancy. I maintain that such a state would be the most miserable in the world.

Even if the inhabitants had enough hardihood to go without so many things required by their needs, the people would waste away every day, and the state would become so weak that no power would be too small to conquer it.

I could go into long detail here and show you that the revenues of private persons would almost entirely cease, and consequently those of the prince as well. There would be almost no proportional relation of means among citizens; that circulation of wealth and that progression of incomes, arising from the dependence of the arts upon one another, would come to a complete stop; each person would draw income only from his land, and would draw from it only what was needed not to die of hunger. But since that is not the hundredth part of a kingdom's revenue, the number of inhabitants would have to diminish proportionally, until only the hundredth part remained.

Pay close attention to how far the revenues of industry extend. A fund yields its owner annually only a twentieth part of its value; but with a pistole's worth of color, a painter will make a picture worth fifty. The same may be said of goldsmiths, workers in wool and silk, and artisans of every sort.

From all this one must conclude, Rhedi, that for a prince to be powerful, his subjects must live in delights; he must labor to procure them every sort of superfluity with as much care as the necessities of life.

*From Paris, the 14th day of the moon of Chalval, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures the broad structure of Usbek's reply well: he grants some of Rhedi's unease about luxury, but argues that the arts sustain power, labor, circulation of wealth, and even a less murderous form of warfare.

### Main differences
- **Monkey image**
  - French: `un singe passablement instruit pourroit vivre avec honneur`
  - Fresh translation keeps the deliberately absurd image of a monkey living `with honor` among backward peoples.
  - Davidson's `with credit` is close, but a little milder.

- **Passing power of conquerors**
  - French: `comme le bruit du tonnerre et des tempêtes`
  - Fresh translation preserves both `thunder` and `storms`.
  - Davidson condenses this into `the noise of a thunderstorm`, which is effective but less expansive.

- **Less bloody battles**
  - French: `il n'y a presque plus de mêlée`
  - Fresh translation makes the point concrete as the near disappearance of hand-to-hand fighting.
  - Davidson's `the armies are seldom intermingled` is accurate, though slightly less vivid.

- **Economic arithmetic**
  - French says the kingdom would be reduced to `la centième partie` of its revenue and inhabitants.
  - Davidson changes this to `a twentieth`, which is a substantial factual drift.

- **Delights vs luxury**
  - French: `il faut que ses sujets vivent dans les délices`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stronger phrase `live in delights`.
  - Davidson's `live in luxury` is plausible, but slightly more moralized and less sensuous.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: argumentative, witty, and surprisingly hard-headed in its economic reasoning.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep both the satire and the long practical defense of labor, circulation, and superfluity.
- **Davidson**: readable and often strong, but he occasionally smooths images and introduces notable numerical drift.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text remains usable, but it preserves a visible line break corruption around `effeminate/` in the middle of the letter.
- The major issue here is not OCR so much as Davidson's `hundredth` -> `twentieth` numerical shift.

## Working conclusion
Letter CVII is important because Davidson is broadly faithful while still introducing one very significant factual change in the economic arithmetic. A fresh translation also preserves Montesquieu's mixture of satire, political economy, and deliberately provocative praise of `superfluities`.
