## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LVI**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
Gaming is much in use in Europe: to be a gambler is a profession in itself. That title alone stands in place of birth, wealth, and probity: it puts every man who bears it among the ranks of respectable people, without inquiry, although there is no one who does not know that in judging so they have very often been mistaken. But people have agreed to remain incorrigible.

Women above all are greatly addicted to it. It is true that in their youth they seldom give themselves to it except in order to favor a dearer passion; but, as they grow older, their passion for play seems to grow young again, and fills all the emptiness left by the others.

They want to ruin their husbands; and to achieve that they have resources for every age, from the tenderest youth to the most decrepit old age: clothes and carriages begin the disorder, coquetry increases it, gaming completes it.

I have often seen nine or ten women — or rather nine or ten centuries — seated around a table. I have seen them in their hopes, their fears, their joys, and above all in their rages. You would have said they would never have time to calm themselves, and that life would leave them before their despair did; you would have hesitated to decide whether the people they were paying were their creditors or their legatees.

It seems that our holy Prophet especially intended to deprive us of everything that can disturb our reason. He forbade us the use of wine, which buries it; by an express precept he also forbade games of chance; and when it was impossible for him to remove the causes of passion, he damped them down. Love among us brings neither turmoil nor frenzy: it is a languid passion that leaves our soul in peace. The plurality of wives saves us from their empire; it tempers the violence of our desires.

*At Paris, the 10th day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the structure of the letter closely and preserves its blend of social satire and Persian self-justification. He is especially good at carrying the sharp comic image of elderly female gamblers transformed into `nine or ten centuries` around a gaming table.

### Main differences
- **`Être joueur` as social rank**
  - Fresh translation: `to be a gambler is a profession in itself`
  - Davidson: `To be a gamester is to have a position in society`
  - Davidson makes the implication explicit, while the French is more acidly compressed: gambling itself becomes a social estate.

- **Birth, wealth, probity**
  - French: `ce seul titre tient lieu de naissance, de bien, de probité`
  - Fresh translation keeps the triplet `birth, wealth, and probity`
  - Davidson paraphrases the list as `well-born, wealthy, nor a man of integrity`, which is accurate but somewhat more explanatory.

- **`Habits et équipages`**
  - Fresh translation: `clothes and carriages begin the disorder`
  - Davidson: `dress and luxury begin the disorder`
  - `Équipages` is more concrete than `luxury`; it points to visible social display.

- **Reason buried by wine**
  - French: `le vin, qui la tient ensevelie`
  - Fresh translation: `wine, which buries it`
  - Davidson: `wine, which steals away man's brains`
  - Davidson is vivid, but it is more idiomatic and less faithful to the image of reason kept buried.

- **The empire of women**
  - Fresh translation: `the plurality of wives saves us from their empire`
  - Davidson: `saves us from the dominion of women`
  - Davidson is close here; the main difference is mostly one of register.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, mocking, and aphoristic; it moves easily from Parisian observation to Islamic self-defense.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the short, cutting social judgments and the strange blend of satire and apologetics.
- **Davidson**: usually faithful, though slightly more expansive and Victorian in phrasing.

## Working conclusion
Letter LVI is compact but pointed: Europe is figured as a place where gambling confers social legitimacy, and Usbek closes by contrasting that disorder with a self-serving idealization of Muslim moderation. Davidson carries the argument well, while the fresh translation keeps closer to the French's tight phrasing and concrete images.
