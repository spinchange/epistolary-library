## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXIX**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
Most legislators have been narrow men, whom chance placed at the head of others and who consulted almost nothing but their prejudices and whims.

It seems that they failed to recognize the greatness and even the dignity of their work: they amused themselves by making childish institutions, with which they did indeed suit petty minds, but discredited themselves with sensible people.

They got lost in useless details; they indulged in particular cases: a mark of a narrow genius that sees things only in parts and grasps nothing in a general view.

Some affected using a language other than the vulgar tongue; an absurd thing in a lawgiver: how can laws be observed if they are not known?

They often abolished without necessity those they found already established; that is to say, they threw peoples into the disorders inseparable from changes.

It is true that, by a peculiarity arising more from human nature than from the human mind, it is sometimes necessary to change certain laws. But the case is rare; and when it occurs, one should touch them only with a trembling hand: so many solemnities should be observed and so many precautions employed that the people will naturally conclude that laws are indeed sacred, since so many formalities are needed to repeal them.

They have often made laws too subtle, and followed logical ideas rather than natural equity. In time those laws were found too harsh; and, in a spirit of fairness, people thought it necessary to depart from them: but that remedy was a new evil. Whatever laws may be, they must always be followed, and regarded as the public conscience, to which the conscience of private persons must always conform.

Yet one must admit that some among them showed a concern that marks considerable wisdom: they gave fathers great authority over their children. Nothing relieves magistrates more; nothing empties the tribunals more; nothing, finally, spreads more tranquility in a state, where morals always make better citizens than laws.

Of all powers, this is the one least abused; it is the most sacred of all magistracies; it is the only one that does not depend on conventions, and indeed preceded them.

It is observed that in countries where more rewards and punishments are placed in paternal hands, families are better ordered: fathers are the image of the creator of the universe who, although he can guide men by love, still binds them by motives of hope and fear.

I will not finish this letter without making you notice the oddity of the French mind. They say that the French have retained from Roman law an infinite number of useless things, and even worse; and yet they did not take from it paternal power, which it established as the first legitimate authority.

*Paris, the 18th day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the argumentative movement of the letter quite well: bad legislators are narrow, excessive change is dangerous, law should be public and stable, and paternal authority is praised as a primary social power. His rendering is generally strong, though it often prefers smoother English abstractions to the sharper rhetoric of the French.

### Main differences
- **Opening characterization of legislators**
  - French: `des hommes bornés`
  - Fresh translation: `narrow men`
  - Davidson: `men of inferior capacity`
  - Davidson is intelligible, but `bornés` suggests limitation and narrowness more than simple lack of ability.

- **From details to particular cases**
  - French: `Ils se sont jetés dans des détails inutiles; ils ont donné dans les cas particuliers`
  - Fresh translation keeps both useless detail and obsession with particular cases.
  - Davidson's `individual interests` is looser than the French.

- **Changing laws with `une main tremblante`**
  - Fresh translation: `with a trembling hand`
  - Davidson: `it requires the most delicate handling`
  - Davidson conveys caution, but the image of fear and reverence in the French is more vivid.

- **Public conscience**
  - French: `la conscience publique`
  - Davidson: `the public conscience`
  - Davidson is close here; the main difference is that the fresh version keeps the sentence's more peremptory cadence.

- **Final sentence on Roman law**
  - French: `la puissance paternelle, qu'elles ont établie comme la première autorité légitime`
  - Fresh translation: `paternal power, which it established as the first legitimate authority`
  - Davidson's wording (`which they have established as the source of all lawful authority`) is more expansive and slightly less exact.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compressed political reflection, severe but lucid.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the aphoristic edge and the reverential language about law.
- **Davidson**: often very good, but a little more essayistic and generalized in phrasing.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXIX is one of the cleaner and more straightforward comparisons in this batch: Davidson follows the argument closely, but the fresh translation preserves more of Montesquieu's compact imagery - especially the trembling hand that should touch law only rarely.
