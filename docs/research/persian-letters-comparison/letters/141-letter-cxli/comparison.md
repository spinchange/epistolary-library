### Translation
I shall come to see you toward the end of the week: how pleasantly the days will pass with you!

A few days ago I was presented to a lady of the court who had some desire to see my foreign face. I found her beautiful, worthy of our monarch's gaze, and of an august rank in the sacred place where his heart rests.

She asked me a thousand questions about the customs of the Persians, and the way Persian women live. It seemed to me that the life of the seraglio was not to her taste, and that she felt some repugnance at seeing one man divided among ten or twelve women. She could not look without envy on the happiness of the one, nor without pity on the condition of the others. As she loves reading, especially poets and novels, she wished me to speak to her of ours. What I told her about them only doubled her curiosity; she asked me to have a fragment of some of those I had brought translated for her. I did so, and a few days later I sent her a Persian tale: perhaps you will be pleased to see it in disguise.

In the time of Sheikh-Ali-Khan, there was in Persia a woman named Zulema. She knew the whole holy Koran by heart; there was not a dervish who understood the traditions of the holy prophets better than she; the Arab doctors had said nothing so mysterious that she did not grasp all its meanings; and she added to so much learning a kind of playful wit that made it hard to guess whether she meant to amuse those who heard her or to instruct them.

One day, when she was with her companions in one of the halls of the seraglio, one of them asked what she thought of the next life, and whether she gave credence to that ancient tradition of our doctors that paradise is made only for men.

`That is the common opinion,' she said to them. `Nothing has been left undone to degrade our sex. There is even a nation spread throughout Persia, called the Jewish nation, which maintains, on the authority of its sacred books, that we have no soul.

`Those opinions, so insulting, have no other source than the pride of men, who want to carry their superiority beyond life itself, and do not consider that, on the great day, all creatures will appear before God as nothing, and that there will be among them no prerogatives except those that virtue has placed there.

`God will not set limits to his rewards. And just as men who have lived well and have made good use of the power they possess over us here below will dwell in a paradise full of heavenly and ravishing beauties, such that, if a mortal had seen them, he would at once kill himself in impatience to enjoy them, so virtuous women will go to a place of delights, where they will be intoxicated by a torrent of pleasures with divine men who will be subject to them. Each of them will have a seraglio in which those men will be shut up, with eunuchs even more faithful than ours to guard them.

`I have read,' she added, `in an Arabic book that there was once a man named Ibrahim whose jealousy was unbearable. He had twelve exceedingly beautiful wives, and he treated them very harshly. He no longer trusted either his eunuchs or the walls of his seraglio. He kept them almost always under lock and key, shut up in their rooms, unable to see or speak to one another; for he was jealous even of an innocent friendship. All his actions took on the color of his natural brutality. Never did a gentle word come out of his mouth, and never did he make the slightest sign that did not add something to the harshness of their slavery.

`One day, when he had gathered them all in one room of his seraglio, one of them, bolder than the rest, reproached him for his evil nature. When one seeks so hard for ways to make oneself feared, she said to him, one always finds first the means to make oneself hated. We are so unhappy that we cannot help wishing for a change. Others in my place would wish for your death; I wish only for my own. And since I can hope to be separated from you only in that way, it will still be sweet to me to be separated by it. This speech, which ought to have touched him, threw him into a furious rage. He drew his dagger and plunged it into her breast. My dear companions, she said in a dying voice, if heaven has pity on my virtue, you will be avenged. With these words she left that unfortunate life to go to the abode of delights, where women who have lived well enjoy a happiness forever renewed.

`At first she saw a smiling meadow, whose greenery was heightened by the colors of the brightest flowers. A stream, whose waters were purer than crystal, wound through it in innumerable turns. She then entered charming groves, whose silence was broken only by the gentle song of birds. Magnificent gardens appeared next: nature had adorned them with all her simplicity and all her splendor. At last she found a superb palace prepared for her and filled with heavenly men destined for her pleasures.

`Two of them at once came forward to undress her; others put her into the bath and perfumed her with the most delicious essences. She was then given clothes infinitely richer than her own; after that she was led into a great hall, where she found a fire made of fragrant woods and a table covered with the most exquisite foods. Everything seemed to conspire to ravish her senses. On one side she heard music all the more divine for being more tender; on the other she saw only the dances of those divine men, wholly occupied with pleasing her. Yet all these pleasures were meant only to lead her by degrees to pleasures still greater. She was taken to her chamber; and after being undressed once more, she was carried to a splendid bed, where two men of charming beauty received her in their arms. It was then that she grew intoxicated, and that her raptures exceeded even her desires. I am wholly beside myself, she said to them; I would think I were dying, if I were not sure of my immortality. It is too much - leave me; I am sinking under the violence of pleasures. Yes, you restore a little calm to my senses; I begin to breathe and to come back to myself. Why have they taken away the lights? Why can I not now behold your divine beauty? Why can I not see... But why see? You are carrying me back into my first transports. O gods, how lovable this darkness is! What! I shall be immortal, and immortal with you! I shall be... No, I beg you for mercy, for I can see well enough that you are people who would never ask any.

`After several commands repeated more than once, she was obeyed; but only when she truly wished to be. She rested languidly and fell asleep in their arms. Two moments of sleep repaired her weariness. She received two kisses that suddenly inflamed her and made her open her eyes. I am uneasy, she said; I fear that you no longer love me. It was a doubt in which she had no wish to remain long; accordingly she obtained from them all the clarification she could desire. I am undeceived, she cried out; pardon, pardon; I am sure of you. You say nothing to me, but you prove it better than all you might say. Yes, yes, I confess it to you: one has never been so much loved. But what! you both dispute the honor of persuading me! Ah! if you quarrel, if you join ambition to the pleasure of my defeat, I am lost; you will both be victors, and there will be no one defeated but me; but I will make you pay dearly for your victory.

`All this was interrupted only by daylight. Her faithful and charming servants entered her chamber and made those two young men rise, and two old men led them back to the places where they were kept for her pleasure. Then she rose in turn and appeared first before that idolatrous court in the charms of a simple undress, and afterward covered with the richest ornaments. That night had beautified her; it had given life to her complexion and expression to her graces. All day there was nothing but dances, concerts, feasts, games, and promenades; and people noticed that Anais slipped away from time to time and flew back to her two young heroes. After a few precious instants of meeting, she returned to the company she had left, always with a face more serene than before. At last, toward evening, she disappeared altogether. She went to shut herself up in the seraglio, where, she said, she wished to make the acquaintance of those immortal captives who were to live forever with her. She therefore visited the apartments in those deepest and most charming places, where she counted fifty slaves of miraculous beauty. She wandered all night from room to room, receiving everywhere homages always different and always the same.

`That is how the immortal Anais passed her life, now in dazzling pleasures, now in solitary pleasures; admired by a brilliant throng, or loved by a frantic lover. Often she left an enchanted palace to go to a rustic grotto. Flowers seemed to spring up beneath her steps, and pleasures crowded before her.

`She had been in that happy dwelling for more than eight days, and, always outside herself, had not made a single reflection. She had enjoyed her happiness without knowing it, and without having had one of those tranquil moments in which the soul, so to speak, gives an account of itself to itself and listens to itself in the silence of the passions.

`The blessed have pleasures so vivid that they can rarely enjoy that freedom of mind. That is why, invincibly attached to present objects, they entirely lose the memory of past things and no longer have any concern for what they knew or loved in the other life.

`But Anais, whose spirit was truly philosophical, had spent almost all her life in meditation. She had carried her reflections much further than one would have expected from a woman left to herself. The austere seclusion in which her husband had kept her had left her only that advantage. It was that strength of mind that had made her despise the fear by which her companions had been struck, and death, which was to be the end of her pains and the beginning of her happiness.

`So she gradually came out of the intoxication of pleasure and shut herself up alone in an apartment of her palace. She gave herself over to very gentle reflections on her former condition and her present happiness. She could not help softening at the misfortune of her companions: one remains sensitive to torments that one has shared. Anais did not stay within the narrow bounds of compassion. Tenderer toward those unhappy women, she felt herself drawn to help them.

`She ordered one of the young men beside her to take on her husband's appearance; to go to his seraglio and make himself master of it; to drive him out; and to remain there in his place until she recalled him.

`The execution was swift. He cleft the air, arrived at the gate of Ibrahim's seraglio, where Ibrahim himself was not present. He knocked, and everything was opened to him. The eunuchs fell at his feet. He flew toward the apartments where Ibrahim's wives were confined. As he passed, he had taken the keys from the pocket of that jealous man, to whom he had rendered himself invisible. He entered and surprised them first by his gentle and gracious manner, and soon after surprised them even more by his eagerness and by the speed of his undertakings. Each of them had her share of astonishment; and they would have taken it for a dream if there had been a little less reality in it.

`While these new scenes were being played in the seraglio, Ibrahim pounded, named himself, stormed, and shouted. After meeting many difficulties, he got in and threw the eunuchs into extreme confusion. He strode forward; but he fell back and seemed stunned out of the heavens when he saw the false Ibrahim, his very image, exercising all the liberties of a master. He shouted for help and wanted the eunuchs to help him kill this impostor; but he was not obeyed. He had only one feeble resource left: to submit the matter to the judgment of his wives. Within an hour the false Ibrahim had won over all his judges. The real one was driven out and shamefully dragged from the seraglio, and he would have received death a thousand times if his rival had not ordered that his life be spared. At last the new Ibrahim, left master of the field of battle, showed himself more and more worthy of such a choice and distinguished himself by miracles hitherto unknown. You do not resemble Ibrahim, those women said. Say rather that that impostor does not resemble me, said the triumphant Ibrahim; what must one do to be your husband, if what I do is not enough?

`Ah! we are in no danger of doubting it, said the women. If you are not Ibrahim, it is enough for us that you have so well deserved to be him. You are more Ibrahim in one day than he has been in the course of ten years. Then you promise me, he replied, that you will declare in my favor against that impostor? Do not doubt it, they said in one voice. We swear you an eternal fidelity. We have been deceived only too long. The traitor did not suspect our virtue; he suspected only his own weakness. We can see very well that men are not made like him; it is you, no doubt, that they resemble. If you knew how much you make us hate him! Ah! I will often give you new reasons to hate him, replied the false Ibrahim; you do not yet know all the harm he has done you. We judge his injustice by the greatness of your vengeance, they replied. Yes, you are right, said the divine man; I have measured the expiation to the crime. I am glad that you are pleased with my way of punishing. But, said those women, if that impostor returns, what shall we do? It would be difficult, I think, for him to deceive you, he answered. In the place I occupy with you, one does not maintain oneself very well by trickery. Besides, I will send him so far away that you will hear no more of him. Then I will take upon myself the care of your happiness. I shall not be jealous. I shall know how to be sure of you without constraining you. I have a high enough opinion of my own merit to believe that you will be faithful to me. If you were not virtuous with me, with whom would you be?

`This conversation lasted a long time between him and those women, who, struck more by the difference between the two Ibrahims than by their resemblance, did not even think of asking for so many marvels to be explained to them. At last the desperate husband came back once more to trouble them. He found his whole house in rejoicing, and the women more incredulous than ever. That place was not tenable for a jealous man. He withdrew in rage, and a moment later the false Ibrahim followed him, seized him, carried him through the air two thousand leagues away, and dropped him there.

`O gods! in what desolation those women found themselves during the absence of their dear Ibrahim! Their eunuchs had already resumed their natural severity; the whole house was in tears; sometimes they imagined that everything that had happened to them had only been a dream. They looked at one another and recalled the smallest circumstances of those strange adventures. At last Ibrahim returned, always more lovable; it seemed to them that his journey had not been difficult. The new master adopted a conduct so opposite to that of the other that it astonished all the neighbors. He dismissed all the eunuchs, made his house accessible to everyone, and would not even allow his wives to veil themselves. It was a singular thing to see them at feasts among men, as free as they were. Ibrahim rightly believed that the customs of the country were not made for citizens like him. Yet he spared himself no expense: he squandered with immense profusion the jealous husband's goods, and when that husband returned three years later from the distant lands to which he had been carried off, he found nothing left but his wives and thirty-six children.'

*Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Gemmadi I, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson conveys the large shape of the letter very well: courtly curiosity, a translated Persian tale, feminist inversion of paradise, erotic fantasy, philosophical reflection, and comic revenge against the jealous husband. He is especially effective at keeping the narrative legible despite its length and rapid tonal shifts.

### Main differences
- **One man `partagé` among many women**
  - French: `voir un homme partagé entre dix ou douze femmes`
  - Fresh translation: `seeing one man divided among ten or twelve women`
  - Davidson: `the idea of one man's being shared among ten or a dozen women`
  - Davidson smooths the syntax; the French is stranger and more literal, and the oddity matters.

- **Women having `no soul`**
  - French: `nous n'avons point d'âme`
  - Fresh translation: `we have no soul`
  - Davidson: `we have no souls`
  - Davidson pluralizes and normalizes the claim; the French singular is sharper and more absolute.

- **The rhetoric of pleasure**
  - French: `un torrent de voluptés`
  - Fresh translation: `a torrent of pleasures`
  - Davidson: `surfeited with a torrent of pleasure`
  - Davidson is vivid, but he adds `surfeited`, which colors the passage more heavily than the French.

- **`Je suis toute hors de moi`**
  - Fresh translation: `I am wholly beside myself`
  - Davidson: `I am transported`
  - Davidson is elegant but more decorous; the French has a stronger sense of being physically and mentally carried out of oneself.

- **Paradoxical repetition**
  - French: `des hommages toujours différents, et toujours les mêmes`
  - Fresh translation preserves the paradox almost literally.
  - Davidson also keeps it, but the fresh version stays closer to the compact rhythm of the original.

- **`citoyens comme lui`**
  - Fresh translation: `citizens like him`
  - Davidson: `such citizens as he`
  - Davidson is accurate, but the French's political joke about custom and citizenship is slightly blunter than his idiom suggests.

- **Name inconsistency in the source text**
  - The woman is introduced as `Zuléma`, but later the narrative calls her `Anaïs`.
  - Davidson preserves the inconsistency because it is already in the French.
  - This looks like a textual or editorial inconsistency worth flagging rather than silently smoothing away.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: playful, erotic, satirical, and philosophical all at once; it moves from salon wit to fantasy and then to social inversion.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the letter's abrupt shifts without over-decorating them.
- **Davidson**: strong on narrative continuity, but often more decorous and Victorian in passages of erotic excess.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXLI is one of the richest comparison cases in this batch. Davidson is impressively readable over a long and complicated tale, but he repeatedly normalizes Montesquieu's oddness - especially in syntax, erotic diction, and philosophical asides. The underlying French remains stranger, sharper, and more mischievous.
