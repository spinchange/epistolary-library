## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CI**

**Rica to the same**

### Translation
The other day I spoke to you of the French's prodigious inconstancy in matters of fashion. Yet it is inconceivable how stubbornly attached they are to it. It is the rule by which they judge everything done among other nations. They bring everything back to it. Whatever is foreign always seems ridiculous to them. I confess I can hardly reconcile this frenzy for their costumes with the inconstancy with which they change them every day.

When I say that they despise everything foreign, I mean only trifles. In important matters, they seem to have distrusted themselves to the point of self-degradation. They readily admit that other peoples are wiser, provided one grants that they themselves are better dressed. They are willing to subject themselves to the laws of a rival nation, so long as French wig-makers may legislate on the form of foreign wigs. Nothing seems so beautiful to them as seeing the taste of their cooks reign from north to south, and the ordinances of their hairdressers carried into every dressing room in Europe.

With such noble advantages, what matters it to them if good sense comes to them from elsewhere, and if they have taken from their neighbors everything that concerns political and civil government?

Who could imagine that a kingdom, the oldest and most powerful in Europe, has for more than ten centuries been governed by laws not made for it? If the French had been conquered, that would not be hard to understand. But they are the conquerors.

They abandoned the old laws made by their first kings in the general assemblies of the nation. And what is singular is that the Roman laws they took in their place were partly made and partly codified by emperors contemporary with those very legislators.

And so that the borrowing might be complete, and all good sense come from elsewhere, they adopted all the constitutions of the popes and made of them a new part of their law - a new kind of servitude.

It is true that in recent times some town and provincial statutes have been put into writing; but almost all of them are taken from Roman law.

This abundance of adopted and, so to speak, naturalized laws is so great that it equally crushes justice and judges. But these volumes of laws are nothing beside that dreadful army of glossators, commentators, and compilers - men as weak in the accuracy of their minds as they are strong in their prodigious number.

That is not all. These foreign laws have introduced formalities that are the shame of human reason. It would be difficult enough to decide whether form became more harmful when it entered jurisprudence or when it lodged in medicine - whether it has done more damage under a jurist's robe than under a physician's broad hat, and whether in the one case it has ruined more people than in the other it has killed.

*Paris, the 17th day of the moon of Saphar, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves Rica's satirical contrast very well: French vanity in externals paired with large-scale legal and institutional borrowing from abroad.

### Main differences
- **Fashion as standard of judgment**
  - French piles up the point: fashion becomes the rule by which everything foreign is judged.
  - Fresh translation keeps that repetitive, mocking structure.
  - Davidson turns the opening into smoother prose.

- **Wig-makers as legislators**
  - French: `les perruquiers françois décident en législateurs`
  - Fresh translation keeps the explicit legislative absurdity.
  - Davidson catches the joke, but the fresh draft preserves the political sting more clearly.

- **Papal constitutions and servitude**
  - French: `toutes les constitutions des papes` / `nouveau genre de servitude`
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh draft keeps the concluding blow a little starker.

- **`formalités` and `forme`**
  - Fresh translation keeps Montesquieu's attack on legal form itself.
  - Davidson's `pedantry` is interpretive and shifts the target from procedure to mentality.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: sneering, compressed, and institutionally sharp.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the satire aimed at both vanity and legal formalism.
- **Davidson**: lucid and often effective, but occasionally too paraphrastic in abstract terms.

### Notable OCR / edition notes

## Working conclusion
Davidson is good on the broad satire, but this letter benefits from a closer translation of legal vocabulary. Montesquieu is attacking proceduralism and borrowed authority, not just generalized pedantry.
