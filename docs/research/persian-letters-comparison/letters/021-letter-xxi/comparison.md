## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXI**

**Usbek to the chief white eunuch**

### Translation
You ought to tremble when opening this letter — or rather, you ought to have trembled when you permitted Nadir's perfidy. You who, in a cold and languishing old age, cannot without crime raise your eyes toward the formidable objects of my love; you to whom it is never permitted to set a sacrilegious foot upon the threshold of the terrible place that hides them from every gaze; you allow those whose conduct is entrusted to you to do what you yourself would not dare do, and you do not see the thunderbolt ready to fall upon them and upon you?

And who are you but vile instruments, which I can break at my pleasure; who exist only insofar as you know how to obey; who are in the world only to live under my laws or die the moment I command; who breathe only as long as my happiness, my love, even my jealousy, have need of your baseness; and who can have no portion but submission, no soul but my will, no hope but my felicity?

I know that some of my wives impatiently bear the austere laws of duty; that the continual presence of a black eunuch bores them; that they are tired of those hideous objects given them to bring them back to their husband. I know it. But you, who lend yourself to this disorder, will be punished in a way to make all who abuse my confidence tremble.

I swear by all the prophets of heaven, and by Ali, the greatest of them all, that if you stray from your duty, I shall look on your life as I do on the insects I find beneath my feet.

*At Smyrna, the 12th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the menacing architecture of the letter and the humiliating catalogue through which Usbek reduces the eunuch to an instrument. The differences are less about missing content than about tone and lexical harshness.

### Main differences
- **Break vs destroy**
  - French: `que je puis briser à ma fantaisie`
  - Fresh translation: `which I can break at my pleasure`
  - Davidson: `whom I may destroy at my pleasure`
  - `destroy` intensifies the clause, while `break` better preserves the image of a disposable instrument.

- **Baseness, not service**
  - French: `ont besoin de votre bassesse`
  - Fresh translation: `have need of your baseness`
  - Davidson: `has no more need of your ignoble service`
  - Davidson softens a humiliating noun into a more functional phrase.

- **No soul but my will**
  - French: `d'autre âme que mes volontés`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stark metaphysical domination.
  - Davidson: `whose soul is my will`
  - Davidson stays close, but the French's syntax is more abrupt and coercive.

- **Hidden wives and `hideous objects`**
  - French: `ces objets affreux, qui leur sont donnés pour les ramener à leur époux`
  - Fresh translation: `those hideous objects given them to bring them back to their husband`
  - Davidson: `those hideous objects, which are appointed to keep them spotless for their husband`
  - Davidson interprets the purpose more morally than the French does.

- **Insects beneath my feet**
  - French: `comme celle des insectes que je trouve sous mes pieds`
  - Fresh translation: `as I do on the insects I find beneath my feet`
  - Davidson: `of no more account than that of the insects which I tread upon`
  - Davidson conveys the contempt, but the French's phrasing is more immediate and physical.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: savage, mechanical, and contemptuous from beginning to end.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the long accumulative syntax that turns the eunuch into a tool of jealousy.
- **Davidson**: strong and legible, but somewhat more stately than brutal.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXI shows Davidson translating overt menace effectively, but the French still lands harder in its degrading diction. The most important word to keep in view is `bassesse`, which Davidson turns into something more serviceable and less humiliating.
