## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXIV**

**Rica to Usbek, at ***.**

### Translation
A few days ago a man I know said to me: "I promised to introduce you into the best houses in Paris; I am now taking you to a great lord who is one of the men in the kingdom who best knows how to represent his rank."

"What does that mean, sir? Is he more polite, more affable than others?" "That is not it," he told me. "Ah, I understand: at every moment he makes everyone who comes near him feel the superiority he has over them. If that is so, I have no need to go there; I accept the sentence already, and yield the whole case to him."

Yet I had to go; and I saw so proud a little man, he took a pinch of snuff with such loftiness, he blew his nose so pitilessly, he spat with such phlegm, he caressed his dogs in a way so offensive to human beings, that I could not tire of admiring him. Ah, good God, I said to myself, if, when I was at the court of Persia, I represented myself in that way, I represented a great fool! We would have had to be of a very bad disposition, Usbek, to go and offer a hundred little insults to people who came to us every day to show us their goodwill; they knew very well that we were above them, and if they had not known it, our kindnesses would have taught them so every day. Having nothing to do in order to make ourselves respected, we did everything to make ourselves loved: we made ourselves accessible to the humblest; in the midst of grandeur, which always hardens, they found us sensitive; they saw nothing above them in us but our heart; we stooped to their needs. But when it was necessary to uphold the majesty of the prince in public ceremonies; when it was necessary to make the nation respected by foreigners; when finally, in dangerous situations, it was necessary to rouse the soldiers, we rose a hundred times higher than we had descended; we brought pride back to our faces; and people sometimes found that we represented ourselves well enough.

*Paris, the 10th day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the satirical anecdote and the larger contrast between French aristocratic hauteur and Persian ideals of dignified accessibility. His version is readable and often lively, but it tends to smooth out Montesquieu's recurring language of `representation` and occasionally weakens the social geometry of the scene.

### Main differences
- **Sender / recipient inversion in the English title**
  - French title: `RICA A USBEK.`
  - Davidson clean text title: `Usbek to Rica, at * * *.`
  - That is not a stylistic variation but a real paratextual mismatch in the English witness.

- **The key verb `représenter`**
  - French: `un des hommes du royaume qui représentent le mieux`
  - Fresh translation: `best knows how to represent his rank`
  - Davidson: `supports his rank as well as any man in France`
  - Davidson gets the broad point, but the French keeps returning to the idea of social performance and self-representation.

- **The proud man's bodily manners**
  - French: `il se moucha si impitoyablement`, `il cracha avec tant de flegme`
  - Davidson's `blew his nose so mercilessly` is strong and close.
  - But the cumulative comedy in the French is a little drier and more relentlessly observational than Davidson's more polished paragraph rhythm.

- **Accessibility to inferiors**
  - French: `nous nous communiquions aux plus petits`
  - Fresh translation: `we made ourselves accessible to the humblest`
  - Davidson: `we were accessible to the humblest`
  - Davidson is close here; the fresh version keeps a little more of the deliberate self-opening in the reflexive French.

- **`Ils ne voyoient que notre coeur au-dessus d'eux`**
  - Fresh translation: `they saw nothing above them in us but our heart`
  - Davidson: `only our hearts appeared to belong to a higher order`
  - Davidson makes the line more abstract and hierarchical. The French is stranger and sharper.

- **Closing return to `represent`**
  - Fresh translation: `people sometimes found that we represented ourselves well enough`
  - Davidson: `we were found to play our part at least adequately`
  - `play our part` is elegant, but it loses the deliberate recurrence of `représenter` from the opening.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: amused, ironic, and socially exact; Rica turns a comic visit into a small theory of rank and dignity.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep both the satire of manners and the repeated vocabulary of representation.
- **Davidson**: fluid and effective, but somewhat more idiomatic and less pointed in its lexical patterning.

## Working conclusion
Davidson's version of Letter LXXIV is broadly faithful in sense, but the fresh draft keeps clearer track of the letter's central conceit: true greatness is shown not by petty insult but by controlled self-representation that can descend graciously and rise majestically when needed.
