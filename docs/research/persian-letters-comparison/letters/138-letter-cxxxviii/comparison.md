## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXXVIII**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
Ministers succeed one another and destroy one another here like the seasons: in the past three years I have seen the financial system changed four times. Today in Persia and in Turkey subsidies are raised in the same way the founders of those monarchies raised them; it is very far from being the same here. It is true that we do not put so much wit into it as Westerners do: we think there is no more difference between administering a prince's revenues and administering those of a private person than there is between counting a hundred thousand tomans and counting a hundred. But here there is much more subtlety and mystery. Great geniuses must work day and night; they must ceaselessly bring forth new plans, and with pain; they must listen to the advice of an infinity of people who work for them without being asked; they must retire and live deep in a cabinet impenetrable to the great and sacred to the small; they must always have their heads full of important secrets, miraculous designs, new systems; and, absorbed in meditation, they are deprived not only of the use of speech, but sometimes even of politeness.

As soon as the late king had closed his eyes, people thought of establishing a new administration. Everyone felt that things were going badly, but nobody knew how to make them better. They had found the unbounded authority of the previous ministers unsatisfactory; they wanted to divide it. For that purpose six or seven councils were created; and that ministry is perhaps the one of all that governed France with the most good sense. Its duration was short, just like the duration of the good it produced.

France, at the death of the late king, was a body crushed by a thousand ailments: N*** took the knife in hand, cut away the useless flesh, and applied a few local remedies; but there still remained an inner defect to cure. A foreigner came who undertook that cure. After many violent remedies, he believed he had restored the body to plumpness; he merely made it swollen.

All those who were rich six months ago are now in poverty, and those who had no bread are overflowing with wealth. Never have those two extremes touched so closely. The foreigner has turned the state the way a rag-dealer turns a coat: he makes what was underneath appear on top; and what was on top he turns inside out. What unexpected fortunes, incredible even to those who made them! God does not draw men from nothing more quickly. How many servants are waited on by their comrades, and perhaps tomorrow by their masters!

All this often produces bizarre things. The lackeys who made their fortunes under the last reign boast today of their birth: they return to those who have just taken off their livery in a certain street all the contempt that was felt for them six months ago; they cry at the top of their lungs: `The nobility is ruined! What disorder in the state! What confusion among the ranks! One sees only unknown people making fortunes!' I promise you that these men will take fine revenge on those who come after them; and that, in thirty years, these gentlemen of quality will make a great deal of noise.

*Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1720.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson handles the broad satire well: ministerial turnover, financial experimentation, social inversion, and the theatrical speed of speculative enrichment all come through clearly. His version is lively and often pointed, especially in the medical metaphor for France's condition.

### Main differences
- **Administrative mystery**
  - French: `un cabinet impénétrable aux grands et sacré aux petits`
  - Fresh translation: `a cabinet impenetrable to the great and sacred to the small`
  - Davidson: `closets inaccessible to the great, and worshipped by the small`
  - `Sacré` here is closer to `sacred` than to `worshipped`; Davidson slightly dramatizes the image.

- **Council count**
  - French: `six ou sept conseils`
  - Fresh translation: `six or seven councils`
  - Davidson: `five or six councils`
  - This is a concrete discrepancy, not just stylistic drift.

- **`embonpoint`**
  - Fresh translation: `restored the body to plumpness`
  - Davidson: `put it into good condition`
  - Davidson weakens the bodily satire. The French keeps the image of unhealthy corpulence.

- **`fripier`**
  - Fresh translation: `rag-dealer`
  - Davidson: `oldclothesman`
  - Davidson is serviceable here, but `rag-dealer` keeps the coarse materiality of the comparison.

- **Social reversal**
  - French: `ils rendent ... tout le mépris qu'on avoit pour eux`
  - Fresh translation keeps the retaliatory sense: they repay to others the contempt once shown to them.
  - Davidson's phrasing is clear, but a little smoother and less vindictive.

- **Final irony about `gens de qualité`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the edge of the final line by letting the nouveaux riches become tomorrow's `people of quality`.
  - Davidson also gets the point, but his English is slightly more formal and less biting.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: fast, sardonic, and socially cruel; Montesquieu relishes the spectacle of speculative reversal.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to keep the bodily metaphors and the social satire visibly connected.
- **Davidson**: vigorous and readable, but occasionally too polished for the roughness of the French imagery.
