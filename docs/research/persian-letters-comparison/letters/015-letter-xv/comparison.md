## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XV**

**The chief eunuch to Jaron, black eunuch, at Erzurum**

### Translation
I pray heaven to bring you back to these places and snatch you from every danger.

Although I have hardly ever known that attachment called friendship, and have wrapped myself entirely within myself, you nevertheless made me feel that I still had a heart; and while I was bronze toward all those slaves who lived under my laws, I watched your childhood grow with pleasure.

The time came when my master cast his eyes on you. Nature had scarcely yet spoken when the knife separated you from nature. I will not tell you whether I pitied you, or whether I felt pleasure at seeing you raised up to my own condition. I calmed your tears and your cries. I thought I saw you take a second birth and emerge from a servitude in which you would always have had to obey, to enter into a servitude in which you would command. I took care of your education. Severity, always inseparable from instruction, long kept you from knowing that you were dear to me. Yet you were; and I would tell you that I loved you as a father loves his son, if those names of father and son could suit our destiny.

You are going to travel through lands inhabited by Christians, who have never believed. It is impossible that you should not contract many defilements there. How could the Prophet look on you in the midst of so many millions of his enemies? I would like my master, on his return, to make the pilgrimage to Mecca. All of you would purify yourselves in the land of angels.

*From the seraglio of Isfahan, the 10th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the emotional movement of the letter closely: the chief eunuch's guarded attachment to Jaron, the violence of castration recollected as promotion, and the closing concern about Christian defilement. He is generally faithful, but he often softens the terseness and paradox of the French.

### Main differences
- **Bronze, not bronze statue**
  - French: `j'étois de bronze`
  - Fresh translation: `I was bronze`
  - Davidson: `I was as a bronze statue`
  - `statue` is an explanatory embellishment; the French is colder and more compressed.

- **Nature had scarcely spoken**
  - French: `Il s'en falloit bien que la nature eût encore parlé`
  - Fresh translation: `Nature had scarcely yet spoken`
  - Davidson: `Nature had not yet whispered her secrets`
  - Davidson heightens the phrase poetically and personifies `nature` more strongly than the French does.

- **Knife and nature**
  - French: `le fer te sépara de la nature`
  - Fresh translation: `the knife separated you from nature`
  - Davidson: `the knife separated you from her for ever`
  - `for ever` intensifies what is already implied; the French's force comes from its bluntness.

- **Servitude, not service**
  - French: `sortir d'une servitude ... pour entrer dans une servitude`
  - Fresh translation keeps `servitude` in both clauses.
  - Davidson shifts to `a state of thraldom` and then `a service in which you would exercise authority`.
  - Davidson preserves the paradox only partly; the repeated word `servitude` matters to the letter's logic.

- **Christians `who have never believed`**
  - French: `les chrétiens, qui n'ont jamais cru`
  - Fresh translation keeps the absolute formulation.
  - Davidson: `unbelieving Christians`
  - Davidson is idiomatic, but the French's phrasing is more pointedly doctrinal.

- **Land of angels**
  - French: `la terre des anges`
  - Fresh translation: `the land of angels`
  - Davidson: `that blessed place`
  - Davidson smooths away a concrete image.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: emotionally constricted, hierarchical, and unsettlingly tender.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the paternal note tied to coercion and mutilation.
- **Davidson**: readable and effective, but often slightly gentler and more literary.

### Notable OCR / edition notes

- There is a visible line-break artifact in `un-` / `believing Christians`, and the date line still reads `loth`.

## Working conclusion
Letter XV is a good example of Davidson's general strength and general limit: he carries the broad meaning well, but the French remains harsher in its lexical choices, especially around `bronze`, `nature`, `servitude`, and `the land of angels`.
