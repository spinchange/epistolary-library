## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXX**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
The fecundity of a people sometimes depends on the slightest circumstances in the world, so that often all it takes is a new turn in its imagination to make it much more numerous than it was.

The Jews, always exterminated and always reborn, have repaired their continual losses and destructions by this single hope shared by all their families: that a powerful king will be born among them who shall be master of the earth.

The ancient kings of Persia had so many thousands of subjects only because of that dogma of the religion of the Magi: that the acts most pleasing to God which men can perform are to beget a child, till a field, and plant a tree.

If China contains within itself so prodigious a people, that comes only from a certain way of thinking. Since children regard their fathers as gods, respect them as such in this life, and honor them after death with sacrifices in which they believe that their souls, annihilated in the Tyen, take on a new life, everyone is inclined to enlarge a family so submissive in this life and so necessary in the other.

On the other hand, the countries of the Mohammedans grow more deserted every day because of an opinion that, holy as it is, nevertheless has very pernicious effects when it is deeply rooted in minds. We regard ourselves as travelers who ought to think only of another homeland. Useful and lasting works, the care of securing the fortune of our children, projects that extend beyond a short and passing life, seem to us something extravagant. Tranquil in the present and without anxiety for the future, we do not take the trouble either to repair public buildings, or to clear uncultivated land, or to cultivate that which is ready to receive our care: we live in a general insensibility and leave everything to Providence.

It was a spirit of vanity that established in Europe the unjust law of primogeniture, so unfavorable to propagation because it fixes a father's attention on only one of his children and turns his eyes away from all the others; because it obliges him, in order to make the fortune of one secure, to oppose the establishment of several; and finally because it destroys the equality of citizens, which is the source of all their prosperity.

*Paris, the 4th day of the moon of Rhamazan, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the line of the letter well: demographic imagination, Jewish messianic hope, Magian doctrine, Chinese ancestor reverence, Muslim otherworldliness, and European primogeniture. His version is readable and often close, but the clean text here also contains intrusive footnote material and a few interpretive turns.

### Main differences
- **Opening idea of imagination**
  - French: `un nouveau tour dans son imagination`
  - Fresh translation: `a new turn in its imagination`
  - Davidson: `to give a new direction to its imagination`
  - Davidson is accurate, though slightly more explanatory than the French.

- **Messianic hope**
  - Fresh translation keeps the strong expectation that `a powerful king will be born among them`.
  - Davidson's `from one of them shall spring a powerful king` is elegant but more elevated in register.

- **Chinese afterlife sentence**
  - Fresh translation keeps the difficult sequence about souls `annihilated in the Tyen` taking on a new life.
  - Davidson is usable here, but the English clean text is interrupted by a footnote marker after `Tyen`, which breaks the sentence's flow.

- **Muslim passivity**
  - French: `nous vivons dans une insensibilité générale`
  - Fresh translation: `we live in a general insensibility`
  - Davidson: `we live generally in a state of indifference`
  - Davidson smooths the phrase into more idiomatic English, but the French is starker.

- **Equality and prosperity**
  - French: `qui en fait toute l'opulence`
  - Fresh translation: `which is the source of all their prosperity`
  - Davidson: `which constitutes all their wealth`
  - Davidson is close, but the French points to the condition that makes collective abundance possible, not only wealth in the abstract.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: schematic, comparative, and aphoristic; it moves rapidly across religions and civilizations.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve that quick comparative logic without flattening the sharper judgments.
- **Davidson**: generally faithful, but somewhat more literary and more vulnerable here to OCR disruption.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text contains clear footnote intrusion around `Tyen` and another break in the sentence beginning `because of a belief, all1 The heaven of the Chinese.`
- Those defects make this letter one that should eventually be checked against the raw source or scan.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXX is mostly intact in Davidson, but the comparison is complicated by OCR contamination. The most important textual features to retain are the role of collective imagination, the severe phrase `general insensibility`, and the tightly argued critique of primogeniture.
