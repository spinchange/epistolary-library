**Letter LXXXVIII**

### Translation
They say man is a social animal. On that basis, it seems to me that the Frenchman is more a man than anyone else: he is man par excellence, for he seems made only for society.

But I have noticed among them people who are not only sociable, but are themselves universal society. They multiply into every corner, and in an instant populate the four quarters of a city. A hundred men of this kind abound more than two thousand citizens. To foreigners they could make up for the ravages of plague or famine. In the schools they ask whether a body can be in several places at once; these men are proof of what philosophers still dispute.

They are always bustling, because they have the important business of asking everyone they see where he is going and where he comes from.

No one could ever get it out of their heads that politeness requires visiting the public every day in detail, not counting the visits they make in bulk to places of assembly; but since that route is too abbreviated, those visits count for nothing in the rules of their ceremonial.

They batter house doors with their hammers more than winds and storms do. If one were to examine the list of all the porters, one would find their names mangled every day in a thousand ways in Swiss lettering. They spend their lives trailing after funerals, in compliments of condolence, or in marriage congratulations. The king cannot bestow a favor on one of his subjects without costing them a carriage ride to go and testify to their joy. At last they come home very tired to rest up, so they can resume their painful duties the next day.

One of them died the other day of sheer weariness, and this epitaph was put upon his tomb: `Here lies the man who never rested. He attended five hundred and thirty funerals. He rejoiced at the birth of two thousand six hundred and eighty children. The pensions on which he congratulated his friends, always in different terms, amounted to two million six hundred thousand livres. The road he covered over the paving stones came to nine thousand six hundred stadia; that in the country, to thirty-six. His conversation was amusing; he had a ready stock of three hundred and sixty-five tales. He also possessed, from his youth, one hundred and eighteen apophthegms drawn from the ancients, which he employed on brilliant occasions. He died at last in the sixtieth year of his age. I stop, traveler; for how could I finish telling you what he did and what he saw?`

*Paris, the 3rd day of the moon of Gemmadi II, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the social satire effectively. He keeps the portrait of compulsive visitors and ceremonial callers whose entire identity is made up of appearing everywhere and acknowledging every public event.

### Main differences
- **`la société universelle`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the absurd phrase `universal society`.
  - Davidson's `society itself` catches the joke but is less expansive.

- **`abondent plus`**
  - Fresh translation stays close to the odd quantitative language of the French.
  - Davidson's `make more appearance than` is witty, though more interpretive.

- **Ceremonial bureaucracy**
  - The French distinction between visiting the public `in detail` and `in bulk` is part of the joke.
  - Davidson preserves it broadly, but my draft keeps the bureaucratic phrasing more explicitly.

- **Epitaph register**
  - Davidson is broadly faithful.
  - His English sometimes adds polish where the French stays deadpan in its arithmetic of sociability.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: hyperbolic, dry, and delightfully bureaucratic in its satire.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the absurd exactness of the social accounting.
- **Davidson**: witty and very usable, but a little more literary than the French's mock-record style.
