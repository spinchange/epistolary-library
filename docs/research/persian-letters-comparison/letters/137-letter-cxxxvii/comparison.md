### Heading
**Letter CXXXVII**

**Rica to the same**

### Translation
The next day he led me into another cabinet. `Here,' he said to me, `are the poets; that is to say, those authors whose trade is to put shackles on common sense, and to crush reason beneath embellishments, just as women were once buried beneath their finery and ornaments. You know them; they are not rare among Eastern peoples, where the hotter sun seems to warm even the imagination.

`There are the epic poems.' `What! and what are epic poems?' `In truth,' he said to me, `I know nothing about it. The connoisseurs say that only two have ever been made, and that the others that are given that name are not epic at all; that too I do not know. They say further that it is impossible to make any new ones; and that is more surprising still.

`Here are the dramatic poets, who in my opinion are the poets above all others, and the masters of the passions. There are two kinds of them: the comic ones, who move us so gently; and the tragic ones, who disturb us and shake us with so much violence.

`Here are the lyric poets, whom I despise as much as I esteem the others, and who make of their art a harmonious extravagance.

`Then one sees the authors of idylls and eclogues, who please even people at court, by giving them the idea of a tranquility they do not have, and by showing it to them in the condition of shepherds.

`Of all the authors we have seen, here are the most dangerous: those who sharpen epigrams, those little slender arrows that make a wound both deep and beyond the reach of remedies.

`Here you see the novelists, who are a kind of poets, and who equally exaggerate the language of wit and that of the heart; who spend their lives looking for nature and always miss it; and who create heroes who are as foreign to it as winged dragons and hippocentaurs.'

`I have seen,' I said to him, `some of your novels; and if you saw ours, you would be even more shocked by them. They are just as unnatural, and besides are extremely constrained by our customs: ten years of passion are needed before a lover has been able even to see his mistress's face. Yet authors are forced to lead their readers through these tedious preliminaries. But it is impossible for incidents to be varied very much, so they resort to an artifice worse than the evil they are trying to cure: marvels. I am sure you would not approve of a sorceress making an army come up out of the earth, or of a hero single-handedly destroying one of a hundred thousand men. Yet such are our novels: those cold adventures, so often repeated, make us languish, and those extravagant marvels revolt us.'

*Paris, the 6th day of the moon of Chalval, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the literary taxonomy of the letter well and keeps most of the jokes intact. He captures the movement from general satire on poetry to the particularly sharp mockery of epigram and romance.

### Main differences
- **Poetry as anti-reason**
  - French: `mettre des entraves au bon sens, et d'accabler la raison sous les agréments`
  - Fresh translation: `put shackles on common sense, and crush reason beneath embellishments`
  - Davidson: `shackle common sense, and smother reason with embellishment`
  - Davidson is close, though `accabler` is a little heavier than `smother` here.

- **Tragedy's violence**
  - French: `nous troublent et nous agitent avec tant de violence`
  - Fresh translation keeps both disturbance and agitation.
  - Davidson's `rouse our passions and shake our dispositions` is more interpretive and slightly more mannered.

- **Lyric poetry**
  - French: `une harmonieuse extravagance`
  - Fresh translation: `a harmonious extravagance`
  - Davidson: `melodious nonsense`
  - This is a notable tonal shift. Davidson makes the line more contemptuous and more colloquial than the French.

- **Epigrams as arrows**
  - French: `petites flèches déliées`
  - Fresh translation: `little slender arrows`
  - Davidson: `little, sharp darts`
  - Davidson sharpens the weapon; the French stresses fineness and penetration.

- **Mythological creatures**
  - Fresh translation keeps `hippocentaurs`.
  - Davidson gives `hippogriffs`.
  - That is a real lexical substitution, not just a stylistic variant.

- **Romance and emotional language**
  - French: `le langage de l'esprit et celui du coeur`
  - Fresh translation: `the language of wit and that of the heart`
  - Davidson: `extravagant in their wit` and `outrageous in their treatment of passion`
  - Davidson communicates the idea, but the elegant parallelism of the French is lost.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: dry, clever, and pointed; the dervish sounds amused even when he is condemning whole genres.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve that neat satirical catalog without inflating it.
- **Davidson**: lively and often very good, but sometimes substitutes brighter Victorian phrasing for Montesquieu's cleaner irony.

## Working conclusion
Letter CXXXVII is another case where Davidson is strong on overall movement but looser on lexical precision. The most notable differences are `hippocentaurs` -> `hippogriffs` and `harmonious extravagance` -> `melodious nonsense`, both of which shift the texture of the satire.
