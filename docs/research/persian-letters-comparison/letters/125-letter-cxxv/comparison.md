## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXV**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
What can be the motive for those immense largesses that princes pour out on their courtiers? Do they want to bind them to themselves? They are already won over as far as they can be. Besides, if princes gain some of their subjects by buying them, by the same logic they must lose infinitely many others by impoverishing them.

When I think of the condition of princes, always surrounded by greedy and insatiable men, I can only pity them; and I pity them still more when they do not have the strength to resist demands always burdensome to those who ask for nothing.

Whenever I hear of their liberality, of the favors and pensions they grant, I give myself up to a thousand reflections: a crowd of ideas comes before my mind; it seems to me that I hear this ordinance proclaimed:

'Since the tireless courage shown by some of our subjects in asking us for pensions has kept our royal magnificence in constant exercise, we have at last yielded to the multitude of petitions presented to us, which until now have formed the chief solicitude of the throne. They have represented to us that, since our accession to the crown, they have never failed to attend our rising; that we have always seen them on our passage, motionless as boundary stones; and that they have climbed very high, onto the tallest shoulders, in order to behold our serenity. We have even received several petitions from certain ladies, who begged us to note that it is notorious how very expensive they are to maintain. Some of them, indeed already very antiquated, implored us, wagging their heads, to remember that they adorned the court of the kings our predecessors; and that, if the generals of their armies made the state formidable by their military exploits, they have made the court no less famous by their intrigues. Thus, desiring to treat the petitioners kindly and grant all their prayers, we have ordained as follows:

'Every plowman with five children shall each day cut away one fifth of the bread he gives them. We enjoin fathers of families to make the reduction on each child as equitably as possible.

'We expressly forbid all those who devote themselves to cultivating their lands, or who have leased them out as farms, to make any repairs to them, of whatever kind.

'We order that all persons engaged in mean and mechanical trades, who have never attended the levee of our majesty, shall henceforth buy clothes for themselves, their wives, and their children only once every four years; and we further most strictly forbid them those little family festivities they were accustomed to hold on the principal feasts of the year.

'And since we are informed that most of the burghers of our good towns are wholly occupied in providing establishments for their daughters, who have made themselves recommendable in our state only by a sad and wearisome modesty, we order that they shall delay marrying them until, having reached the age fixed by the ordinances, the daughters themselves compel them to it. We forbid our magistrates to provide for the education of their children.'

*Paris, the 1st day of the moon of Chalval, 1718.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the general satirical design: princely generosity to courtiers is financed by hidden exactions imposed on peasants, tradesmen, and bourgeois families. But this letter is one of the messier English witnesses in the current clean files.

### Main differences
- **Opening loss in the English clean excerpt**
  - The English clean file begins mid-sentence, so Davidson's title and opening question are not actually preserved in this excerpt.
  - The missing title and first line survive only in the raw spillover at the end of Letter CXXIV's OCR excerpt.

- **`d'un entretien très-difficile`**
  - Fresh translation: `very expensive they are to maintain`
  - Davidson's surviving clean text breaks the phrase across a page turn and produces nonsense about being `circumspect` in conversation.
  - The French is financial and satirical, not moral.

- **`immobiles comme des bornes`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the image of courtiers standing `motionless as boundary stones`.
  - Davidson's `motionless as posts` gets the basic stiffness, but loses some of the dead, landmark-like absurdity.

- **Court women and antiquity**
  - French: `quelques-unes même très-surannées`
  - Fresh translation keeps the bite of `already very antiquated`.
  - Davidson's `very ancient dames` is serviceable, but a little smoother and more decorous.

- **The fake decree's political point**
  - The French repeatedly turns pensions into anti-social legislation.
  - Davidson conveys much of this, but the fresh draft keeps the cruelty of the administrative voice slightly more plainly before the reader.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: sustained mock-edict, dry, cruel, and economically lucid.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the faux-royal bureaucratic voice and the social transfer of cost from courtiers to everyone else.
- **Davidson**: often good where readable, but badly compromised here by page-break damage and OCR contamination.
