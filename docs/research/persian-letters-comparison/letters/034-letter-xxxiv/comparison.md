## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXXIV**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The women of Persia are more beautiful than those of France; but the women of France are prettier. It is hard not to love the former, and hard not to take pleasure in the company of the latter: the first are more tender and more modest, the second are gayer and more playful.

What gives the blood such beauty in Persia is the regular life the women lead there: they do not gamble or sit up late, they do not drink wine, and they are almost never exposed to the open air. It must be admitted that the seraglio is made more for health than for pleasure: it is an even life, with nothing piquant in it; everything there bears the mark of subordination and duty; even pleasures are grave there, and joys severe; and they are hardly ever tasted except as marks of authority and dependence.

Even the men in Persia do not have the same cheerfulness as the French: one does not see among them that freedom of mind and that air of contentment which I find here in every rank and every condition.

It is even worse in Turkey, where one might find families in which, from father to son, no one has laughed since the founding of the monarchy.

This gravity of the Asians comes from the little intercourse there is among them: they see one another only when ceremony forces them to; friendship, that sweet engagement of the heart which makes life pleasant here, is almost unknown to them; they withdraw into their houses, where they always find a company waiting for them, so that each family is, so to speak, isolated from the others.

One day, when I was talking about this with a man of this country, he said to me: `What shocks me most in your customs is that you are obliged to live with slaves whose hearts and minds always bear the stamp of the baseness of their condition. Those cowardly people weaken in you the feelings of virtue that come from nature, and ruin them from childhood by constantly surrounding you.

`For after all, rid yourself of prejudice: what can one expect from the upbringing one receives from a wretch who makes his honor consist in guarding another man's women, and glories in the vilest employment among human beings; who is contemptible even in his fidelity, the only one of his virtues, because he is driven to it by envy, jealousy, and despair; who, burning to avenge himself on the two sexes by whom he is rejected, consents to be tyrannized by the stronger so long as he can torment the weaker; who draws from his imperfection, his ugliness, and his deformity all the splendor of his condition, and is esteemed only because he is unworthy of esteem; who at last, riveted forever to the door to which he is fastened, harder than the hinges and bolts that hold it, boasts of fifty years of life in that degrading post, where, burdened with his master's jealousy, he has exercised all his baseness?'`

*From Paris, the 14th day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson follows the structure of the letter closely: the contrast between Persian and French women, the disciplined life of the seraglio, the broader social cheerfulness of France, and the final denunciation of eunuch-dominated upbringing. His version is readable and often pointed, but it repeatedly softens or reframes Montesquieu's sharper phrasing.

### Main differences
- **`Belles` vs `jolies`**
  - French: `plus belles` / `plus jolies`
  - Fresh translation: `more beautiful` / `prettier`
  - Davidson: `finer` / `prettier`
  - `Finer` is not wrong, but the French contrast is more directly about beauty versus prettiness or charm.

- **Health versus pleasure, not health versus happiness**
  - French: `plutôt fait pour la santé que pour les plaisirs`
  - Fresh translation: `made more for health than for pleasure`
  - Davidson: `more conducive to health than to happiness`
  - Davidson shifts the contrast from bodily regimen versus pleasure to bodily regimen versus happiness, which is broader and less exact.

- **The texture of seraglio life**
  - French: `c'est une vie unie, qui ne pique point`
  - Fresh translation: `it is an even life, with nothing piquant in it`
  - Davidson: `it is so dull and uniform`
  - Davidson captures the general idea, but the French is less blunt and slightly more social in tone.

- **Friendship as a bond of the heart**
  - French: `ce doux engagement du coeur`
  - Fresh translation: `that sweet engagement of the heart`
  - Davidson: `that dear solace of the heart`
  - Davidson sentimentalizes the line a little; `engagement` stresses a bond or attachment, not just consolation.

- **The corruption of upbringing**
  - French: `le coeur et l'esprit se sentent toujours de la bassesse de leur condition`
  - Fresh translation: `whose hearts and minds always bear the stamp of the baseness of their condition`
  - Davidson: `whose thoughts and inclinations are always subdued to the vileness of their condition`
  - Davidson is effective, but he turns an imprint of condition into a more active moral submission.

- **The eunuch's hard mechanical image**
  - French: `plus dur que les gonds et les verrous qui la tiennent`
  - Fresh translation: `harder than the hinges and bolts that hold it`
  - Davidson: `harder than the bolts and bars which secure it`
  - `Hinges and bolts` keeps the physical image of the French more exactly.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: cool, sociological, and increasingly savage; it moves from national comparison into a bitter anatomy of eunuch power.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the crisp oppositions and the long, accumulating violence of the final sentence.
- **Davidson**: fluent and often strong, but somewhat more moralized and Victorian in diction.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXXIV is broadly well represented by Davidson, but the fresh translation keeps closer track of the original's exact contrasts: beauty versus prettiness, health versus pleasure, and above all the colder, more corrosive rhetoric of the closing attack on eunuchs.
