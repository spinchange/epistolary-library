## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXXVI**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
Coffee is very much in use in Paris: there are a great many public houses where it is served. In some of these houses people tell the news; in others they play chess. There is one where coffee is prepared in such a way that it gives wit to those who drink it: at least, among all those who leave it, there is not one who does not believe he has four times more wit than when he went in.

But what shocks me about these fine minds is that they do not make themselves useful to their country, and that they amuse their talents with childish things. For example, when I arrived in Paris, I found them heated over the most trifling dispute imaginable: it concerned the reputation of an old Greek poet whose birthplace, as well as the time of his death, has been unknown for two thousand years. Both parties admitted that he was an excellent poet: the only question was how much merit should be attributed to him. Everyone wanted to fix his price; but among these distributors of reputation, some weighed better than others: that was the quarrel. It was a very lively one, for the two sides hurled at each other, in the warmest manner, such coarse insults and such bitter jokes that I admired the manner of the dispute no less than the subject of the dispute. `If someone,' I said to myself, `were rash enough to attack before one of these defenders of the Greek poet the reputation of some honest citizen, he would not be left unanswered; and I believe this delicate zeal for the reputation of the dead would indeed blaze up in defense of the living! But whatever the case,' I added, `God preserve me from ever drawing upon myself the enmity of the censors of this poet, whom a stay of two thousand years in the tomb has not been able to protect from so implacable a hatred! At present they strike blows in the air; but what would it be if their fury were animated by the presence of an enemy?'`

Those I have just spoken of dispute in the vulgar tongue; and they must be distinguished from another sort of disputants who use a barbarous language that seems to add something to the fury and obstinacy of the combatants. There are districts where one sees something like a dark and thick melee of people of that kind; they feed on distinctions, they live on obscure arguments and false consequences. This trade, in which one ought to die of hunger, nevertheless brings in a living. A whole nation has been seen, driven from its country, to cross the seas and settle in France, carrying with it, to provide for the necessities of life, nothing but a formidable talent for disputation. Farewell.

*From Paris, the last day of the moon of Zilhagé, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson gives the broad satire effectively: Parisian coffeehouses, literary quarrels, and scholastic disputation all become objects of amused foreign scrutiny. But his version is hampered by OCR debris and by some smoothing choices that reduce the French's sharpness and comic compression.

### Main differences
- **News, not mere gossip**
  - French: `on dit des nouvelles`
  - Fresh translation: `people tell the news`
  - Davidson: `they meet to gossip`
  - Davidson makes the coffeehouse chatter slightly more trivial than the French requires.

- **`Beaux esprits`**
  - French: `ces beaux esprits`
  - Fresh translation: `these fine minds`
  - Davidson: `these geniuses`
  - `Geniuses` is stronger and more flattering than the lightly ironic French expression.

- **Distributing reputation**
  - French: `parmi ces distributeurs de réputation`
  - Fresh translation: `among these distributors of reputation`
  - Davidson: `among those apportioners of praise`
  - Davidson is lively, but `reputation` is slightly broader and more social than `praise`.

- **The image of empty combat**
  - French: `Ils frappent à présent des coups en l'air`
  - Fresh translation: `At present they strike blows in the air`
  - Davidson: `At present they fight the air`
  - Davidson is close, though the French keeps the more physical image of repeated blows.

- **The `barbarous language` of scholastics**
  - French: `une langue barbare`
  - Fresh translation: `a barbarous language`
  - Davidson: `a barbarous language`
  - This is a close match; Davidson is strongest when he stays literal.

- **`Fausses conséquences`**
  - French: `fausses conséquences`
  - Fresh translation: `false consequences`
  - Davidson: `false inferences`
  - `Inferences` is a reasonable interpretation, but the French's mock-logical phrasing is slightly better preserved by `consequences`.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: urbane, sardonic, and fast-moving; it satirizes both literary vanity and scholastic pedantry.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the epigrammatic movement and the comic metaphor of argument as physical combat.
- **Davidson**: often effective sentence by sentence, but sometimes more expansive and less dryly pointed.

### OCR / cleanup notes
- The English clean text contains obvious footnote and page debris, including the stray line `century.` between paragraphs.
- Notes also interrupt the early discussion of the coffeehouse and the classical quarrel.
- This is a letter where comparison is still possible, but the clean text should definitely be checked against the raw source before any final edition-level use.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXXVI is one of the clearer examples of why fresh translation work matters even when Davidson is serviceable: the overall satire survives, but the original's nimble, compressed irony is easier to see when the prose stays closer to the French and is not obscured by OCR damage.
