## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XIII**

**Usbek to the same**

### Translation
I cannot speak enough to you of the virtue of the Troglodytes. One of them said one day: My father must plow his field tomorrow; I shall rise two hours before him, and when he goes there, he will find it already plowed.

Another said to himself: It seems to me that my sister has some liking for a young Troglodyte among our kin; I must speak to my father and persuade him to make that marriage.

Another was told that robbers had carried off his flock. I am very sorry for it, he said, for there was a completely white heifer in it that I wanted to offer to the gods.

Another could be heard saying: I must go to the temple to thank the gods, for my brother, whom my father loves so much and whom I love so dearly, has recovered his health.

Or again: There is a field adjoining my father's, and those who cultivate it are every day exposed to the heat of the sun. I must go plant two trees there, so that those poor people may sometimes rest in their shade.

One day, when several Troglodytes were assembled, an old man spoke of a young man whom he suspected of having committed a bad action, and reproached him for it. We do not believe he committed that crime, said the young Troglodytes; but if he did, may he die the last of his family.

A Troglodyte was told that strangers had plundered his house and carried everything away. If they were not unjust men, he replied, I would wish the gods to grant them a longer use of it than they granted me.

So much prosperity was not regarded without envy. Neighboring peoples gathered together and, under a vain pretext, resolved to carry off their herds. As soon as this decision became known, the Troglodytes sent ambassadors to meet them, who spoke thus:

What have the Troglodytes done to you? Have they carried off your wives, stolen your cattle, ravaged your lands? No. We are just, and we fear the gods. What then do you ask of us? Do you want wool to make clothes? Do you want milk from our flocks, or fruits from our lands? Lay down your arms, come among us, and we will give you all of that. But we swear by what is most sacred that, if you enter our lands as enemies, we will regard you as an unjust people and treat you like wild beasts.

These words were sent back with contempt. Those savage peoples entered the land of the Troglodytes in arms, believing it defended only by innocence.

But the Troglodytes were well prepared for defense. They had placed their wives and children in the midst of them. They were astonished by the injustice of their enemies, not by their number. A new ardor had seized their hearts. One wanted to die for his father, another for his wife and children, this one for his brothers, that one for his friends, all for the Troglodyte people. The place of the man who died was at once taken by another who, besides the common cause, had now a personal death to avenge.

Such was the combat between injustice and virtue. Those cowardly peoples, who sought only plunder, were not ashamed to flee; and they yielded to the virtue of the Troglodytes even without being touched by it.

*From Erzurum, the 9th day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson handles the exemplary structure of the letter well and keeps the sequence of civic vignettes clear. His version is close in content, but sometimes shades moral force into smoother literary phrasing.

### Main differences
- **Family curse**
  - French: `puisse-t-il mourir le dernier de sa famille`
  - Fresh translation: `may he die the last of his family`
  - Davidson: `may he outlive all his family`
  - The meanings converge, but Davidson's phrasing is more indirect than the French curse formula.

- **Wish for the thieves**
  - French: `je souhaiterois que les dieux leur en donnassent un plus long usage qu'à moi`
  - Fresh translation preserves the startling generosity of the line.
  - Davidson is close, though slightly more polished.

- **Wild beasts**
  - French: `nous vous traiterons comme des bêtes farouches`
  - Fresh translation: `we will treat you like wild beasts`
  - Davidson's wording is similar, but the French's bluntness matters to the moral contrast.

- **Virtue in battle**
  - French: `ils cédèrent à la vertu des Troglodytes`
  - Fresh translation keeps `virtue`, not simply `prowess`.
  - Davidson's ending edges toward martial admiration, where the French retains the ethical framework.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: exemplary, severe, and civic; even the battle scene remains moralized.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the proverb-like simplicity of the opening examples and the ethical framing of the conclusion.
- **Davidson**: narratively effective, though a little more heroic and less austere.

## Working conclusion
Letter XIII continues the Troglodyte sequence by turning virtue into habit, generosity, and military courage. Davidson remains close, but his diction sometimes shifts the balance from moral demonstration toward literary color.
