## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter III**

**Zachi to Usbek, at Tabriz**

### Translation
We ordered the chief of the eunuchs to take us into the country; he will tell you that no mishap befell us. When we had to cross the river and leave our litters, we placed ourselves, according to custom, in boxes: two slaves carried us on their shoulders, and so we escaped every gaze.

How could I have gone on living, dear Usbek, in your seraglio at Ispahan, in those places which, by constantly recalling my past pleasures, inflamed my desires each day with fresh violence? I wandered from apartment to apartment, always looking for you and never finding you, yet everywhere encountering the cruel memory of my past happiness. At one moment I saw myself again in the place where, for the first time in my life, I received you in my arms; at another, in the room where you settled that famous quarrel among your wives. Each of us claimed to surpass the others in beauty. We appeared before you after exhausting everything that imagination can supply in adornment and ornament: you looked with pleasure on the miracles of our art; you admired how far the ardor to please you had carried us.

But you soon made those borrowed charms yield to graces more natural; you undid all our work: we had to strip off those ornaments which had become burdensome to you; we had to appear before your eyes in the simplicity of nature. I counted modesty as nothing; I thought only of my glory. Happy Usbek, what charms were spread before your eyes! Long we saw you wander from enchantment to enchantment: your uncertain soul remained for a long time unable to settle; every new grace demanded its tribute from you; in an instant we were all covered with your kisses; your curious eyes were carried into the most secret places; in a moment you made us pass through a thousand different positions; always new commands, and always new obedience. I confess it to you, Usbek: a passion even more vivid than ambition made me desire to please you.

Little by little I saw myself becoming the mistress of your heart; you took me, you left me, you came back to me, and I knew how to keep you: triumph was wholly mine, and despair belonged to my rivals. It seemed to us that we were alone in the world: everything around us was no longer worthy to occupy us. Would to Heaven my rivals had had the courage to remain witnesses to all the marks of love I received from you! Had they truly seen my transports, they would have felt the difference between my love and theirs; they would have seen that, if they might contend with me in beauty, they could not contend with me in feeling... But where am I? Where is this vain recital carrying me? It is a misfortune not to be loved; but it is an insult to be loved no longer. You leave us, Usbek, to go wandering through barbarous climates. What! do you count for nothing the advantage of being loved? Alas, you do not even know what you are losing! I breathe sighs that are not heard; my tears fall, and you do not enjoy them; it seems as though love itself breathes through the seraglio, and your insensibility keeps taking you farther and farther from it. Ah, my dear Usbek, if you knew how to be happy!

*From the seraglio of Fatmé, the 21st day of the moon of Maharram, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures the broad emotional arc of the letter well: confinement, erotic recollection, rivalry among the wives, and the final reproach against Usbek's absence. His version is vivid and often elegant, but it tends to smooth the French into a more romantic and literary register.

### Main differences
- **Opening logistics and visibility**
  - French: `nous échappâmes à tous les regards`
  - Fresh translation: `we escaped every gaze`
  - Davidson: `we were seen by nobody`
  - Davidson is accurate but flatter; the French has a more theatrical sense of evasion.

- **Intensity of remembered desire**
  - French: `irritoient tous les jours mes désirs avec une nouvelle violence`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stronger force: memory inflames desire with renewed violence.
  - Davidson's `provoking daily my desires with renewed vehemence` is good, but slightly more decorous.

- **Glory vs conquest**
  - French: `je ne pensai qu'à ma gloire`
  - Fresh translation: `I thought only of my glory`
  - Davidson: `I thought only of conquest`
  - This is a significant tonal shift. `Glory` preserves vanity, triumph, and rank; `conquest` makes the line more aggressively erotic than the French requires.

- **Usbek's gaze**
  - French: `tes curieux regards`
  - Fresh translation: `your curious eyes`
  - Davidson: `your eager looks`
  - Davidson changes curiosity into appetite, which intensifies the sensual tone.

- **Sensitivity / feeling**
  - French: `elles ne pouvoient pas disputer de sensibilité`
  - Fresh translation: `they could not contend with me in feeling`
  - Davidson: `they could not vie with me in tenderness`
  - `Tenderness` is graceful, but `sensibilité` is broader and slightly less sentimental.

- **Final reproach**
  - French: `c'est un affront de ne l'être plus`
  - Fresh translation: `it is an insult to be loved no longer`
  - Davidson: `to have love withdrawn from one is an outrage`
  - Davidson is strong here and close in force, though less direct in syntax.

- **Striking closing line**
  - French: `si tu savois être heureux`
  - Fresh translation: `if you knew how to be happy`
  - Davidson: `if you only knew your happiness`
  - Davidson changes the sense. The French suggests incapacity or failure in Usbek himself, not ignorance of a happiness already possessed.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: intensely sensuous, competitive, wounded, and theatrical; Zachi moves between recollection, boasting, complaint, and accusation.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve both the erotic bravado and the abrupt self-conscious turn in the final paragraph.
- **Davidson**: often heightens the romance while slightly reducing the sharper vanity and accusation in the French.

## Working conclusion
Letter III widens the comparative pattern:
- Davidson is strongest when carrying the large emotional movement of the letter
- but he frequently substitutes a smoother Victorian emotional vocabulary for sharper French terms like `gloire` and `sensibilité`
- and the final line is especially important, because it changes Zachi's accusation from `you do not know how to be happy` into the softer `you do not know your happiness`.
