### Heading
**Letter LIV**

**Rica to Usbek**

### Translation
This morning I was in my room, which, as you know, is separated from the others only by a very thin partition pierced in several places, so that one hears everything said in the neighboring room. A man, pacing with long strides, was saying to another: I do not know what it is, but everything turns against me. It has been more than three days since I have said anything that did me credit; in every conversation I have found myself jumbled in with the rest, without the least attention being paid to me, or anyone addressing a word to me twice. I had prepared a few sallies to brighten my discourse; no one would allow me to bring them in. I had a very pretty story to tell; but every time I tried to edge it closer, it was dodged as though on purpose. I have a few bons mots which for four days have been growing old in my head, without my being able to make the least use of them. If this continues, I believe I shall end by becoming a fool: it seems to be my star, and I cannot escape it. Yesterday I had hoped to shine with three or four old women who certainly do not overawe me, and I was going to say the prettiest things in the world. For more than a quarter of an hour I steered the conversation toward them; but they never kept to a continuous subject, and, like the fatal Fates, they cut the thread of all my speeches. Shall I tell you? The reputation of a wit is dearly bought. I do not know how you managed to attain it.

"An idea occurs to me," replied the other. "Let us work together to give ourselves wit; let us form a partnership for that purpose. Every day each of us will tell the other what he ought to talk about; and we shall support one another so well that if someone comes to interrupt us in the middle of our ideas, we shall draw him in ourselves, and if he will not come willingly, we shall force him. We shall agree on the moments when one must approve, on those when one must smile, and on others when one must laugh outright and with full throat. You will see that we shall set the tone in every conversation, and that people will admire the liveliness of our minds and the felicity of our repartees. We shall protect one another with mutual nods. You will shine today; tomorrow you will be my second. I shall enter a house with you and cry out, pointing to you: I must tell you a very amusing reply this gentleman just made to a man we met in the street. Then I shall turn toward you: He did not expect it; he was greatly astonished. I shall recite some of my verses, and you will say: I was there when he wrote them; it was at a supper, and he did not pause for a moment. Often, indeed, we shall rally one another, and people will say: See how they attack, how they defend themselves; they do not spare each other; let us see how he comes out of this; admirable! what presence of mind! this is a real battle. But no one will say that we had already skirmished the day before. We shall have to buy certain books, collections of bons mots composed for the use of those who have no wit and want to imitate it: everything depends on having models. I want us, before six months are out, to be capable of sustaining an hour's conversation made up entirely of good sayings. But we must pay attention to one thing: we must sustain their fortune. It is not enough to utter a good saying; it must be spread and sown everywhere, otherwise it is as good as lost. And I confess to you that nothing is so disheartening as to see a pretty thing one has said die in the ear of a fool who hears it. True, there is often some compensation: the foolish things we say also pass incognito; and that is the only thing that can console us on such occasions. There, my dear fellow, is the course we must take. Do as I tell you, and I promise you a place in the Academy before six months are over. That is to say the labor will not be long; for then you may renounce your art, and you will be a wit whether you like it or not. It is observed in France that as soon as a man enters a company, he at once catches what is called the esprit du corps; you will be the same, and the only thing I fear for you is embarrassment from applause."

*At Paris, the 6th day of the moon of Zilcadé, 1714.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures the comic premise very well: an unsuccessful wit laments his repeated failures in society, and his friend proposes an absurdly methodical scheme for manufacturing brilliance through rehearsal, cueing, and mutual promotion.

### Main differences
- **`Saillies` and social wit**
  - Fresh translation: `a few sallies to brighten my discourse`
  - Davidson: `some brilliant passages to lighten my conversation`
  - Davidson is readable, but `brilliant passages` sounds more literary and prepared than the quick conversational thrust of `saillies`.

- **Managing interruptions**
  - French: `nous l'attirerons nous-mêmes; et s'il ne veut pas venir de bon gré, nous lui ferons violence`
  - Fresh translation keeps the stage-management and mild violence: `we shall draw him in ourselves ... we shall force him`
  - Davidson turns this into `we shall inspire him with admiration` and then `he will be coerced`, which blunts the joke that they will physically haul the conversation back under control.

- **`Second` vs `foil`**
  - Fresh translation: `tomorrow you will be my second`
  - Davidson: `to-morrow you will be my foil`
  - `Second` preserves the duel / military coloring of the French; `foil` pushes the line more explicitly toward theatrical contrast.

- **The military metaphor of rehearsal**
  - French: `nous nous étions escarmouchés dès la veille`
  - Fresh translation: `we had already skirmished the day before`
  - Davidson: `we practised it all beforehand`
  - Davidson gives the sense, but loses the comic martial metaphor.

- **`Sustain their fortune`**
  - French: `soutenir leur fortune`
  - Fresh translation keeps the idea that a bon mot needs a social career.
  - Davidson's `the fate of our witticisms` is apt, but the French more strongly suggests active reputation-management.

- **`L'esprit du corps`**
  - Davidson keeps the French phrase itself.
  - The fresh translation leaves the phrase in French too, because the point is precisely that one catches the collective social manner of a group.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: quick, theatrical, maliciously observant, and very alert to conversation as performance.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the eavesdropped immediacy and the increasingly elaborate absurdity of the proposed scheme.
- **Davidson**: often very funny, but slightly more literary and polished than the sharp salon satire of the French.

## Working conclusion
Letter LIV satirizes social wit as something manufactured by rehearsal, signaling, and mutual inflation. Davidson preserves the scene effectively, but the fresh translation stays a little closer to the French's idiom of sallies, skirmishes, and conversational stagecraft.
