## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXVI**

**Usbek to Roxane, at the seraglio in Isfahan**

### Translation
How happy you are, Roxane, to be in the gentle land of Persia and not in these poisoned climates where neither modesty nor virtue is known. How happy you are! You live in my seraglio as in the abode of innocence, inaccessible to the assaults of all humankind. You dwell, with joy, in a fortunate incapacity to go astray. No man has ever sullied you with his lascivious looks. Your father-in-law himself, in the freedom of banquets, has never seen your lovely mouth: you have never failed to fasten over it a sacred veil. Happy Roxane! When you have gone into the country, eunuchs have always walked before you, dealing death to every rash man who did not flee at your sight. And I myself, to whom heaven gave you to make my happiness, what pains did I not have to become master of that treasure you defended with such constancy! What sorrow for me, in the first days of our marriage, not to see you! And what impatience when I did see you! Yet you did not satisfy it: on the contrary, you inflamed it by the obstinate refusals of an alarmed modesty. You confused me with all those men from whom you hide yourself without ceasing. Do you remember the day when I lost you among your slaves, who betrayed me and hid you from my search? Do you remember that other day when, seeing your tears had no effect, you used your mother's authority to arrest the furies of my love? Do you remember, when every other resource failed you, the one you found in your courage? You took a dagger in hand and threatened to immolate a husband who loved you, if he continued demanding from you what you cherished more than your husband himself. Two months passed in that combat of love and virtue. You carried your chaste scruples too far. You did not surrender even after being overcome. You defended, to the last extremity, a dying virginity. You looked on me as an enemy who had outraged you, not as a husband who had loved you. For more than three months you did not dare look at me without blushing. Your embarrassed air seemed to reproach me for the advantage I had taken. I did not even possess you in peace; you hid from me, as much as you could, those charms and graces, and I was intoxicated by the greatest favors without having obtained the least.

If you had been brought up in this country, you would not have been so troubled. The women here have lost all reserve. They present themselves before men with uncovered face, as if they wanted to ask for their defeat. They seek men with their looks. They see them in mosques, on walks, and in their very homes. The custom of being served by eunuchs is unknown to them. Instead of that noble simplicity and amiable modesty that reign among you, one sees a brutal shamelessness to which it is impossible to grow accustomed.

Yes, Roxane, if you were here, you would feel yourself outraged by the frightful ignominy to which your sex has fallen. You would flee those abominable places and sigh for that sweet retreat where you find innocence, where you are secure in yourself, where no danger makes you tremble, and where at last you can love me without fearing ever to lose the love you owe me.

When you heighten the brilliance of your complexion with the finest colors; when you perfume your whole body with the most precious essences; when you put on your most beautiful garments; when you seek to distinguish yourself from your companions by the graces of dance and the sweetness of song; when you graciously contend with them in charms, sweetness, and liveliness, I cannot imagine that you have any other object than pleasing me. And when I see you blush modestly, your eyes seeking mine, and hear you insinuate yourself into my heart by soft and flattering words, I cannot, Roxane, doubt your love.

But what am I to think of the women of Europe? The art of composing their complexions, the ornaments with which they adorn themselves, the care they take of their persons, the continual desire to please that occupies them — all these are so many stains laid upon their virtue and so many affronts to their husbands.

It is not, Roxane, that I think they carry the offense as far as such conduct might make one believe, or that they push debauchery to that horrible excess — enough to make one shudder — of wholly violating conjugal faith. There are very few women abandoned enough to carry crime so far. All carry within their hearts a certain mark of virtue engraved there, which birth gives and education weakens but does not destroy. They may relax in the outward duties modesty requires; but when the last steps are at issue, nature revolts. Thus when we confine you so tightly, have you guarded by so many slaves, and restrain your desires so strongly when they fly too far, it is not because we fear the final infidelity. It is because we know that purity cannot be too great and that the slightest stain can corrupt it.

I pity you, Roxane. Your chastity, so long put to the proof, deserved a husband who had never left you and who could himself restrain the desires that your virtue alone knows how to subdue.

*From Paris, the 7th day of the moon of Rhégeb, 1712.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson reproduces the broad argument well: Persia as enforced innocence, Europe as shameless display, and Roxane as the idealized wife whose resistance once heightened desire. Still, his English often smooths Usbek's self-incriminating logic and sometimes makes the violence of possession sound more courtly than it does in the French.

### Main differences
- **Mastery versus possession**
  - French: `me rendre maître de ce trésor`
  - Fresh translation: `become master of that treasure`
  - Davidson: `entering upon the possession of that treasure`
  - Davidson softens the language of mastery at a crucial point in the letter.

- **Confusing husband with all other men**
  - French: `vous me confondiez avec tous ces hommes`
  - Fresh translation: `You confused me with all those men`
  - Davidson: `You failed to distinguish between me and all other men`
  - Davidson is accurate in sense, but the French is more accusatory and more humiliating in its phrasing.

- **Dying virginity**
  - French: `une virginité mourante`
  - Fresh translation keeps the violent and paradoxical phrase.
  - Davidson also keeps it, though the surrounding syntax is somewhat more polished and decorous.

- **The line about favors**
  - French: `j'étois enivré des plus grandes faveurs sans avoir obtenu les moindres`
  - Fresh translation preserves the paradox.
  - Davidson's `without having received the least favours, I was ravished with the greatest` is close, but the French's contradiction is one of the revealing absurdities of Usbek's voice and should remain stark.

- **Ask for their defeat**
  - French: `comme si elles vouloient demander leur défaite`
  - Fresh translation preserves the martial-erotic metaphor.
  - Davidson's `as if they sought their overthrow` is close, though slightly more abstract.

- **Charms, sweetness, and liveliness**
  - French: `de charmes, de douceur et d'enjouement`
  - Fresh translation: `charms, sweetness, and liveliness`
  - Davidson: `beauty, tenderness, in vivacity`
  - Davidson shifts the first two terms away from the French's exact set of social and sensual qualities.

- **Love you owe me**
  - French: `l'amour que vous me devez`
  - Fresh translation: `the love you owe me`
  - Davidson: `that love which it is your duty to feel for me`
  - Davidson makes the duty explicit; the French is colder and more proprietary because it states the debt more directly.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: self-justifying, controlling, erotically possessive, and conceptually revealing.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve the way tenderness and domination remain inseparable in Usbek's voice.
- **Davidson**: often very close, but his smoother phrasing can make Usbek seem less brutal and less absurdly self-blinding.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXVI is an important comparison letter because the content is already self-contradictory: Usbek praises confinement as innocence while recalling erotic conquest as marital virtue. Davidson preserves the main line, but the French's language of mastery, coercion, and possessive entitlement should remain sharper.
