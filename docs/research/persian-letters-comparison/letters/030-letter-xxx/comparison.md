## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XXX**

**Rica to the same, at Smyrna**

### Translation
The inhabitants of Paris have a curiosity that goes as far as extravagance. When I arrived, I was looked at as if I had been sent from heaven. Old men, men, women, children — everyone wanted to see me. If I went out, everyone set themselves at the windows. If I was in the Tuileries, I at once saw a circle forming around me; the women themselves made a rainbow, shaded in a thousand colors, that surrounded me. If I was at the spectacles, I immediately saw a hundred lorgnettes raised against my face. In short, no man has ever been so much looked at as I was. I sometimes smiled to hear people who had scarcely ever left their room saying to one another, `One must admit that he looks very Persian.` Admirable thing! I found my portraits everywhere. I saw myself multiplied in every shop, on every mantelpiece, so fearful were they of not having seen enough of me.

All these honors were still burdensome. I did not think myself so curious and rare a man; and, though I have a very good opinion of myself, I would never have imagined that I should disturb the peace of a great city where I was not known. That made me decide to leave off Persian dress and put on a European one, to see whether there would still remain in my face anything admirable. This experiment taught me what I was really worth. Free of all foreign ornaments, I saw myself valued at the just rate. I had reason to complain of my tailor, who had in an instant made me lose public attention and esteem; for I suddenly entered into a dreadful nothingness. I would sometimes remain an hour in a company without having been looked at or given occasion to open my mouth. But if someone happened to inform the company that I was Persian, I at once heard around me a buzzing: `Ah! ah! Monsieur is Persian? That is a very extraordinary thing! How can one be Persian?`

*From Paris, the 6th day of the moon of Chalval, 1712.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson's version of this famous letter is very readable and keeps the central joke intact: Rica is fascinating as an exotic costume and invisible as soon as the costume disappears. The differences are mostly in the handling of key images and in a few places where Davidson paraphrases too freely.

### Main differences
- **Sent from heaven**
  - French: `comme si j'avois été envoyé du ciel`
  - Fresh translation: `as if I had been sent from heaven`
  - Davidson: `as if I had dropped from the sky`
  - Davidson is lively, but the French has a slightly more pointed mock-messianic flavor.

- **At the spectacles**
  - French: `Si j'étois aux spectacles`
  - Fresh translation: `If I was at the spectacles`
  - Davidson: `When I went sight-seeing`
  - This is a meaningful shift. Rica is talking about theaters and entertainments, not about sightseeing in general.

- **Foreign ornaments**
  - French: `Libre de tous les ornements étrangers`
  - Fresh translation preserves the language of external adornment.
  - Davidson's `Divested of everything foreign in my garb` is close, though slightly more explanatory.

- **A dreadful nothingness**
  - French: `un néant affreux`
  - Fresh translation: `a dreadful nothingness`
  - Davidson: `the merest nonentity`
  - Davidson catches the sense, but the French has a more abrupt existential force.

- **The final question**
  - French: `Comment peut-on être Persan?`
  - Fresh translation: `How can one be Persian?`
  - Davidson's `However can anybody be a Persian?` is memorable and close, though a touch more expansive.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: brisk, comic, self-aware, and sharpened by social observation.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep the speed of Rica's anecdote and the bluntness of its social conclusion.
- **Davidson**: lively and quotable, though he occasionally substitutes a freer idiom for the exact theatrical and sartorial imagery.

## Working conclusion
Letter XXX remains one of the clearest demonstrations of Rica's social method. Davidson preserves the famous punchline well, but his mistranslation of `spectacles` and his slightly freer handling of key images show why a fresh translation is still worthwhile.
