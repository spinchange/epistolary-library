## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XI**

**Usbek to Mirza, in Isfahan**

### Translation
You renounce your own reason in order to try mine; you lower yourself to consult me; you believe me capable of instructing you. My dear Mirza, there is one thing that flatters me even more than the good opinion you have formed of me: it is the friendship that procures it for me.

To carry out what you ask of me, I did not think I should employ highly abstract arguments. There are certain truths that it is not enough to persuade people of; they must also be made to feel them. Such are the truths of morality. Perhaps this piece of history will touch you more than subtle philosophy.

There was in Arabia a small people called the Troglodytes, descended from those ancient Troglodytes who, if we believe historians, resembled beasts more than men. These were not so misshapen, nor hairy as bears, nor did they whistle; they had eyes. But they were so wicked and so ferocious that there was among them no principle of equity or justice.

They had a king of foreign origin who, wishing to correct the wickedness of their nature, treated them severely. They conspired against him, killed him, and exterminated the whole royal family.

Once the deed was done, they assembled to choose a government; and after many dissensions they created magistrates. But scarcely had they elected them before they found them unbearable and massacred them too.

Freed from this new yoke, the people consulted only their savage nature. Every individual agreed that he would obey no one any longer, and that each would look only to his own interest, without consulting that of others.

This unanimous resolution greatly pleased them all. They said: Why should I go kill myself working for people I do not care about? I shall think only of myself. I shall live happily; what matters it to me whether others are happy? I will secure all my own needs, and provided I have them I do not care whether all the other Troglodytes are miserable.

It was the month for sowing the land. Each man said: I will till my field only enough for it to provide the grain I need for food. A greater quantity would be useless to me. I will take no trouble for nothing.

The lands of that little kingdom were not all of the same sort. Some were barren and mountainous; others, lying low, were watered by several streams. That year the drought was very great, so that the high lands failed entirely, while those that could be watered were highly fertile. Thus the mountain people almost all perished of hunger through the hardness of the others, who refused to share the harvest.

The following year was very rainy. The high places found themselves extraordinarily fertile, while the low lands were flooded. Half the people cried famine a second time; but those wretches found others as hard as they themselves had been.

One of the principal inhabitants had a very beautiful wife. His neighbor fell in love with her and carried her off. A great quarrel arose, and after many insults and blows they agreed to submit to the decision of a Troglodyte who had held some credit while the republic lasted. They went to him and wanted to tell him their reasons. What does it matter to me, said that man, whether the woman belongs to you or to you? I have my field to plow. I will not perhaps use my time settling your disputes and working at your affairs while neglecting my own. Leave me in peace, and trouble me no more with your quarrels. With that he left them and went to work his land. The ravisher, who was the stronger, swore he would die rather than restore the woman. The other, pierced by his neighbor's injustice and the judge's hardness, went home in despair when on the road he met a young and beautiful woman returning from the fountain. Since he no longer had a wife, that one pleased him; and she pleased him still more when he learned she was the wife of the very man he had wanted as judge and who had shown so little feeling for his misfortune. He carried her off and took her to his house.

There was also a man who possessed a fairly fertile field and cultivated it with great care. Two of his neighbors joined together, drove him from his house, and occupied his field. They made a pact to defend themselves against anyone who might try to usurp it, and they in fact held out in that way for several months. But one of the two, tired of sharing what he might have alone, killed the other and became sole master of the field. His dominion did not last long: two other Troglodytes came to attack him, and as he was too weak to defend himself, he was massacred.

A Troglodyte almost naked saw wool for sale and asked the price. The merchant said to himself: naturally I should hope for from my wool only as much money as is needed to buy two measures of grain, but I will sell it for four times as much and thus have eight measures. The buyer had to submit and pay the demanded price. I am glad of it, said the merchant; now I shall have grain. What is that? replied the stranger. You need grain? I have some to sell, but the price may astonish you. Grain is very dear, and famine reigns almost everywhere. Give me back my money and I will give you a single measure of grain, for I will not part with it otherwise, though you should burst with hunger.

Meanwhile a cruel disease ravaged the land. A skillful physician arrived from a neighboring country and administered his remedies so aptly that he cured all who placed themselves in his hands. When the illness had ceased, he went to everyone he had treated to ask his fee, but found nothing but refusals. He returned to his own country, overwhelmed by the fatigues of such a long journey. Soon after, however, he learned that the same disease had returned and afflicted that ungrateful land more than ever. This time they went to him and did not wait for him to come to them. Go away, he said to them, unjust men. You have in your souls a poison more deadly than the one from which you want healing. You do not deserve to occupy a place on earth, because you have no humanity and the rules of equity are unknown to you. I would think I was offending the gods, who punish you, if I opposed the justice of their anger.

*From Erzurum, the 3rd day of the second moon of Gemmadi, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson tells the Troglodyte parable clearly and with energy. He handles the sequence of examples well, but he often turns the French's moral plainness into a more literary or fabulist idiom.

### Main differences
- **History, not fable**
  - French: `ce morceau d'histoire`
  - Fresh translation: `this piece of history`
  - Davidson: `the following fable`
  - That is a meaningful reframing: Usbek presents the story as historical moral illustration, not as an admitted fable.

- **Anthropological oddity**
  - French: `ils avoient des yeux`
  - Fresh translation keeps the absurdly flat `they had eyes`.
  - Davidson embellishes the description into something more stylistically polished.

- **Self-interest formula**
  - French: `chacun veilleroit uniquement à ses intérêts`
  - Fresh translation: `each would look only to his own interest`
  - Davidson generalizes the idea into broader moral prose, slightly softening the aphoristic sharpness.

- **Merchant scene**
  - French: `dussiez-vous crever de faim`
  - Fresh translation: `though you should burst with hunger`
  - Davidson's `though you were to die of hunger` is faithful in sense, but the French is more crude and contemptuous.

- **Physician's curse**
  - French: `vous avez dans l'âme un poison plus mortel`
  - Fresh translation: `you have in your souls a poison more deadly`
  - Davidson is close, though his surrounding sentence is more sermonic.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: moral, severe, and structurally simple; its power comes from accumulation rather than ornament.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to keep that fable-like sequence without turning it into polished allegory.
- **Davidson**: highly readable, but more literary and occasionally more explanatory than the French.

## Working conclusion
Letter XI is a foundational moral letter. Davidson represents it competently, but his decision to call the narrative a `fable` and his generally elevated diction slightly change the way Usbek presents moral knowledge.
