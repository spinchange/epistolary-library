## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXVIII**

**Rica to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
You have heard a thousand times of the famous king of Sweden: he was besieging a town in a kingdom called Norway; while he was visiting the trenches, alone with an engineer, he received a shot in the head from which he died. His chief minister was immediately arrested; the estates assembled and condemned him to lose his head.

He was accused of a great crime: he had slandered the nation and made it lose the confidence of its king - an offense that, in my opinion, deserves a thousand deaths.

For if it is a bad action to blacken in a prince's mind the lowest of his subjects, what is it when one blackens an entire nation and deprives it of the goodwill of him whom providence has established to make it happy?

I would like men to speak to kings as angels speak to our holy Prophet.

You know that in the sacred banquets, where the Lord of lords descends from the most sublime throne in the world to communicate himself to his slaves, I made it a strict law to keep an unruly tongue in captivity; no one has ever seen me let fall a single word that could be bitter to the least of his subjects. When I had to cease being sober, I did not cease being an honest man; and, in that test of our fidelity, I risked my life and never my virtue.

I do not know how it happens that there is almost never a prince so wicked that his minister is not more wicked still. If the prince commits some evil action, it has almost always been suggested to him; so the ambition of princes is never as dangerous as the baseness of soul in their advisers. But can you understand how a man who is only of yesterday in the ministry, who perhaps will not be there tomorrow, can in a moment become the enemy of himself, his family, his country, and the people yet to be born from those he is about to oppress?

A prince has passions; the minister stirs them up. That is the side from which he conducts his ministry; he has no other aim, and wishes to know no other. Courtiers seduce the prince by their praises; and the minister flatters him more dangerously by his advice, by the designs he inspires in him, and by the maxims he proposes to him.

*Paris, the 25th day of the moon of Saphar, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the broad polemical line of the letter: Charles XII's death occasions a reflection on the minister who alienates a nation from its prince, and from there Rica expands into a general attack on corrupt political counsel.

### Main differences
- **Slandering a nation**
  - French: `avoir calomnié la nation`
  - Fresh translation keeps the legal-moral force of `slandered the nation`.
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh draft keeps the accusation sounding less generalized and more forensic.

- **`captiver une langue indocile`**
  - Fresh translation: `keep an unruly tongue in captivity`
  - Davidson's `restrain an unruly tongue` is good idiomatic English, but the French is more visibly coercive.

- **`Quand il m'a fallu cesser d'être sobre`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the line almost literally.
  - Davidson also preserves it, but the fresh draft makes the ethical contrast with `honest man` stand out more sharply.

- **Ministerial evil as derivative and amplifying**
  - French makes the minister both the mover of princely passion and the active designer of oppression.
  - Davidson captures this well, though the fresh draft keeps the repeated structure of `by his advice... by the designs... by the maxims` more tightly.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: indignant, aphoristic, and politically moralizing.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the public accusation and the widening reflection on counsel and power.
- **Davidson**: strong overall, with only minor smoothing and some Victorian elevation of tone.

### Notable OCR / edition notes

## Working conclusion
Davidson gives a solid version of Letter CXXVIII. The fresh draft mainly restores a little more bite to the political accusations and a little more pressure to the language of moral self-command.
