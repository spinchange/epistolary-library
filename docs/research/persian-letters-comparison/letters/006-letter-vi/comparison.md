## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter VI**

**Usbek to his friend Nessir, in Isfahan**

### Translation
A day's journey from Yerevan, we left Persia and entered lands under Turkish obedience. Twelve days later we arrived at Erzurum, where we shall stay three or four months.

I must confess it to you, Nessir: I felt a secret pain when Persia disappeared from view and I found myself among the perfidious Ottomans. As I advanced into the country of those profane people, it seemed to me that I myself was becoming profane.

My country, my family, my friends came back before my mind; my tenderness awoke again; and a certain uneasiness completed my disturbance and made me see that, for the sake of my peace, I had undertaken too much.

But what most afflicts my heart is my wives. I cannot think of them without being consumed by grief.

This is not, Nessir, because I love them. In that respect I find in myself an insensibility that leaves me without desires. In the crowded seraglio where I lived, I forestalled love and destroyed it by itself; but out of that very coldness there arises a secret jealousy that devours me. I see a troop of women left almost to themselves; I have only cowardly souls to answer for them. I would scarcely feel secure if my slaves were faithful; what will it be if they are not? What sad news may reach me from the distant countries I am going to travel through! It is an evil for which my friends can provide no remedy. It is a place whose sad secrets they must not know; and what could they do there anyway? Would I not a thousand times rather have obscure impunity than a spectacular punishment? I place all my sorrows in your heart, my dear Nessir: it is the only consolation left to me in the state I am in.

*From Erzurum, the 10th day of the second moon of Rebiab, 1711.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the letter's movement from homesickness into jealousy. He is strong on the broad psychology, but he smooths several sharper turns in Usbek's self-description.

### Main differences
- **Profane language**
  - French: `il me sembloit que je devenois profane moi-même`
  - Fresh translation: `it seemed to me that I myself was becoming profane`
  - Davidson: `I become more and more of a pagan`
  - `Profane` is the key word in the French; `pagan` Christianizes and over-specifies it.

- **Cowardly souls**
  - French: `je n'ai que des âmes lâches qui m'en répondent`
  - Fresh translation: `I have only cowardly souls to answer for them`
  - Davidson: `except some low-minded wretches, no one is answerable`
  - Davidson recasts the line more freely and loses the compact contempt of `âmes lâches`.

- **Jealousy from coldness**
  - French: `de ma froideur même, il sort une jalousie secrète`
  - Fresh translation keeps the paradox: jealousy emerges from coldness itself.
  - Davidson's version is close, but slightly more explanatory.

- **Obscure impunity**
  - French: `une obscure impunité`
  - Fresh translation: `obscure impunity`
  - Davidson: `such faults should remain unknown because uncorrected`
  - Davidson explains the thought well, but the French's terse formulation is more striking.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: intimate, anxious, and morally uneasy; homesickness quickly curdles into possessive jealousy.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the self-accusing and self-protective tension in Usbek's voice.
- **Davidson**: faithful in general sense, but somewhat more expansive and interpretive.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text includes a lingering footnote marker in the second paragraph.
- Otherwise the letter is usable for comparison without major reconstruction.

## Working conclusion
Letter VI matters because it establishes Usbek's emotional logic early: coldness does not cancel domination, but breeds jealousy. Davidson gets that, though some of the French's harsher phrasing is softened or generalized.
