## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter CXXXI**

**Rhedi to Rica, at Paris**

### Translation
One of the things that most stirred my curiosity on arriving in Europe is the history and origin of republics. You know that most Asians do not even have an idea of this sort of government, and that imagination has not served them enough even to let them understand that there could be any other on earth than the despotic one.

The first governments in the world were monarchical: republics were formed only by chance and over the succession of centuries.

After Greece had been engulfed by a flood, new inhabitants came to people it. It drew almost all its colonies from Egypt and the neighboring regions of Asia; and, as those countries were governed by kings, the peoples who came from them were governed in the same way. But when the tyranny of those princes became too oppressive, the yoke was shaken off; and from the debris of so many kingdoms arose those republics that made Greece flourish so greatly, alone refined in the midst of barbarians.

Love of liberty and hatred of kings preserved Greece for a long time in independence and spread republican government far and wide. The Greek cities found allies in Asia Minor; they sent there colonies as free as themselves, which served them as ramparts against the enterprises of the kings of Persia. Nor is that all: Greece peopled Italy; Italy, Spain, and perhaps Gaul. It is known that great Hesperia, so famous among the ancients, was at first Greece, which its neighbors regarded as an abode of blessedness. The Greeks who did not find that happy land at home went to seek it in Italy; those of Italy, in Spain; those of Spain, in Baetica or Portugal. Thus all these regions bore that name among the ancients. Those Greek colonies brought with them a spirit of liberty they had acquired in that gentle land. Thus one scarcely sees, in those remote ages, monarchies in Italy, Spain, or Gaul. One will soon see that the peoples of the North and of Germany were no less free; and, if one finds traces of some royalty among them, it is because leaders of armies or republics have been mistaken for kings.

All this happened in Europe; for Asia and Africa have always been crushed under despotism, except for some cities of Asia Minor of which we have spoken, and the republic of Carthage in Africa.

The world was divided between two powerful republics: that of Rome and that of Carthage. Nothing is so well known as the beginnings of the Roman republic, and nothing is so little known as the origin of the Carthaginian one. We know absolutely nothing of the succession of African princes after Dido, or of how they lost their power. The prodigious growth of the Roman republic would have been a great blessing for the world had there not been that unjust difference between Roman citizens and conquered peoples; had governors of provinces been given less authority; had the laws, so sacred in preventing their tyranny, been observed; and had those governors not used, to silence those laws, the very treasures their injustice had amassed.

It seems liberty was made for the genius of the peoples of Europe, and servitude for that of the peoples of Asia. It was in vain that the Romans offered the Cappadocians that precious treasure: that cowardly nation refused it, and rushed toward servitude with the same eagerness that other peoples ran toward liberty.

Caesar crushed the Roman republic and subjected it to arbitrary power.

Europe groaned for a long time under a violent military government, and Roman mildness was changed into cruel oppression.

Meanwhile an infinity of unknown nations came out of the North and spread like torrents through the Roman provinces; and, finding it as easy to make conquests as to practice piracy, they dismembered them and made kingdoms of them. These peoples were free, and they limited the authority of their kings so greatly that they were properly only chiefs or generals. Thus those kingdoms, though founded by force, did not feel the yoke of the conqueror. When the peoples of Asia, like the Turks and Tartars, made conquests, subject as they were to the will of a single man, they thought only of giving him new subjects and establishing his violent authority by arms. But the peoples of the North, free in their own countries, when they seized the Roman provinces, did not give their chiefs much authority. Some of those peoples, like the Vandals in Africa and the Goths in Spain, deposed their kings as soon as they were dissatisfied with them; and among the others the authority of the prince was limited in a thousand different ways: a great number of lords shared it with him; wars were undertaken only with their consent; the spoils were divided between the chief and the soldiers; no tax existed for the benefit of the prince; laws were made in assemblies of the nation. There is the fundamental principle of all those states that formed themselves from the ruins of the Roman Empire.

*Venice, the 20th day of the moon of Rhegeb, 1719.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the broad political sweep of the letter: republics arise historically, Europe is marked by a recurrent capacity for liberty, Asia by despotism, and the post-Roman northern peoples carry forms of limited kingship into the new kingdoms.

### Main differences
- **`seule polie au milieu des barbares`**
  - Fresh translation keeps Greece `alone refined in the midst of barbarians`.
  - Davidson's `the only cultured nation among a crowd of barbarians` is close, but slightly more expansive than the French.

- **`la douceur romaine`**
  - Fresh translation: `Roman mildness`
  - Davidson's `the gentle Roman sway` interprets and politicizes the phrase a bit more.
  - The French is terser and more aphoristic.

- **`pirateries`**
  - Fresh translation keeps the explicit comparison between conquest and piracy.
  - Davidson's `to rob` gets the point, though it is less historically pointed than `piracy`.

- **`aucun impôt en faveur du prince`**
  - Fresh translation restores the clause that no tax existed for the prince's benefit.
  - In the current English clean excerpt, that clause drops out of the final list.
  - This matters because the whole passage is enumerating concrete limits on royal authority.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: compressed, schematic, and strongly argumentative.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the historical sweep while staying close to Montesquieu's political abstractions.
- **Davidson**: often very good, but the surviving English witness is materially compromised and sometimes more expansive than the French.
