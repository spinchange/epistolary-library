## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCVIII**

**Usbek to Hassein, dervish of the mountain of Jaron**

### Translation
O wise dervish, whose curious mind shines with so much knowledge, listen to what I am about to tell you.

There are philosophers here who, it is true, have not risen to the summit of Eastern wisdom. They have not been carried up to the luminous throne; they have not heard the ineffable words with which the concerts of angels resound, nor felt the formidable seizures of divine frenzy. But, left to themselves and deprived of holy marvels, they quietly follow the traces of human reason.

You could not believe how far that guide has led them. They have disentangled chaos and explained, by a simple mechanics, the order of divine architecture. The author of nature gave motion to matter: nothing more was needed to produce that prodigious variety of effects we see in the universe.

Let ordinary lawgivers offer us laws to regulate human societies - laws as subject to change as the minds of those who propose them and of the peoples who observe them. These philosophers speak to us only of general, immutable, eternal laws, observed without any exception, with order, regularity, and infinite promptness across the immensity of space.

And what do you think these laws are, divine man? Perhaps you imagine that, entering into the counsel of the Eternal, you are about to be astonished by sublime mysteries. In advance you give up hope of understanding; you mean only to admire.

But you will soon change your mind. They do not dazzle by false majesty. Their simplicity caused them long to be misunderstood, and it was only after much reflection that all their fruitfulness and extent were recognized.

The first is that every body tends to describe a straight line unless it encounters some obstacle diverting it. The second, which is only a consequence of the first, is that every body turning around a center tends to move away from it, because the farther it is, the more the line it describes approaches the straight line.

There, sublime dervish, is the key to nature. There are fruitful principles from which consequences are drawn without end, as I shall show you in a separate letter.

The knowledge of five or six truths has filled their philosophy with miracles and let them perform more prodigies and wonders than everything told to us about our holy prophets.

For I am convinced that there is not one of our doctors who would not have been embarrassed if told to weigh in a balance all the air around the earth, or to measure all the water that falls every year upon its surface; and who would not have thought more than four times before saying how many leagues sound travels in an hour, how long a ray of light takes to come from the sun to us, how many toises there are from here to Saturn, or according to what curve a ship should be cut in order to sail as well as possible.

Perhaps if some divine man had adorned the works of these philosophers with lofty and sublime words, mixed into them bold figures and mysterious allegories, he would have made a fine work inferior only to the holy Alcoran.

Yet, to tell you what I think, I have little taste for figurative style. There are in our Alcoran a great many childish things that always strike me as such, however much they are heightened by the force and life of expression. At first one might think inspired books are only divine ideas rendered in human language. On the contrary, in our holy books one finds the language of God and the ideas of men - as if, by an admirable caprice, God had dictated the words and man supplied the thoughts.

You will perhaps say that I speak too freely of what is holiest among us, and that this is the fruit of the independence in which one lives in this country. No. Thanks be to heaven, the mind has not corrupted the heart; and while I live, Ali will be my prophet.

*Paris, the 15th day of the moon of Chahban, 1716.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the main movement of the letter: admiration for modern natural philosophy, delight in simple universal laws, and a provocative reflection on sacred style and revelation.

### Main differences
- **Scientific vocabulary and simplicity**
  - French emphasizes a few simple laws that generate immense consequences.
  - Fresh translation keeps the language of `simple mechanics`, fruitful principles, and consequences drawn `without end`.
  - Davidson often smooths this into more familiar Victorian phrasing such as `simple mechanism` and `pass beyond our ken`.

- **False grandeur**
  - French: `elles n'éblouissent point par un faux respect`
  - Fresh translation: `They do not dazzle by false majesty`
  - Davidson: `they do not dazzle us by a pretended profundity`
  - Davidson interprets the line rather than translating it closely.

- **Qur'anic critique**
  - French: `le langage de Dieu, et les idées des hommes`
  - Fresh translation keeps the aphoristic force of that contrast.
  - Davidson is close, but slightly more explanatory and less sharp.

- **Name forms**
  - Davidson's clean text has `HASSIM` and `Hali`.
  - The French reads `HASSEIN` and `Ali`.
  - The fresh draft normalizes these to the French witness rather than preserving English OCR-era spellings.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: intellectually excited, irreverent, and carefully provocative.
- **Fresh translation**: tries to preserve both the scientific wonder and the theological audacity.
- **Davidson**: readable and often impressive, though sometimes more explanatory and less pointed.

### Notable OCR / edition notes
- The English clean text is mostly legible here.
- The largest issues are transliteration choices and editorial smoothing rather than hard OCR corruption.

## Working conclusion
Letter XCVIII is an important comparison case because Davidson is strong at the level of general sense, but the French letter's provocations depend on precision. A fresh translation restores some of the philosophical edge.
