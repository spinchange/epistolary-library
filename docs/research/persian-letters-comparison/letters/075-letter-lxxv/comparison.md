## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter LXXV**

**Usbek to Rhedi, at Venice**

### Translation
I must confess it to you: I have not noticed among Christians that lively conviction of their religion which is found among Muslims. With them there is a great distance from profession to belief, from belief to conviction, from conviction to practice. Religion is less a matter of sanctification than a subject of dispute belonging to everyone: courtiers, soldiers, even women rise up against the clergy and demand that they prove what they are determined not to believe. It is not that they have reached this position by reason, and taken the trouble to examine the truth or falsity of the religion they reject: they are rebels who felt the yoke and shook it off before they had known it. Thus they are no firmer in their unbelief than in their faith; they live in an ebb and flow that carries them unceasingly from one to the other. One of them said to me one day: I believe in the immortality of the soul by semesters; my opinions depend entirely on the state of my body; according as I have more or fewer animal spirits, as my stomach digests well or badly, as the air I breathe is fine or heavy, as the food I eat is light or solid, I am Spinozist, Socinian, Catholic, impious, or devout. When the physician is by my bed, the confessor finds matters in his favor. I know very well how to prevent religion from distressing me when I am well; but I allow it to console me when I am ill: when I have nothing more to hope for on one side, religion appears and wins me over by its promises; I am quite willing then to give myself up to it and die on the side of hope.

Long ago the Christian princes freed all the slaves in their states, because, they said, Christianity makes all men equal. It is true that this act of religion was very useful to them: by it they humbled the great lords, from whose power they were withdrawing the common people. Afterwards they made conquests in lands where they saw that it was advantageous to them to have slaves; they allowed them to be bought and sold, forgetting that principle of religion which had moved them so strongly. What do you want me to say? Truth at one time, error at another. Why do we not do as the Christians do? We are very simple to refuse settlements and easy conquests in happy climates because the water there is not pure enough to wash us according to the principles of the holy Koran!

I give thanks to almighty God, who sent Ali his great prophet, that I profess a religion which makes itself preferred to every human interest, and which is as pure as the sky from which it descended.

*Paris, the 13th day of the moon of Saphar, 1715.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson captures the broad argument well: Christian religion often appears disputatious and inconsistent, especially when political advantage is involved, while Usbek closes with an ironized profession of Muslim purity. The English version is generally serviceable, but it is more Victorian in idiom and is badly interrupted by OCR and edition debris.

### Main differences
- **Faith, persuasion, and conviction**
  - French: `cette persuasion vive de leur religion`
  - Fresh translation: `that lively conviction of their religion`
  - Davidson: `that lively faith in their religion`
  - `Faith` is understandable, but the French emphasis is slightly more psychological and argumentative than devotional.

- **Sanctification vs holiness**
  - French: `La religion est moins un sujet de sanctification qu'un sujet de disputes`
  - Fresh translation keeps `sanctification`.
  - Davidson substitutes `holiness`, which is readable but less close to the French theological vocabulary.

- **Rebellion before understanding**
  - French: `ils ont senti le joug, et l'ont secoué avant de l'avoir connu`
  - Davidson is close, but the fresh version keeps the sharper paradox: they reject the yoke before they even know what it is.

- **`par semestre`**
  - Fresh translation: `I believe ... by semesters`
  - Davidson: `for six months at a time`
  - Davidson clarifies the idiom, but the fresh version preserves the comic pseudo-scholarly phrasing of the French.

- **Closing of the skeptical anecdote**
  - French: `mourir du côté de l'espérance`
  - Fresh translation: `die on the side of hope`
  - Davidson: `die with hope on my side`
  - Davidson is close, though the French formulation is slightly stranger and more pointed.

- **Final pious irony**
  - French: `Ali son grand prophète`
  - Davidson's clean text reads `Hali`, which is likely OCR or transcription drift.
  - The fresh version restores the expected name while keeping the sentence's deliberately emphatic orthodoxy.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: skeptical, dry, and ironic; the closing thanksgiving is not simple piety but part of the satirical frame.
- **Fresh translation**: aims to preserve the argumentative movement and the irony of the conclusion.
- **Davidson**: generally accurate, but slightly more devotional in register and less crisp in the satire of inconsistency.

## Working conclusion
Letter LXXV is a good example of Davidson's usual strengths and weaknesses: he conveys the argument, but his version is filtered through a smoother Victorian idiom and is currently obscured by substantial OCR and paratextual contamination.
