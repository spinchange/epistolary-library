## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XLVI**

**Usbek to Rhédi, at Venice**

### Translation
Here I see people arguing endlessly about religion; yet it seems that they are also competing to see who will observe it least.

Not only are they no better Christians; they are not even better citizens, and that is what touches me. For, in whatever religion one lives, obedience to the laws, love for one's fellow men, and reverence toward one's parents are always the first acts of religion.

Indeed, should not the first object of a religious man be to please the deity who established the religion he professes? But the surest means of doing so is surely to observe the rules of society and the duties of humanity. For, whatever religion one lives under, once one supposes there is such a religion, one must also suppose that God loves men, since He established religion to make them happy; and if He loves men, one is certain to please Him by loving them too — that is, by fulfilling all the duties of charity and humanity toward them and by violating none of the laws under which they live.

One is far more certain of pleasing God in that way than by observing this or that ceremony; for ceremonies have no degree of goodness in themselves. They are good only by relation, and on the supposition that God has commanded them. But that is matter for great dispute, and one may easily go wrong in it, since one has to choose the ceremonies of one religion out of those of two thousand.

A man made this prayer to God every day: Lord, I understand nothing of the disputes people never cease to raise about you. I would like to serve you according to your will; but every man I consult wants me to serve you according to his. When I wish to pray to you, I do not know in what language I ought to speak. Nor do I know what posture I should take: one says I must pray standing, another wants me seated, another demands that my body rest on my knees. That is not all: some claim that I must wash myself every morning with cold water; others insist that you will look on me with horror if I do not have a small piece of my flesh cut away. The other day I happened to eat a rabbit in a caravansary: three men nearby made me tremble, and all three maintained that I had grievously offended you — one because the animal was unclean, another because it had been strangled, and the third because it was not a fish. A Brahmin passing by, whom I took as judge, told me: They are wrong, for apparently you did not kill the animal yourself. I did indeed, I told him. Ah! then you have committed an abominable act, and God will never forgive you, he answered in a severe voice. How do you know whether your father's soul had not passed into that beast? All these things, Lord, throw me into inconceivable perplexity. I cannot move my head without being threatened with offending you; yet I wish to please you and to spend in that service the life I have from you. I may be mistaken, but I believe the best way to reach that end is to live as a good citizen in the society where you caused me to be born, and as a good father in the family you have given me.

*At Paris, the 8th day of the moon of Chahban, 1713.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson preserves the letter's argumentative movement well: religious dispute, ethics above ceremony, and the comic prayer that exposes contradictory ritual demands. His version remains lucid and usable throughout.

### Main differences
- **First acts of religion**
  - French: `les premiers actes de religion`
  - Fresh translation keeps `acts`.
  - Davidson: `the principal part of any religion`
  - Davidson generalizes a vivid moral formulation.
- **Charity and humanity**
  - French joins `charité` and `humanité`.
  - Fresh translation keeps both terms.
  - Davidson also keeps them, and is strong here.
- **The rabbit anecdote**
  - French has a rapid comic accumulation of mutually incompatible certainties.
  - Davidson is close, though his phrasing is somewhat more formal and less conversational.
- **Closing formulation**
  - French: `en bon citoyen ... et en bon père`
  - Fresh translation preserves the parallel exactly.
  - Davidson also does, but with a slightly heavier cadence.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: ironical, philosophically clear, and lightly comic rather than solemn.
- **Fresh translation**: aims for plain modern prose that keeps the argumentative elegance and the deadpan humor.
- **Davidson**: faithful and intelligent, though somewhat more sermon-like in rhythm.

### Notable OCR / edition notes

## Working conclusion
Letter XLVI is less about doctrinal detail than about ethical common sense. Davidson handles it well, but the fresh translation keeps more of the French contrast between moral clarity and ritual absurdity.
