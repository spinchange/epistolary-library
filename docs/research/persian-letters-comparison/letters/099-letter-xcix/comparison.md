## Fresh translation draft

### Heading
**Letter XCIX**

**Usbek to Ibben, at Smyrna**

### Translation
There is no country in the world where fortune is so inconstant as in this one. Every ten years revolutions occur that cast the rich into misery and lift the poor on swift wings to the summit of wealth. One is astonished at his poverty, the other at his abundance. The newly rich man admires the wisdom of Providence; the poor man admires the blind fatality of destiny.

Those who collect taxes swim amid treasures; there are few Tantaluses among them. Yet they begin this trade in the last misery. They are despised like mud while they are poor; when they are rich, they are esteemed enough. So they neglect nothing in order to acquire esteem.

They are now in a terrible condition. A chamber has just been established called a chamber of justice, because it is going to take all their wealth away from them. They can neither divert nor hide their assets, for they are compelled to declare them exactly on pain of death. Thus they are made to pass through a very narrow defile - between life and their money. To complete their misfortune, there is a minister known for his wit, who honors them with his jokes and makes light of all the council's deliberations. One does not every day find ministers ready to make the people laugh; they ought to be grateful to this one for having undertaken it.

The body of footmen is more respectable in France than elsewhere. It is a seminary of great lords. It fills the void of the other estates. Those who belong to it take the place of unfortunate grandees, ruined magistrates, and gentlemen killed in the furies of war. And when they cannot make up the deficiency by themselves, they restore all the great houses by means of their daughters, who are like a kind of manure that enriches mountain and barren lands.

I find, Ibben, Providence admirable in the way it has distributed wealth. If it had granted wealth only to good people, it would not have been sufficiently distinguished from virtue, and all its nothingness would not be felt. But when one examines who the people are that are burdened with the most of it, by dint of despising the rich one ends at last by despising riches.

*Paris, the 26th day of the moon of Maharram, 1717.*

## Comparison against Davidson

### Davidson text summary
Davidson keeps the letter's satire on financial reversals, tax farmers, performative justice, and the social role of domestic service in replenishing the elite.

### Main differences
- **Blind fate**
  - French: `l'aveugle fatalité du destin`
  - Fresh translation keeps the full fatalistic phrase.
  - Davidson's `the mischance of a blind fate` is readable, but weaker and less solemnly ironic.

- **Chamber of justice joke**
  - French calls the institution `une chambre qu'on appelle de justice, parce qu'elle va leur ravir tout leur bien`.
  - Fresh translation keeps the direct black humor of the name.
  - Davidson is close, but a touch smoother in the satirical turn.

- **`séminaire de grands seigneurs`**
  - Fresh translation keeps `seminary of great lords`.
  - Davidson's `nursery of great lords` is vivid, but it loses some of the institutional joke in `séminaire`.

- **Nothingness of wealth**
  - French: `on n'en auroit plus senti tout le néant`
  - Fresh translation: `all its nothingness would not be felt`
  - Davidson: `their insignificance would not have been fully recognized`
  - Davidson is accurate in sense, but the French's `néant` is harsher and more metaphysical.

### Tone comparison
- **French original**: financially concrete, socially cruel, and dryly comic.
- **Fresh translation**: keeps the irony close to the French wording.
- **Davidson**: effective and often lively, but slightly smoother in moral phrasing.

## Working conclusion
Davidson is mostly reliable in Letter XCIX. The fresh translation mainly restores the harsher moral and social edge of terms like `néant`, `séminaire`, and the satirical naming of institutions.
